# Shared clones make random cell halves look reproducible in pooled screens 

## Xuanrui Huang 

Department of Biomedical Informatics, Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine, National University of Singapore, Singapore. Corresponding author. E-mail: huangxuanrui@u.nus.edu 

Abstract 

Single-cell perturbation screens are used to train and evaluate models that pre-dict the effects of perturbations. A model’s score is compared with the ceiling that the data themselves allow. This ceiling, and which perturbations have reliable effects, are usually judged by splitting each perturbation’s cells into two random halves and asking whether the effects measured in the two halves point the same way. This assumes that the halves are independent replicates. In pooled single-cell screens such as Perturb-seq, however, guide RNAs are delivered by lentivirus at a low dose and the cells then expand, so the cells that carry a guide descend from a limited number of transduced cells. All descendants of one transduced cell form a clone, and two random halves contain the same clones. This is pseudorepli-cation, and its unit, the clone, is not marked in the data. Using perturbations that should have no effect as controls, we compared random halves with indepen-dent replicates from separate transductions, and tested directly with halves that differ only in their clones. In a genome-scale screen of primary T cells, pertur-bations that should have no effect pointed the same way between random halves almost always and between independent replicates only half the time, the rate expected from noise alone; measuring more cells did not remove this agreement. When the cells were split into halves that share no clone, by the constant region of the T-cell receptor or by a clone barcode in the vector, the excess agreement largely disappeared. Its size equals the part of the expression differences between cells that is set by clones, divided by the effective number of clones per perturba-tion, which is smaller when fewer cells are transduced per guide and when their expansion is more uneven. It was largest in T cells transferred into mice and in the genome-scale primary T-cell screen; one to two orders of magnitude smaller in cell lines, and in their CRISPRi screens also small relative to the real effects; and smallest in non-dividing neurons in vivo. Shared clones inflate benchmark ceilings and reliability labels. When a model’s input and the answer used to score 

1arXiv:2610.32962v1 [q-bio.QM] 23 Oct 2026 it come from cells of the same transduction, published models that transfer per-turbation effects to a new cell state appear better than a baseline that ignores the perturbation; scored against cells from another transduction, most fall below it. Whether an effect would reappear in another experiment should be judged by comparing cells from separate transductions, not two random halves of the same cells.

# Introduction 

Single-cell perturbation screens now measure the transcriptional changes caused by thousands of genetic perturbations across cell types and cell states 1–3 . These data are used to train and evaluate so-called virtual-cell models, which predict the effects of perturbations or cellular contexts that have not been measured 4,5 . How good a model’s score is has to be judged against the ceiling that the data themselves allow: only the part of a measured effect that reappears in a repeated experiment can be predicted. Recent work has therefore argued that benchmarks should report the reproducibility ceiling of the data alongside model scores 6.The usual way to estimate reproducibility is to split each perturbation’s cells into two random halves and compare the effects measured in the two; we call this ran-dom halves. At least three practices rest on it. The evaluation tool cell-eval uses the agreement between random halves, corrected by the Spearman–Brown formula, as the ceiling of the data 7–9 . Per-perturbation reliability labels, proposed for cleaning train-ing and evaluation data, are based on the same agreement 6. And a class of methods splits perturbation effects into a part common to all cellular contexts and a part spe-cific to each context, uses random halves to separate reproducible variation from noise, and builds on this split to transfer effects to new contexts 10 . All three assume that the cells of a perturbation are independent samples, so that two subsets of them differ only by sampling noise. The evaluation of transfer models makes the same assump-tion implicitly: a perturbation’s cells in the source context are used for training and its cells in the target context serve as the answer for scoring 11 ; if the cells of the two contexts come from the same transduction, they are not independent either. In a widely used class of screens this assumption does not hold. These screens have three features. First, guide RNAs (sgRNAs) are introduced into cells by an integrating lentivirus, a step called transduction; to give each cell a single sgRNA, the dose of virus is low, and the sgRNA passes to the cell’s descendants with its genome. Second, the cells divide and expand after transduction, for example during drug selection, culture, proliferation after transfer into an animal, or the development of an organoid. Third, single-cell sequencing reads each cell’s sgRNA together with its transcriptome, and cells are then grouped by sgRNA (Perturb-seq and related methods). The cells that carry a given sgRNA at sequencing therefore all descend from a limited number of transduced cells, whose expansion is often very uneven 12–14 . Random halves come from the same transduction and may contain the same clones, so their agreement may reflect the state of those clones rather than the effect of the perturbation. Two sets of cells from separate transductions, such as the cells of the two sgRNAs against one gene or the cells of different donors, share no clone; we call them independent replicates. Statistically this is pseudoreplication: observations that are not independent are treated as independent replicates. Single-cell differential expression analysis has long recognized that treating cells from the same individual as independent samples inflates false positives 15,16 . In screens at high multiplicity of infection, where each cell car-ries many sgRNAs, the combination of sgRNAs identifies clones, and clonal expansion has been shown to produce false-positive differentially expressed genes 17 . It has also been noted that random halves measure sampling precision rather than experimen-tal reproducibility 18 . In the common screens at low multiplicity of infection, however, clones carry no label in the data. Individuals are labelled and can be pooled or mod-elled; invisible clones cannot be handled in this way. How large this effect is in such screens, what determines its size and what it does to the practices above has, to our knowledge, not been measured. Here we measure it in screens that contain independent replicates (Fig. 1). We compare random halves with independent replicates, using perturbations that should have no effect as controls, and test directly under conditions in which the two halves differ only in their clones: once with a natural marker of T cells and once with a clone barcode in the vector. We then measure the size of the effect in 16 screens and relate it to the effective number of clones per perturbation. Finally, we test what it does to benchmark ceilings, reliability labels, cross-context structure and the evaluation of transfer models. 

# Results 

## Null perturbations look reproducible under random halves 

We first asked whether a perturbation with no expected effect looks reproducible when its cells are split into random halves. The genome-scale CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) screen of primary human CD4+ T cells 3 profiles cells of four donors in a resting and a stimulated state, with two sgRNAs against each gene. We used two kinds of perturbation with no expected effect, together called null perturbations. The first are sgRNAs against genes not expressed in T cells. The screen measures expression with probes against individual genes, so the expression of each target gene is itself measured, and 1,239 targets in donor 1 and 872 in donor 2 are not expressed in T cells (Methods); an sgRNA against a gene that is not expressed cannot change the transcriptome through that gene. The second are non-targeting control sgRNAs, which have no target in the genome; these control cells are also the cells that benchmark tools split at random when they compute ceilings. A positive value means that the two effects point broadly the same way, and its expected value equals the energy, the sum of squares over genes, of the part of the effect that the two halves share. If the halves differ only by sampling noise, the two effects fluctuate around zero independently, the cross-half signal is positive or negative with equal probability, and about half of the null perturbations are positive; our pipeline returns this in simulated screens (Supplementary Fig. S1a). Under random halves, 96–99% of the unexpressed targets had a positive cross-half signal, in both donors and both states (Fig. 2a,b). With independent replicates the share returned to chance: 49–54% with one sgRNA in each half, and 53–55% with donors 1–2 against donors 3–4 (Fig. 2b). In CD4, non-targeting sgRNAs behaved in the same way: 97–99% were positive under random halves, against 49–50% for halves taken from two different non-targeting sgRNAs; in cell lines, the share under random halves was also above one half (60–76%) but lower than in CD4 (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Table S1). Random halves therefore report a reproducible response for perturbations that should have none, and independent replicates do not. We call the excess agreement of random halves over independent replicates the shared component. The halves of an independent replicate differ not only in their clones but also in the sgRNA or the donor, so these results do not yet show that the shared component comes from clones. If each sgRNA had an action of its own beyond its target, both random halves would contain cells of that sgRNA and would also agree. The next two sections first exclude sampling noise and then answer this question with halves that carry the same sgRNA and differ only in their clones. 

## More cells do not remove the shared component 

If the shared component were sampling noise, measuring more cells would shrink it. To test this, we split each perturbation’s cells at random into eight parts and let each half use 1/8, 1/4, 3/8 and 1/2 of the cells in turn, on the same targets (Fig. 2d). Under random splits, the cross-half signal of null perturbations was the same at all four cell numbers (7.05–7.12 in resting cells of donor 1, from 23 to 92 cells per half); with the two sgRNAs on opposite sides, it was close to zero at every cell number. The Pearson correlation between the two halves of these null perturbations nevertheless rose with the number of cells, from 0.08 to 0.24 in donor 1. This is what a shared component that does not change with cell number predicts: with more cells, sampling noise falls while the shared component stays, so the correlation between random halves keeps rising. 6This has a direct consequence for benchmark ceilings. Benchmarks extrapolate the split-half correlation to all cells with the Spearman–Brown formula and use the result as the ceiling. The formula predicted the rise above correctly (from the correlation at 1/4 of the cells it predicts 0.25 at 1/2, against an observed 0.24), but what it extrap-olates is the agreement within the same clones: however many cells are measured, the “ceiling” of a null perturbation approaches the agreement within its clones, not zero. 

## The halves share clones 

If the shared component comes from clones, a quantity that marks clones should agree between random halves and not between the two sgRNAs. T cells carry such a marker. During its development, each T cell cuts its T-cell receptor (TCR) β gene and joins it at one of two D-J segments. Joining at the second cuts out the first segment together with the TRBC1 constant region, so the cell can make only TRBC2; joining at the first makes the cell produce TRBC1 19 . The joined DNA passes to all of the cell’s descendants, so the cells of one clone use the same constant region (Fig. 3a), and the readout of the CD4 screen measures both constant regions. If a perturbation’s cells came from many clones, their TRBC1 share would be close to that of the whole population; if they came from few, it would deviate from it. Scaled as a z-score against random assignment of cells, the deviation would have variance 1 in the first case; it was 34 in donor 1 and 81 in donor 2 (Fig. 3b). The deviation was nearly the same between the random halves of a perturbation (r = 0.93 and 0.96) and unrelated between its two sgRNAs (r ≈ 0; Fig. 3c). Random halves contain the same clones, and the two sgRNAs contain different ones. The marker also allows a direct test. When a perturbation’s cells are split by constant region, one half holding cells in which only TRBC2 was detected and the other cells with a high TRBC1 share, the halves share no clone, while the sgRNA, donor, library and state are the same. Each cell was referenced to control cells of the same constant-region class, and the TCR genes themselves were excluded (Methods). For comparison, the same cells were split at random. In resting and stimulated cells of donors 1 and 2, 95–99% of the unexpressed targets were positive under random halves and 54–61% under constant-region halves, close to chance; the cross-half signal kept only 8–16% of its random-half value, and 92–96% of the targets had a higher signal under random halves (paired sign test, p < 10 −150 ; Fig. 3d). The halves carry the same sgRNA, so an action of the sgRNA itself cannot explain the difference. The small remainder is consistent with clones leaking between the halves through the few cells assigned to the wrong class. TRBC distinguishes only two classes of clones. 10 The size of the shared component depends on the effective number of clones per perturbation 

How large the shared component is follows from its source. Suppose that a pertur-bation’s cells come from several clones, that clone k makes up a share p k of all the cells and that its expression deviates by c k. Two random halves each contain about the same share of every clone, so the deviation they have in common is Σ p kck, whose expected energy is proportional to Σ p k2; sampling noise of different cells is uncorre-lated between the halves and does not enter this term (derivation in Supplementary Note 1). Dividing the energy of this common deviation by the per-cell variance gives a quantity that does not change with the number of cells, which we call clone strength. It can be measured directly with null perturbations: the cross-half signal of random halves, minus the cross-half signal between two different null perturbations, divided by the per-cell variance. The derivation shows that clone strength is the ratio of two factors. The numerator is the intraclass correlation (ICC), the share of the expression differences between single cells that is set by clones; it depends on the cell type. The denominator is the effective number of clones, K eff = 1/Σ p k2: the number of clones when all are equally large, and smaller the more their sizes differ. If N 0 cells were transduced per sgRNA and the clone sizes after expansion have coefficient of variation CV, then approximately K eff ≈ N0/(1 + CV 2). This is a special case of the design effect of cluster sampling 21–23 : the effective sample size is set by the distribution of cluster sizes, not by the number of individuals. In the organoid screen, the clone composition and the ICC of each perturbation can be measured directly, so this relation can be tested directly. Each perturbation had a median of 51 clones of uneven size, and a median K eff of 12; the ICC measured by analysis of variance had a median of 0.010, so within a cell type, clones set only about 1% of the expression differences between cells. Across the 32 perturbations, clone strength increased with Σ p k2 (Spearman correlation 0.78) with a proportionality constant of 0.0096 (95% interval from a bootstrap over perturbations, 0.0085–0.0109), in agreement with the independently measured ICC of 0.010 (0.003–0.017; Fig. 4a). When the ICC measured in one half of the perturbations was used to predict the clone strength of the other half, the Spearman correlation between predicted and measured values was 0.80, and the measured total was 0.94 times the predicted total (medians over 500 random splits). The CD4 screen has no clone-by-clone labels, but it allows an indirect test: each perturbation has two clone readouts, the deviation of its TRBC1 share and the shared component of its expression. Under this relation both scale with the same K eff , so their ratio should stay the same across donors. The two readouts each differed about threefold among the four donors, whereas their ratio stayed between 0.021 and 0.029, falling slightly as the readouts rose (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Table S2). A small effective number of clones can come from few transduced cells or from uneven expansion; the first is set by the design and the second by the cells and their culture, and the remedies differ. In CD4 the two can be separated. The size of the TRBC1 deviation implies that a perturbation’s cells behave as if they came from at most about 7 (donor 1) and 4 (donor 2) equally sized clones (Methods), whereas of 11 the order of 500 cells were transduced per perturbation. Many cells were transduced and yet the effective number of clones is a single digit, so in CD4 the problem comes mainly from very uneven expansion: a few clones expanded far more than the rest. We measured clone strength in 16 screens with the same method (Fig. 4c; Sup-plementary Table S3). These screens differ in cell type, type of perturbation and sequencing depth, and most did not record how many cells were transduced per sgRNA, so they can show only whether the orders of magnitude are consistent with the relation above. Clone strength was highest in cells that passed through an engraft-ment bottleneck and long expansion after transduction: 0.045–0.10 in human chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells after 21 days in mice 24 and 0.010–0.015 in mouse T cells 8 days after transfer into infected mice 25 ; in the genome-scale primary CD4 screen it was 0.004–0.012, highest in the donor whose perturbations were most clonal by the TRBC marker. It was lowest in non-dividing neurons 26 : the sgRNAs were delivered by intravenous adeno-associated virus (AAV) into mice 16 days after birth, and the cells sequenced weeks later were neurons that no longer divide, so the transduced cells had no descendants; clone strength was 0.0001–0.0002. The other systems, including seven cell-line screens 1,2,4,27 , a primary T-cell screen with only 146 sgRNAs 28 , T cells trans-ferred into tumour-bearing mice 29 , primary leukaemia cells 30 , human brain organoids 20 

and embryonic mouse cortex 31 , lay between 0.0002 and 0.003 (the organoid and cor-tex screens have a single null sgRNA and were estimated with clone halves or library halves; Methods). Primary cells are not uniformly high: among primary T cells, the small-library screen was several times lower than the genome-scale CD4 screen. What sets the size thus appears to be not whether the cells are primary or in vivo but how many effective clones a perturbation’s cells come from; this was tested directly in the screen that recorded clones and is a consistent explanation for the other data. Clone strength also has to be read against the size of the real effects. In CD4, the random-half signal of non-targeting sgRNAs was comparable to that of a typical targeting perturbation (0.8–1.1 times); in the CRISPRi screens of cell lines 1,2 and in the H1 embryonic stem cell data of the 2025 Virtual Cell Challenge 4 it was only 1– 20% (Supplementary Table S3). The same clone strength therefore matters more in screens whose real effects are weak.

To predict a perturbation’s effect in a context where it has not been measured, two things are available: the perturbation’s effects in the measured contexts, and the effects of other perturbations in the new context. Decomposition methods use the first to estimate the perturbation’s common part and the second to learn how the common part maps to the part specific to that context. The key step of the transfer is thus to predict the specific part from the common part, and this is the step we tested. We trained a linear predictor f on many perturbations to predict the specific part computed from one half of the cells (B) from the common part computed from the other half (A), d B ≈ f(s A), and scored it on perturbations it had not seen. Taking the two parts from different halves is meant to keep sampling noise from linking them. Random halves keep the noise apart but not the clones: the halves contain the same clones, these clones are present in both states, and a clone’s deviations in the two states are related but not identical. If a clone shifts expression by c at rest and by qc after stimulation (q ̸ = 1), it adds (1 + q)c/2 to s and (1 − q)c/2 to d, and when A and B contain the same clone, f can predict d B from s A through c alone (Fig. 6a). In CD4, the predictor explained about half of the reproducible specific energy under random halves and almost none under sgRNA halves (Fig. 6d), and it carried the deviations of unexpressed targets into its predictions as much as those of expressed targets (Supplementary Table S5): what it learned was the state of the clones, not the response to the perturbation. A linear predictor is used here because the leakage caused by clones is itself linear: a clone adds to the specific part exactly (1 − q)/(1 + q) times what it adds to the common part. Its failure across independent replicates shows only that there is no linear relation that reproduces across transductions; published nonlinear models are tested in the next section. A model with three terms reproduces these observations (Fig. 6e,f; Supplementary Fig. S1; Methods). Each cell carries an additive state of its clone, a multiplicative gain of its clone on the response to the perturbation, and sampling noise. The additive term makes null perturbations reproducible and raises the ceilings; neither clone term shrinks with more cells; and when contexts share clones, the predictor explains 10% of the specific energy even when the true cross-context relation is zero. The model also shows the opposite risk: large clone deviations along the directions of the response can hide a real cross-context structure between independent replicates. At the ratios of clone energy to signal energy found in the CD4 screen (4.0 and 7.5; Methods), a true explained share of 70% is measured as 10% and 3% between independent replicates. When the predictor gives almost nothing under sgRNA halves, there are therefore two possibilities: the CD4 screen has no such cross-context structure, or it has one that clone deviations hide between independent replicates. Box 1 summarizes how to check whether random halves can be trusted and how to anticipate the risk. 

Box 1 | Checking whether random halves can be trusted 

Do the two halves come from the same transduction? Random halves of the same cells come from one transduction and may share clones; cells of different sgRNAs, different donors or a separate transduction do not. The latter can be used as replicates, and a null control should still be reported. 

If they come from the same transduction (random halves, or cells of one transduction divided among contexts), reliability labels, ceilings, cross-context structure and the scores of transfer models can all be inflated. How much depends on the effective number of clones per perturbation: the fewer cells transduced per sgRNA, the longer and more uneven the expansion after transduction and the narrower the bottleneck the cells pass through (for example transfer into an animal), the smaller the effective number of clones and the larger the inflation. In the data measured here, the inflation was large in T cells transferred into mice and in the genome-scale primary T-cell screen; clone strength was one to two orders of magnitude lower in cell lines, and in the CRISPRi screens of cell lines also small relative to their real effects; it was lowest in non-dividing neurons in vivo. 

In every case , include perturbations that should have no effect, such as non-targeting sgRNAs or unexpressed targets, and compare their agreement between random halves with that between two different null perturbations. In libraries selected on a growth phenotype, unexpressed targets were selected because their sgRNAs affected growth, most likely by acting at the promoter of a neighbouring gene; their effects are real and they cannot serve as null perturbations, so non-targeting sgRNAs should be used instead. Where possible, a clone barcode in the vector allows clone halves that remove this component directly. 

# Discussion 

Random halves are halves within the same clones, and they answer a narrower question than the one they are usually asked. Three questions can be distinguished. First, how precisely the effect has been estimated in this set of cells, for example whether measuring more cells would sharpen the estimate: random halves are the right tool 20 for this question. Second, whether the effect of the same sgRNA would reappear in another experiment: this needs independent transductions of the same sgRNA. Third, how reproducible the effect of knocking down the gene is: this needs different sgRNAs, and the differences between sgRNAs are then part of the variation to be measured. Benchmark ceilings and reliability labels are meant to answer the second or the third question, but use the tool for the first. Pseudoreplication has long been known in single-cell differential expression analysis 15,16 , false positives caused by clonal expansion have been reported in single-cell CRISPR screens at high multiplicity of infection 17 , and it has been pointed out that random halves measure sampling precision 18 . Our results add four points. First, in the common screens at low multiplicity of infection, clones carry no label; with halves that differ only in their clones, we show directly that the excess agreement of random halves comes from clones, and we measure its size. Second, this component does not shrink with the number of cells, so more cells, the remedy that sampling models imply, do not remove it. Third, its size follows a simple relation, the expres-sion differences set by clones divided by the effective number of clones; the relation was tested directly in a screen that recorded clones and matched the orders of mag-nitude in 16 screens, so the size can be anticipated from the number of transduced cells and the expansion. Fourth, shared clones affect not only reproducibility but also cross-context structure and the evaluation of transfer models: when the input and the answer used for scoring come from cells of the same transduction, much of a model’s advantage can come from clones. Labelled and unlabelled units are handled very differently. In the whole-brain screen of non-dividing neurons, the cells of each sgRNA are spread over 74 mice, and differences between mice are also shared by random halves; but mice are labelled, ref-erencing each cell to control cells of the same mouse removes them, and their measured contribution was small. Clones carry no label, and no reference within one experiment can remove them; only independent transductions, a larger effective number of clones or a clone barcode in the vector can. We do not question the conclusions of the original studies. They used appropriate replicates in their own analyses; the CD4 screen, for example, treats donors as biologi-cal replicates 3. The problem arises downstream: when these data are used to compute benchmark ceilings, to label reliable perturbations and to decompose cross-context effects, the two halves are random halves of the same cells; and when transfer models are evaluated, the training cells and the answer may come from the same transduction. How much this matters depends on clone strength and on its size relative to the real effects. In the CRISPRi screens of cell lines and in the H1 data of the 2025 Virtual Cell Challenge, the shared component of non-targeting sgRNAs was only 1–20% of the signal of a typical targeting perturbation, and ceilings computed on those data are little affected. In the genome-scale primary CD4 screen it was comparable to the signal of a typical targeting perturbation, and in T cells transferred into mice it was larger. For a new data set, the most direct check is to measure it with the data’s own null perturbations, comparing the agreement between random halves with that between two different null perturbations. 21 Our analysis has limitations, which we group by the conclusion they affect. On the mechanism: constant-region halves distinguish only two classes of clones, and a few cells are assigned to the wrong class; in the organoids, a few clone barcodes are shared by several integrations. Both place clones that should be separated into both halves, which makes the tests conservative: they can underestimate the role of clones, not overestimate it. On the size: the relation was tested directly in one screen that recorded clones, and its clone effect is weak; the 16 screens also differ in cell type, type of perturbation (interference, activation, knockout) and sequencing depth, and most did not record the number of cells transduced per sgRNA, so on these data “the size depends on the effective number of clones” is an explanation consistent with the orders of magnitude; several of them have only 4–10 null sgRNAs, and their values indicate orders of magnitude only. On the consequences: the comparisons of ceilings and reliability labels used two CD4 donors; the transfer models were compared for one pair of states, STATE was repeated in four donors and the other models were run in one donor, and several settings differ from the authors’ defaults (Supplementary Table S6). Finally, the three-term model shows only that these observations can arise from clones; it was not fitted to the data and does not give their sizes. Future screens can build independent replicates into their design. Two or more sgRNAs per gene are already standard, and clone barcodes add little cost 12,20 ; separate transductions and donors cost more but give the most direct test. For existing data, a set of null perturbations is the minimum check, and the number of cells transduced per sgRNA and their expansion after transduction can be used to anticipate the risk. 

# Methods 

## Data 

Primary human CD4+ T cells. The genome-scale CRISPRi Perturb-seq screen of Zhu et al. 3 profiles cells of four donors, resting and stimulated (48 h), with a probe-based readout and two sgRNAs per target. We used the single-cell files of donors 1 and 2 (all four donors for clone strength and the transfer models), without cells flagged as low quality or lacking an assigned sgRNA, and the released pseudobulk counts of all four donors. 

Other screens. We used the raw single cells of the K562 essential-gene (day 6), K562 genome-wide (day 8, part 1) and RPE1 essential-gene screens of Replogle et al. 1, normalized to 10,000 counts over the genes common to the three screens, log1p transformed and corrected additively for the control mean of each GEM group (one 10x Genomics channel); the Jurkat and HepG2 essential-gene screens of Nadig et al. 2;the H1 hESC training data of the 2025 Virtual Cell Challenge 4 (221,273 cells, 150 targets and 31 non-targeting constructs); the brain organoid screen of Li et al. 20 with its clone barcodes; and eight further screens for clone strength 24–31 . Systems, uses and accessions are listed in Supplementary Table S7. 22 Null perturbations 

Null perturbations are of two kinds: sgRNAs against targets whose mean expression in control cells is below 0.01 log1p CP10K (unexpressed targets), and non-targeting sgRNAs. Libraries selected on a growth phenotype are not suitable sources of the first kind. The K562 essential-gene library 1 contains unexpressed targets that were selected because their sgRNAs affected growth, most likely by acting at the promoter of a neighbouring gene 37 . In two independent K562 screens (the essential-gene and the genome-wide screen), 85% of the structure of these targets recurred (Supplementary Fig. S3), so their effects are real. In such libraries we used only non-targeting sgRNAs as null perturbations. 

## Effects and gene filters 

The effect of a perturbation in a set of its cells is their mean log1p CP10K expression (the natural logarithm of 1 plus counts per 10,000) minus that of control cells, the non-targeting sgRNAs. Controls were split like the perturbed cells in CD4; in the Replogle data, both halves used all controls, which cancels after centering. The target gene was set to zero in its own effect, except in the ceiling analyses, which keep it as the benchmark tool does. Unless stated otherwise, effects were centered over perturbations within each half and context. Genes were kept if their control mean log1p CP10K was at least 0.1: in CD4 single cells, in both states for analyses that combine states and in the analyzed state otherwise; in the CD4 pseudobulk, in every donor and state; and in the Replogle data, in all three screens. cell-eval used genes with a resting control mean of at least 0.01, together with the target genes. TCR genes (TRAC, TRBC, TRAV, TRBV, TRGC and TRDC) were excluded from the CD4 null, depth, constant-region and clone-strength analyses. 

## Splits and target sets 

In CD4, each cell carries two random bits, r1 and r2 (seed 21). Random halves split on r1; the bootstrap and the reliability labels also used r2 and r1 ⊕ r2. sgRNA halves split on the parity of the sgRNA number in its name, so that each sgRNA falls on the same side in every file, and controls were split likewise by non-targeting sgRNA. Donor halves sum the pseudobulk counts over donors 1–2 or 3–4, both sgRNAs and both sequencing runs (log1p CP10K of the summed counts minus that of the summed controls; at least 40 cells per half and state). In the Replogle data, the cells of each perturbation were permuted (seeds 0, 1 and 2 for the three screens) and cut into equal halves. Unexpressed targets are probed targets with control mean log1p CP10K below 0.01; expressed targets have at least 0.5. They are an operational null: their knockdown cannot act through the target gene, although an sgRNA can act elsewhere, for example at a neighbouring promoter. Analyses that combine states (Fig. 2a,b, the specific share, the predictor and the reliability labels) apply the threshold in both states; analyses within one state apply it in that state. In CD4, perturbations needed at least 10 cells on each side of both the random and the sgRNA halves in both states for Fig. 2a,b (1,239 unexpressed targets in donor 1 and 872 in donor 2), and at least 10 on each 23 side of all four halvings in both states for the predictor, the specific share and the reliability labels. 

## Cross-half signal 

The cross-half signal of a perturbation is Σ g xA,g xB,g , the gene-wise product of its two half-effects summed over genes; with independent noise in the two halves, it is an unbiased estimate of the energy of the reproducible effect. For null perturbations, every perturbation was referenced to the null baseline, the mean effect of the unexpressed targets in each half. Referencing to the mean of all perturbations instead (template removal) makes a null perturbation equal to the negative template in both halves; in the K562 genome-wide screen it raised the share of reproducible null targets from 83% to 89%. Non-targeting sgRNAs were treated as perturbations, with the same random bit per cell and effects referenced to the mean over all kept non-targeting sgRNAs in the half (genes with a mean of at least 0.1 over non-targeting cells, TCR genes excluded; at least 10 cells per half). For halves from different sgRNAs, sgRNAs were paired at random and the first half of one was compared with the second half of the other, at the same depth; the share of positive pairs is the mean over 50 pairings. The same analysis was run in the Replogle, Nadig and H1 data, with one pseudo-perturbation per non-targeting construct. 

## Cell number 

To compare cell numbers on the same targets, we re-aggregated the single-cell files of CD4 donors 1 and 2 (resting and stimulated), with the cells, sgRNA parity and the two random bits above, and one further independent random bit per cell (seed 22). The three bits divide the cells of each sgRNA into eight equal parts. Random splits take m parts for one half and m other parts for the other half, so that both halves contain cells of both sgRNAs; sgRNA splits take 2m parts of the first sgRNA for one half and the same 2m parts of the second sgRNA for the other, so that the halves have the same numbers of cells as under random splits (m = 1–4, that is, 1/8 to 1/2 of the cells per half). Each m averages several disjoint choices of parts. Effects are the mean of the half minus the mean of control cells with the same random parts, and the cross-half signal is referenced to the mean effect of the unexpressed targets. Targets needed at least 4 cells on every side of every split, and all cell numbers used the same targets (donor 1: 1,178 resting and 1,151 stimulated unexpressed targets; donor 2: 709 and 725). The between-half Pearson correlation is the mean over targets of the per-target correlation between half-effects, and the Spearman–Brown value is 2r/(1 + r). 

## TRBC clone marker 

We used cells with at least two TRBC1 plus TRBC2 counts and their TRBC1 share f = TRBC1/(TRBC1 + TRBC2). For a perturbation with n such cells, z = (mean f − f0)/(sd f/√n), where f 0 and sd f are the mean and standard deviation over all cells, so that var(z) ≈ 1 under random assignment. var(z) used perturbations with at least 30 cells, and its permutation value is the mean over five shuffles of perturbation 24 labels. The deviation of a set of cells is mean f − f0; its reproducibility is its Pearson correlation between two halves (at least 15 cells per half), and between states it was correlated over the perturbations of the main analysis with at least 30 cells carrying TRBC counts in both states (6,582 in donor 1 and 4,963 in donor 2). If the n cells of a perturbation come from K equally sized clones with intraclass correlation ICC of the TRBC1 share, E[z 2] ≈ 1 + ICC(n/K − 1), so K ≤ n/z 2 for ICC 

≤ 1. We report the median of n/z 2 over resting perturbations with z 2 > 1 (85–91% of them), with all sgRNAs of a perturbation pooled. The screen transduced 30 million cells per donor at a multiplicity of infection of about 0.2 with 23,256 sgRNAs 3, about 250 transduced cells per sgRNA and 500 per perturbation. 

Constant-region halves. Cells in which only TRBC2 was detected, with at least 4 counts, formed one class; cells with a TRBC1 share of at least 0.5 and at least 2 counts formed the other; the remaining cells were not used in this test. The cells of one clone carry one TCR β rearrangement, so the two classes share almost no clone. The exception is a cell of a TRBC1 clone in which only TRBC2 counts happened to be detected: for a true TRBC1 share of 0.5 and 4 counts this has a probability of about 6%. Such cells let the halves share a few clones and make the test conservative. For each target, the two classes formed the two halves, and random halves of the same classified cells, with two independent random bits (seed 31), served as the comparison. Each cell was first referenced to the mean of control cells of the same class, which removes expression differences between the two classes of T cells that are unrelated to the perturbation; genes had a control mean of at least 0.1, without TCR genes. Each class and each random half needed at least 10 cells. For unexpressed and expressed targets we report the share with a positive cross-half signal, the mean cross-half signal and one-sided paired sign and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests of random minus constant-region halves. About 1.0–1.3 million cells were classified per donor and state. 

## Clone barcodes in brain organoids 

The processed single-cell object of Li et al. 20 (Zenodo 7083558) contains 28,344 cells of the first batch of eight libraries, each with the sgRNA and cell type assigned by the authors. Amplicon sequencing of the clone barcodes came from ArrayExpress E-MTAB-13148 (82 file pairs of the first batch). Read 1 holds a 16-base cell barcode and a 12-base UMI; in read 2, a 16-base insert follows the fixed sequence GGTTCGCCGG, ends in a fixed G and is followed by GCGCGC, and 98% of reads had this structure. We took the first 15 bases of the insert as the clone barcode, allowing at most one mismatch in the flanking sequence and no N. Files were downloaded in parts and parsed as they arrived, without storing the reads, and accepted only if both reads had the same number of records. Clones were called as in the original study. Within a cell barcode and UMI, clone barcodes with fewer than 20% of the reads of the most abundant one were removed; combinations with fewer reads than 1% of the library’s median maximal UMI read count per cell were removed; and a clone barcode one base away from a more abundant one was merged into it. A cell’s clone is its clone barcode with the most UMIs, required to have at least 2 UMIs and at least twice the UMIs of the second. Clones were called for 10,967 cells (39%). Of the clones with at least 2 cells, 82% carried a single sgRNA, 25 and in clones with several sgRNAs 86% of the cells carried the most frequent one. A clone was defined as one clone barcode within one perturbation, so a barcode shared by different sgRNAs can only merge different clones of one perturbation, which makes clone halves conservative. 36% of the clones occurred in more than one library: the transduced cells expanded before they were divided among organoids, so library halves are not fully independent replicates. Expression was log1p CP10K for genes with a mean of at least 0.1 over all cells. The deviation of a cell is its expression minus the mean of the cells of other perturbations in the same library and cell type ( celltype cl coarse2 ); a second reference used the library only, which includes differences in cell-type composition. Perturbations needed at least 60 cells with a called clone (32 perturbations). The cross-half signals of random halves and of clone halves (each clone assigned as a whole to a random half) are means over 1,000 splits; with 50 splits, the order of the two for the perturbation with the fewest cells (DEAF1, 64 cells) changed with the draw. In the permutation test, the clone labels were shuffled among the cells of each perturbation, keeping the clone sizes, 200 times, and clone halves were formed from the shuffled labels; the statistic is the mean over perturbations of the random-half minus the clone-half signal, divided by the per-cell variance. 

## Clone strength and the effective number of clones 

Clone strength is the energy of the deviation that the two halves of a null perturbation share, divided by the per-cell variance. In data with several null sgRNAs, the cells of each null sgRNA were split at random (mean over 20 splits), which gives the random-half signal; the first half of one null sgRNA was paired with the second half of another (50 random pairings), which gives the signal between two different null perturbations; their difference was divided by the per-cell variance of the null cells (the sum over genes of the variance around the mean of null cells of the same sample). Different null perturbations share no clone, so the signal between them removes the common shift that the reference introduces. Each effect was referenced to cells of the same sample (10x channel, library, mouse or donor), with two references: the null cells of the sample (excluding the null sgRNA’s own cells), or all assigned cells of the sample (excluding the perturbation’s own cells). With few null sgRNAs, the first reference carries the clone states of the other null sgRNAs, which raises the random-half signal and lowers the signal between different null perturbations; in the second, each sgRNA’s clones carry little weight. We report the range over both references. sgRNAs needed at least 30 cells. Data with a single null sgRNA (the GFP control of Jin et al.; Control2 of the organoid screen) cannot be estimated in this way; for these we used, over all pertur-bations, the random-half signal minus that of halves sharing no clone: clone halves in the organoids, and library halves for Jin et al. (different libraries are independent transductions of different batches of embryos, but each library pools several embryos, so this is an upper bound on the clonal part). These two screens have open symbols in Fig. 4c. 26 The effective number of clones is K eff = 1/Σ p k2, with p k the share of clone k among a perturbation’s cells with a called clone. The ICC was estimated by one-way random-effects analysis of variance, with between-clone and within-clone mean squares MSB and MSW summed over genes, V clone = (MSB − MSW)/n 0, n 0 = (N −

Σ n k2/N)/(K − 1) and ICC = V clone /(V clone + MSW). The slope of clone strength on Σ p k2 through the origin was compared with the median ICC over perturbations, both with 95% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of perturbations. For the out-of-sample prediction, perturbations were split at random into two halves (500 times), and the median ICC of one half times each perturbation’s Σ p k2 in the other half was compared with its measured clone strength. The two clone readouts of CD4 are the TRBC readout, the excess variance of each sgRNA’s mean TRBC1 share over its sampling variance, divided by the per-cell variance of the share and averaged over sgRNAs, and the clone strength of null perturbations above (geometric mean of the two references). By the relation, each equals its own ICC divided by the same K eff , so their ratio does not depend on the donor. The statistical model, the derivation and five propositions with their symbolic checks and recovery in simulated screens are given in Supplementary Note 1. 

## Ceilings and reliability labels 

We ran cell-eval version 0.8.2 7 in an isolated environment on resting cells of donor 1, with at least 40 cells per sgRNA: 150 unexpressed targets drawn at random and the 150 targets with the largest mean knockdown among those whose two sgRNAs both knocked down the target, the weaker reaching a ∆log1p of at most −0.25 and at least 70% of the stronger. Each perturbation was capped at 400 cells, 15,000 control cells were drawn, and cells were downsampled to equal numbers per sgRNA (median 62 per side); perturbations with fewer than 10 cells per side were dropped. The random-half ceiling is the tool’s compute ceiling with seeds 0–2. The sgRNA ceiling runs the tool’s compute with one sgRNA’s cells as “real” and the other’s as “prediction”, in both directions, and applies the tool’s Spearman–Brown function to the mean over perturbations. Following ref. 6, the reliability ρ is the Spearman–Brown value of the median between-half Pearson correlation, and perturbations with ρ ≥ 0.5 are labelled reliable; for random halves the median was taken over the three halvings r1, r2 and r1 ⊕ r2, and undefined values counted as not reliable. To split the random-half signal of expressed targets into three parts, the share reproduced by the other sgRNA is the ratio of the sgRNA-half to the random-half cross-half signal, for the same state and targets. To separate the remainder into sgRNA-specific and transduction-level parts, we formed four pseudobulk effects per target and state from all four donors: sgRNA rank 0 or 1 in donors 1–2 or 3–4. The same-sgRNA design pairs each sgRNA across the two donor groups, and the different-sgRNA design pairs sgRNA 0 with sgRNA 1. Their difference, divided by the different-sgRNA signal, estimates sgRNA-specific energy relative to the signal shared across sgRNAs (0.80 resting, 0.48 stimulated); multiplied by the single-cell sgRNA-half share, it gives the sgRNA-specific part, and the rest is the transduction-level part. The single-cell sgRNA-half signal also contains donor-specific responses, so this transfer tends to overestimate the sgRNA-specific part. 27 Shesha split-half reproducibility 

We installed shesha-geometry 0.2.29 from PyPI 32 and ran its split-half reproducibil-ity function ( shesha.bio.split half reproducibility ; 50 random splits, base seed 320, at least 30 cells) on resting cells of CD4 donor 1: all cells of the unexpressed tar-gets, of 400 expressed targets drawn at random (seed 5) and 20,000 non-targeting cells drawn at random as controls, as log1p CP10K expression of the 2,000 genes with the highest control mean among those kept above (TCR genes excluded). For the compar-ison with independent replicates, we applied the same cosine to fixed halves formed by the two sgRNAs of each target (at least 15 cells each), with the same control centroid. Targets with both values were compared (1,438 unexpressed and 229 expressed). 

## Common and specific parts, and the predictor 

For two contexts X and Y, the common part is (X + Y)/2 and the specific part is (X − Y)/2, after centering each context over perturbations. The specific share is the cross-half signal of the specific part divided by the sum of the cross-half signals of both parts. The features of a perturbation are its half-A common part projected on the top k right singular vectors of the training perturbations’ common parts (k = 20). A ridge regression maps the features to the specific part in half B, with λ chosen from {10 −3,. . . , 10 2} × tr(Z TZ)/k by five-fold inner cross-validation, and perturbations were split into five outer folds. The explained share is (Σ ∥dB∥2 − Σ∥dB − F∥2)/Σ d A · dB,where F is the held-out prediction and the denominator is the reproducible energy of the specific part. A shuffled control, permuting the features across perturbations, gave values near zero throughout. For K562 and RPE1, perturbations needed at least 20 cells in all three screens, and the independent common part was (other K562 screen + RPE1 half A)/2. Strength-matched knockdowns in CD4 are expressed targets whose two sgRNAs both knocked down the target as above, in both states (615 targets in donor 1 and 470 in donor 2). 

## Model and simulation 

A cell of perturbation p in context c from clone k has expression μpc (1 + g k) + a k + e, with clone state a k ˜ N(0, σa2I), clone gain g k ˜ N(0, σg2) and noise e ˜ N(0, σe2I). For random halves, E[x A · xB] = |μ|2(1 + σg2/K eff ) + G σa2/K eff ; for independent halves it is |μ|2. The simulation used G = 1,000 genes, 800 perturbations (20% null), true responses of rank 10 and log-normal effect sizes. Each perturbation and transduction has K = 6 clones with Dirichlet(0.5) weights, σa = 0.2, σg = 0.6 and σe = 1, and each half draws 60 cells from the clones. Clones are shared between contexts (additive part scaled by 0.5 in the second) or separate. In the scenario without true structure (Fig. 6e), the effect scale was 0.1, the specific parts were unrelated to the common parts and clone states lay along the common response directions. In the scenario with true structure (Fig. 6f), part of each specific part was a linear function of the common part, set so that the predictor explains 70% without clone effects, and σa was 0.2, 0.6, 1.2, 1.5 or 2.0, either in random directions or along the common response directions; 

σa of 1.5 and 2.0 give clone-to-signal energy ratios of 4.1–4.2 and 7.2–7.6, the range of the CD4 screen. The clone-to-signal energy ratio is the null cross-half signal under 28 random halves divided by the expressed cross-half signal under independent halves. The predictor was a two-fold version of the one above (k = 10), and results are means over three seeds. 

## Transfer models 

For each CD4 donor we used resting and stimulated (48 h) cells. Test targets were the unexpressed targets and strength-matched knockdowns of the ceiling analysis whose two sgRNAs each had at least 40 cells in both states; 1,000 further targets were drawn at random for training. Each test sgRNA contributed at most 200 cells per state, each training sgRNA at most 80, and 6,000 non-targeting cells were used per state. Genes were the 2,000 with the highest variance of training-target effects among genes with a control mean of at least 0.05 in both states, together with the measured test targets. Perturbations were defined by sgRNA; the resting cells of test sgRNAs entered training and their stimulated cells were held out. This is the few-shot split of the State code ([fewshot] : specified perturbations of one cell type are held out and everything else is used for training), with the stimulated state as that cell type. The effect of an sgRNA is the mean expression of its cells minus that of control cells of the same state. The score is the Pearson correlation over genes between the predicted and the answer effect (target gene excluded), averaged over test sgRNAs, with standard errors clustered by target. The answer is the stimulated cells of the same sgRNA (shared clones) or of the other sgRNA against the same target (another transduction); in the cross-donor control, it is the stimulated cells of the same or the other sgRNA in another donor. Versions, settings, deviations from the authors’ defaults and checks that each model learned per-sgRNA effects are given in Supplementary Table S6. The melanoma screen 27 was used in its scPerturb version, with cells carrying a single sgRNA, from control to IFN-γ treatment; sgRNAs needed at least 30 cells in both conditions, and test perturbations were cross-fitted in two folds. For the comparison, the CD4 cells of each test sgRNA were subsampled to the cell numbers of the melanoma data (48 resting and 75 stimulated). 

## Statistics 

Confidence intervals are 2.5th–97.5th bootstrap percentiles over 1,000 resamples of perturbations, unless stated otherwise. They describe variation over perturbations within one experiment; agreement across the available independent replicates (two donors in the CD4 single-cell analyses, four in its pseudobulk and for the transfer models, and two K562 screens) is reported separately. Sign tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests are one-sided and paired over targets or perturbations.

Contents: Supplementary Note 1; Supplementary Tables S1–S7; Supplementary Figures S1–S3. 33 Supplementary Note 1 | Statistical model of clone strength: propositions and checks 

Model. Consider one perturbation measured on G genes. Its cells come from K clones; clone k has n k cells, a share p k = n k/N of all N cells. Cell i belongs to clone k(i), and its expression vector, after subtracting a fixed reference, is x i = μ + c k(i) + e i. Here μ is the perturbation’s true effect; c k is the deviation of clone k, independent between clones, with mean zero and variance σc2 per gene; e i is the cell’s own variation and sampling noise, independent between cells, with mean zero and variance σe2 per gene. The intraclass correlation is ICC = σc2/( σc2 + σe2), the share of the differences between single cells that is set by clones. A null perturbation has μ = 0. Clone deviations and cell noise are assumed independent of each other and of the perturbation, and the reference is estimated from many cells, so that its error can be neglected (the handling of reference error is described in Methods, Clone strength). 

Proposition 1 (expected cross-half signal). Let A and B be two sets of cells of a perturbation with no cell in common, in which clone k has shares a k and b k. Then E[¯ xA · ¯xB ] = |μ|2 + G σc2 Σk akbk. If A and B share no clone (independent replicates), the second term is zero and only the energy of the true effect, |μ|2, remains. Reason. 

¯xA = μ + Σ k akck + ¯ eA. In the expanded inner product only the terms of the same clone, E[c k · ck] = G σc2, are non-zero; A and B have no cell in common, so ¯ eA and ¯eB are uncorrelated and do not enter. Check. With two clones and symbolic shares, the expansion by sympy equals the right-hand side. 

Proposition 2 (random halves and clone strength). When the N cells are split into two halves by a fair coin, E[Σ k akbk] = Σ k pk2 (1 + O(1/N)). For a null perturbation, the difference between the cross-half signals of random halves and inde-pendent replicates, divided by the per-cell variance G( σc2 + σe2), is therefore the clone strength, ICC × Σk pk2 = ICC/K eff with K eff = 1/Σ k pk2, and it does not change with the number of cells. Reason. The number of cells of clone k in the first half is binomial, Bin(n k, 1/2), and each half has about N/2 cells, so the leading term is (n k/2)(n k/2)/((N/2)(N/2)) = p k2. Check. The leading term was verified with sympy. The estimator was tested in simulated screens with known truth: ICC of 0.01, 0.05 and 0.2, 5–200 clones, coefficients of variation of clone size of 0.5–2, 100 or 400 cells per perturbation and 60 null perturbations. Computed as on the data (random halves minus two different null perturbations, divided by the per-cell variance), clone strength correlated with the true ICC × Σ p k2 at 0.999, with a ratio of 0.98. 

Proposition 3 (effective number of clones, transduction and expansion). 

If N 0 cells were transduced per perturbation and the clone sizes after expansion have mean m and coefficient of variation CV, then to leading order Σ k pk2 = (1 + CV 2)/N 0,that is, K eff = N 0/(1 + CV 2). Reason. Σk pk2 = Σ k nk2/(Σ k nk)2 ≈ N0E[n 2]/(N 0m) 2,and E[n 2] = m 2(1 + CV 2). Check. Verified with sympy. The relation is approximate: it treats the sequenced cells as a proportional reduction of the clone sizes, and when only a small fraction of cells is sequenced, fewer than N 0 clones are observed. 

Proposition 4 (limit of the Spearman–Brown extrapolation). For a null perturbation with a fixed number of clones and σc > 0, the correlation over genes between the two half-means tends to 1 as the number of cells grows, and so does the Spearman–Brown corrected reliability; yet its effect in an independent experiment 34 is zero. The Spearman–Brown formula extrapolates the agreement within the same clones. Reason. With more cells the noise terms of both halves vanish, and both halves tend to the same Σ k pkck. Check. In a simulated null perturbation from 4 clones with an ICC of 0.05, the split-half correlation rose from 0.23 to 0.95 and the corrected reliability from 0.38 to 0.98 as the number of cells rose from 50 to 3,200. 

Proposition 5 (leakage across contexts). Let two contexts R and S contain the same clones, with clone k deviating by c k in R and by qc k in S. Let the common part be s = (x R + x S)/2 and the specific part d = (x R − xS)/2, taken from two sets of cells A and B with no cell in common. Then E[s A · dB] = G σc2 (1 + q)(1 − q)/4 

× Σk akbk. When q ̸ = 1 and the two sets share clones this is non-zero, so a predictor of d B from s A can score positively even without any true cross-context relation; when they share no clone it is zero. Check. Verified with sympy. The formal tests reported in the main text are summarized in Supplementary Table N1.
