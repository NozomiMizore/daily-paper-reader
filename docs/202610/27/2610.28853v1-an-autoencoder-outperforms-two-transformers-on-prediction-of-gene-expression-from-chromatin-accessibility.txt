# An autoencoder outperforms two transformers on prediction of gene expression from chromatin accessibility 

Jesse G. Meyer 1

> 1

Department of Computational Biomedicine, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, Los Angeles, CA, USA 

Draft of October 22, 2026 

Abstract 

Models that predict RNA abundance from chromatin accessibility are rarely compared under the same features, targets and metrics. We built one benchmark on 126,604 paired single-nucleus profiles from 19 human studies with a shared vocabulary of 50,000 peaks and 3,185 genes, ten 5×2 cross-validation folds and one scoring target, and compared seven implemented pipelines and two reference predictors across 70 trained runs; each pipeline keeps its own input normalisation and native training target. The scButterfly-B pipeline, built on dual variational autoencoders, outperformed every transformer pipeline, including an autoregressive Perceiver model (Omic Translator) and a support-free reformulation of Cisformer, on per-gene and within-cell Spearman correlation in all sixty paired fold comparisons, with 3.8 times shorter training. Transformer pipelines come within 0.02–0.04 on within-cell profile shape but lag by 0.10–0.24 on per-gene correlation. On two withheld cell types per-gene correlation is low; controls show the podocyte values are typical of within-type correlation for seen cell types of the same size, whereas Paneth values match a cell-agnostic shuffle for every Omic Translator arm. Within the transformer family, prespecified ablations show the Perceiver encoder was worth +0 .0439 in within-donor per-gene correlation over full self-attention, a chromosome-aware genomic attention prior reduced it by 0.0096 (exact donor-level sign-flip test, Holm-adjusted p = 1 .2 × 10 −4), and decoder output order had no detectable effect. A training-mean profile reaches 0.314 within-cell correlation with no input and a constant at the true expressed-gene positions reaches 0.996 per-gene correlation, so oracle-support protocols are reported only as diagnostics. Code, results and an audited release are provided. 

## 1 Introduction 

Paired single-cell measurements of chromatin accessibility and RNA abundance offer a direct supervised signal for learning how regulatory state maps to transcriptional output. A model that learned this mapping well could impute an unmeasured modality, denoise sparse measurements, and, if its internal structure were interpretable, point at regulatory elements that matter for particular genes. A number of architectures have been proposed for the translation task. BABEL couples modality-specific autoencoders through a shared latent space [1]; scButterfly aligns two variational autoencoders with discriminators and supports translation in both directions [2]; Polarbear trains semi-supervised autoencoders on partially paired data [3]; CMOT frames translation as optimal transport [4]; MultiVI and GLUE learn joint embeddings that can be decoded into either 1arXiv:2610.28853v1 [q-bio.GN] 20 Oct 2026 modality [5, 6]. More recently, attention-based models such as Cisformer [7] and foundation models pre-trained on large ATAC compendia [8, 9] have been applied to the same problem. Two things make it difficult to learn from these papers collectively. The first is that they rarely share a feature space, a split, or a metric, so a reported improvement can come from any of those choices rather than from the architecture. The second is that several of them supply information at inference time that a deployed imputation model would not have. Cisformer, for example, is evaluated by asking the decoder for abundance values only at genes already known from the target RNA profile to be expressed. That is a legitimate task, but it is a different and much easier task than generating an RNA profile from chromatin alone: we show below that a constant written at the true expressed-gene positions scores 0.996 on per-gene correlation without any model. Recent benchmarking of single-cell deep learning in other settings has found that simple baselines are often competitive with or better than large models [10, 11], which makes it important to include deliberately trivial controls rather than only published competitors. This paper has two aims. The first is to test, under prespecified contrasts with seeds, batch size, peak cap and hardware held fixed, which parts of a transformer translator actually contribute to accuracy. The architecture we test, Omic Translator, was designed around three ideas that seemed sensible a priori: that compressing thousands of sparse peak events into a small set of latents is better than attending over all of them [12]; that attention from a latent to a peak should be biased toward peaks on the same chromosome and penalised with genomic distance, in the spirit of linear attention biases [13]; and that an autoregressive decoder should emit genes in a fixed order so that earlier emissions can condition later ones. Only the first of these survived contact with the data. The second aim is to place that model family against published pipelines in a way that is honest about what the comparison can and cannot support. We compare the implemented Omic Translator, scButterfly-B and support-free Cisformer pipelines, each run with its own input normalisation and native training target, against one common scoring target on 126,604 paired nuclei from 19 human studies, with ten 5 ×2 cross-validation folds [14], every metric recomputed from saved cell-level predictions, inference clustered on the donor, and two reference predictors that bracket the metric scale. The result is a clear ranking of pipelines that we decline to generalise into a statement about architecture classes or about models trained on equivalent targets, for reasons we spell out. 

## 2 Results 

2.1 A benchmark in which only the model changes 

The experimental design is summarised in Figure 1. The data are 126,604 quality-controlled nuclei with paired snATAC-seq and snRNA-seq from the multi-tissue atlas distributed with EpiFoun-dation [8], which draws on 19 ENCODE [15] and GEO studies, one study per tissue (Methods; Supplementary Table S1). All pipelines receive the same 50,000-peak accessibility vocabulary and are scored against the same 3,185-gene target matrix with one metric implementation. The feature vocabularies were selected once on the whole atlas before any split was made, using the RNA values of every cell including those later held out, so feature selection is transductive; each pipeline then applies its own input normalisation and trains on its own native target (Methods). What is compared is the implemented pipelines as run, scored on a common target. Two cell types, podocytes ( n = 299, kidney cortex) and Paneth cells ( n = 345, ileum), were removed from an initial stratified partition and serve as withheld sets. The 18,947 cells of that partition’s test fraction form a fixed same-atlas holdout that no run trains or validates on. The remaining 107,013 cells form the 2cross-validation pool. We use 5 ×2 cross-validation: five independent random bisections of the pool, stratified by tissue and cell-type annotation, each used in both directions, giving ten folds in which every cell in the pool is a test cell exactly five times. Within each training half, 10% is carved out as an inner validation set that is the only signal for early stopping. The test half of each fold contains 53,506 or 53,507 cells and is scored in full; we do not subsample cells for any metric. The ten folds are not ten independent observations, and we never treat them as such; inference is described below. The atlas object carries no donor, sample or library identifiers, and the per-study accession is one-to-one with tissue, so group-protected splitting was not possible without holding out whole tissues. We therefore split at the cell level and treat the cross-validation estimates as in-distribution generalisation. Donor identity is, however, recoverable from the upstream ENCODE and GEO records: the 11 ENCODE accessions come from 8 donors, one of whom contributes colon, adrenal gland and fallopian tube and another muscle and bile duct, while each GEO study is a single sample (Supplementary Table S1). The independent sampling unit for inference is therefore the donor, with 16 units rather than 19 studies, and the statistical analysis below is clustered accordingly. Seven trained pipelines enter the matrix: five variants of Omic Translator that implement the prespecified ablations, scButterfly-B, and Cisformer in a support-free mode. Two reference predictors accompany them. 2.2 Two metrics and two reference predictors 

We report two Spearman correlations (Figure 1d). The per-gene correlation ρgene asks, for each gene, whether the model orders cells correctly by that gene’s abundance, and then averages over evaluable genes, that is, genes whose target varies across the scored cells (3,145 of 3,185 on a cross-validation half). A gene whose target varies but whose prediction is constant across cells receives ρgene = 0 rather than being dropped, so a sparse decoder cannot raise its average by emitting only easy genes. 

Coverage is the fraction of evaluable genes for which a model’s prediction varies across cells; it is a property of the gene axis, not a count of genes emitted per cell. The within-cell correlation ρcell 

asks, for each cell, whether the model orders all 3,185 genes correctly within that cell, including the genes it predicts as zero, and averages over cells. The two metrics have different reference points, and we use two deliberately simple predictors to 4mark them rather than treating either as a mathematical bound. A profile equal to the training-mean expression, tiled across every test cell without looking at chromatin, has ρgene = 0 because every column is constant, but it reaches ρcell = 0 .314 because much of the within-cell rank structure is shared across cells. A within-cell value near 0.314 is therefore consistent with no cell-specific information, although matching that one scalar does not by itself show that a model predicts the average cell. At the other end, a constant 1 written at exactly the positions where the target is non-zero scores ρgene = 0 .996 and ρcell = 0 .999. Any protocol that conditions on target support, including the published Cisformer evaluation, must be read against that diagnostic, which is why we evaluate Cisformer support-free and report oracle-support results only as diagnostics. 

2.3 Genomic inductive biases reduce accuracy; the Perceiver encoder is what helps 

The four prespecified contrasts are shown in Figure 2 and Table 1. Each compares two Omic Translator variants that differ in one declared setting, with model seed, batch size, peak cap, learning-rate schedule and GPU model identical. Three of the contrasts change nothing but a mask or an ordering. The fourth, Perceiver versus full attention, replaces the two cross-attention and two latent self-attention blocks with two full self-attention blocks over the 8,192 tokens, gives the decoder an 8,192-slot memory instead of 256 latents, and changes the parameter count from 19.78 to 18.13 million; it is a comparison of those two implemented encoders, not an isolation of compression alone.

2.5 The three families emit very different numbers of genes 

The three model families produce very different numbers of non-zero genes per cell (Figure 4). The target contains 233 non-zero genes per cell on average. Omic Translator emits about 270, scButterfly 546, and Cisformer, whose output is a softmax expectation over abundance classes, is dense and emits all 3,185. Matching the target count is not evidence of correct support or calibrated probabilities, and we make no such claim. What the counts do show is that the per-gene ranking is not a consequence of how many genes a model attempts: Cisformer attempts every gene and still scores below every Perceiver variant of Omic Translator. Whether scButterfly’s advantage comes from identifying which genes are expressed or from ordering cells correctly given the support cannot be separated with these metrics; a support precision-recall analysis on a common gene mask is the experiment that would separate them, and we list it as future work. 2.7 Per-gene accuracy is low on two withheld cell types 

Per-gene correlation is low on both withheld cell types (Figure 5; Table 4). On Paneth cells every pipeline is at or indistinguishable from zero and 4 of the seven are negative. On podocytes the best pipeline, scButterfly-B, reaches 0.117, about a third of its in-distribution value, and the transformer pipelines reach 0.02–0.07. Two controls computed from the saved predictions put these values in context (Table 5; Methods), and they separate the two withheld sets. First, a per-gene correlation 9computed within one cell type is low for seen types too: matched-size subsets ( n = 299) of the 26 seen annotations with enough cells in the cross-validation test half give a median within-type per-gene correlation of 0.034 for the primary Omic Translator (10th to 90th percentile 0.003–0.124). The podocyte value lies above the median of those seen-type subsets (60% of them score lower), so on podocytes the pipelines do about as well as they do within a seen cell type of the same size; the drop from the pooled in-distribution value reflects the within-type nature of the statistic more than a failure to transfer. The Paneth value, by contrast, lies below nearly every seen-type subset (2% score lower). Second, shuffling each pipeline’s prediction rows among the cells of a withheld set, which keeps its outputs but destroys their assignment to cells, gives per-gene correlations of -0.000 on podocytes and 0.000 on Paneth cells. On podocytes the observed value exceeds the shuffle null in 10 of ten folds for the primary Omic Translator, and in ten of ten for every pipeline; on Paneth cells it does so in 0 of ten for the primary Omic Translator and for every other Omic Translator arm, in seven of ten for scButterfly-B, and in ten of ten for Cisformer, whose Paneth value of 0.012 is tiny but consistently above chance. So the pipelines carry some cell-specific information about podocytes and essentially none about Paneth cells, where the claim of an absent signal is supported by the shuffle control. The within-cell picture differs between the two sets and must not be pooled, and it is where the no-input reference earns its place (Figure 5b). On podocytes, 4 of the seven models, including the primary Omic Translator, score no higher than the training-mean profile’s 0.351. Read without that reference, within-cell values of 0.33–0.35 would look like moderate success; with it, they are consistent with the models reproducing shared profile structure rather than anything podocyte-specific. On Paneth cells every model except the full-attention arm scores well above the reference, with scButterfly at 0.657; profile shape is partly recovered there even though cell-to-cell variation is not. Several things limit what this experiment can say. The two withheld sets are small (299 and 345 cells), come from single studies that are themselves represented in training, and have far fewer evaluable genes (2,048 and 1,425 against 3,145 in a cross-validation half), so the per-gene statistic is not on the same footing as the in-distribution one. The ileum study is an engineered cell line rather than primary tissue (Supplementary Table S1). The experiment is a cell-type holdout within seen studies, not a held-out donor or held-out study experiment, and nothing here speaks to held-out-donor generalisation. 12 3 Discussion 

The most useful result here is a negative one about design intuition. The two genomic inductive biases built into Omic Translator were motivated by the biology: regulatory elements act mostly in 

cis , and proximity to a gene is the single most predictive feature for enhancer-gene linkage. Encoding that as a chromosome mask and a distance penalty on latent-to-peak attention nonetheless made the model worse, reproducibly, on both metrics. We think the explanation is that the prior is in the wrong place. The latents are not genes; they are a learned compression of the whole accessibility profile, and the decoder that emits genes never sees genomic coordinates at all. A latent anchored to a stretch of chromosome 7 is forbidden from integrating a cell-state signal that happens to be spread over chromosome 12, even though that signal is what the decoder needs. A cis prior would have to be expressed between peaks and the specific genes being emitted, which is a different architecture, and nothing here speaks against biological priors implemented that way. The null result for output order points the same way: the decoder does not appear to exploit genomic adjacency between consecutive emissions, so ordering them genomically buys nothing detectable. The one encoder change that helped, replacing full self-attention over 8,192 sparse tokens with a Perceiver, is also the least biologically specific. Its benefit is partly optimisation: the full-attention arm is unstable across folds and stops early, and its within-cell correlation is barely above the no-input reference. Because the contrast also changes the decoder memory and the parameter count, and because one shared training recipe does not guarantee that both encoders are comparably tuned, we read it as evidence that the implemented Perceiver configuration trains better, not as a general statement about compression. The benchmark result is equally clear and more limited in what it licenses. The scButterfly-B pipeline beats every transformer pipeline we trained, in every fold, on both metrics, at about a quarter of the training time. We do not conclude that autoencoders beat transformers for this task, for three reasons. First, it is one autoencoder-based pipeline against one transformer family plus one adapted external transformer; the class-level comparison has n = 1 on each side. Second, the contrast is not controlled: scButterfly brings its own preprocessing, TF-IDF transformation, panel- normalised training target, output space and training objective, so nothing in the comparison isolates “autoencoder”, and the three pipelines are not trained on equivalent targets. Third, the three families produce outputs of very different sparsity, and our metrics cannot separate support identification from abundance ordering given the support. What the data support is narrower: on this atlas, under support-free prediction with a shared feature vocabulary and a common scoring target, the scButterfly-B pipeline as implemented is the strongest tested, and its advantage lies in cross-cell per-gene variation rather than within-cell profile shape, where the best transformer pipeline is within 0.02. The withheld-type controls also change how the two withheld sets should be read. A per-gene correlation computed within one cell type has far less cell-to-cell variation to explain than the pooled multi-tissue statistic, and the matched-size seen-type subsets show that values of a few hundredths are the norm, not the exception, for every pipeline. By that yardstick podocytes are unremarkable: the pipelines recover about as much cell-specific signal there as within a seen type of the same size, and more than a shuffle of their own outputs. Paneth cells are the genuine failure, below nearly every seen-type subset and at the shuffle null for every Omic Translator arm; whether that reflects the engineered origin of the ileum sample, the distinctive secretory programme, or a true limit of chromatin-based prediction cannot be decided from one population. The reference predictors deserve more attention than they usually get. A constant written on 13 the true support scores 0.996; a dense model evaluated that way cannot be distinguished from a lookup of which genes are on. Our support-free Cisformer reformulation, which queries all 3,185 genes with a fully open decoder mask and supervises the zero class, is stricter than the published protocol, and its placement near the bottom of the ranking should be read as a statement about that stricter task, not about the published model. The training-mean profile is just as important in the other direction: 4 of seven models on podocytes achieve within-cell correlations that look respectable in isolation and are in fact no higher than what a model with no input achieves. Several limitations shape how far these results travel. Feature selection used the whole atlas, including the RNA values of cells later held out, and scButterfly’s own preprocessing statistics were fitted on the pooled object, so the protocol is transductive with respect to the feature space and the baseline’s normalisation; a vocabulary fixed across folds is not an independently defined vocabulary, and we make no claim of fully inductive evaluation. The three pipelines are trained on different native representations of the same counts, and rank metrics do not make those equivalent for the per-gene statistic (Methods); the ranking is a ranking of pipelines on a common scoring target, not of architectures on equivalent targets. The split is at cell level, so cells from one donor appear in both halves of every fold; the cross-validation numbers are in-distribution estimates and say nothing about held-out-donor generalisation. The withheld cell types are the only evidence about transfer, and that evidence is limited in the ways described above. The decoding cap of 384 steps was reached in a substantial fraction of cells; re-decoding at 768 steps raised the Omic Translator per-gene scores by about +0.0108 on a test subset, which does not change any ranking but means the reported Omic Translator values are slight underestimates of what the checkpoints can produce. The ranking family was declared post hoc. Each model was early-stopped on its own validation loss, so the selection rule is harmonised but the quantity is not. And the whole study is one atlas with one feature vocabulary; whether the ordering holds under a different peak set or a different normalisation is an open question. We think the practical recommendations are simple. Benchmarks of chromatin-to-expression translation should state whether target support is supplied at inference, include a no-input profile reference and a support-only diagnostic, report both per-gene and within-cell correlation, charge zero for genes a model does not vary on or report coverage alongside, recover and use the true independent sampling unit in the inference, and prespecify architectural contrasts before looking at results. None of these is hard, and in our hands each one changed a conclusion we would otherwise have drawn. 

## 4 Methods 

4.1 Paired multiome atlas 

The data are a paired snATAC-seq/snRNA-seq compendium of human tissues assembled as a Seurat weighted-nearest-neighbour object [16, 17] and distributed with the EpiFoundation resource [8]; the underlying libraries come from ENCODE [15] and GEO. The object contains 133,549 nuclei, 36,601 measured genes and 57 cell-type annotations from 19 studies, each study contributing one tissue (Supplementary Table S1). The RNA matrix is stored in Seurat LogNormalize form, log(1 + cig / N i × 10 4) for count cig and library size Ni; we verified by exact inversion on the stored matrix that integer counts are recovered (maximum deviation 6 × 10 −12 ), and the released preparation route repeats that check before writing the library-size file. The accession identifier is the only grouping variable in the object and is one-to-one with tissue. Donor identity was recovered 14 after the fact from the ENCODE experiment records (biosample donor accession) and the GEO sample records; the mapping is released with the code. The 11 ENCODE accessions correspond to 8 donors; the 8 GEO studies are single samples without cross-series donor identifiers and are treated as one unit each, giving 16 independent units. We applied the following cell-level filters: ATAC fragment count ≥ 1,000, ATAC features ≥ 500, nucleosome signal ≤ 4, TSS enrichment ≥ 2, RNA UMI count ≥ 500, RNA features ≥ 200, and mitochondrial fraction ≤ 20%. The library-size range in the object (1,001–24,997 UMIs) indicates that upstream filtering had already been applied; our filters removed 154 cells on TSS enrichment and 6,791 on mitochondrial fraction, leaving 126,604. 

4.2 Shared feature spaces 

The peak vocabulary is the 50,000 most broadly accessible peaks by number of cells with a non-zero count, computed over all 133,549 nuclei, sorted by chromosome and start coordinate, covering chromosomes 1–22, X and Y. Accessibility is binary: every non-zero entry of the stored ATAC matrix is 1. Cells have on average 4,262 accessible vocabulary peaks (median 3,657). The gene vocabulary was constructed by selecting 5,000 highly variable genes with scanpy [18] on the whole RNA object and intersecting them with protein-coding and lncRNA genes from GENCODE v44 basic [19] that have unique symbols and coordinates, which leaves 3,185 genes ordered by chromosome and transcription start site. Both vocabularies were therefore fixed on the complete atlas before any partition. The selection is transductive: the highly-variable-gene statistics were computed from the RNA values of every cell, including the cells of the fixed holdout, the withheld cell types and every future test half, so held-out RNA informed the choice of target genes; the peak vocabulary likewise used the accessibility of every cell. The vocabulary is identical for every pipeline and fold, which removes it as a source of between-pipeline differences, but a vocabulary fixed across folds is not an independently defined vocabulary, and no part of the evaluation is fully inductive. In this space the target matrix is 92.7% zero and a cell expresses on average 231 vocabulary genes (233 on the cross-validation test halves). 

4.3 Target and native input representations 

The scoring target for every model and every metric is the stored LogNormalize value restricted to the 3,185 vocabulary genes; no further transformation is applied at scoring time. The three models are trained on different representations of the same underlying counts, and we state them explicitly because Spearman correlation across cells is invariant only to a common monotone transformation of a gene’s values, not to a transformation whose scale differs from cell to cell. Omic Translator regresses the stored LogNormalize value directly. scButterfly receives integer counts recovered by inverting the LogNormalize transform with each cell’s library size, restricted to the 3,185-gene panel, and applies its own total-count normalisation over that panel followed by log(1 + x); the per-cell scale factor is therefore the panel total rather than the full library size. Cisformer receives log(1 + count) discretised into eight classes relative to each cell’s own maximum. Rank metrics computed against the common target compare each model’s native output to one reference, but a per-cell rescaling can reorder cells for a given gene, so the per-gene ranking is a comparison of methods as run on their native representations rather than of architectures on an identical target. A common-target sensitivity analysis is the experiment that would remove this ambiguity and is listed as future work. 15 4.4 Held-out sets and cross-validation design 

An initial stratified random split by annotation (70/15/15, seed 42) was made once. All podocytes (n = 299) and Paneth cells ( n = 345) were then removed from every partition of that split and stored as two withheld sets. The 18,947 cells remaining in the 15% test partition form the fixed same-atlas holdout. The union of the remaining training and validation partitions, 107,013 cells from 19 tissues and 55 annotations, is the cross-validation pool. For each of five repetitions (outer seeds 100, 200, 300, 400, 500) the pool was dealt into two halves of 53,506 and 53,507 cells: within each tissue × annotation stratum the cells were permuted and alternated between halves, with the larger side of odd-sized strata alternating across strata so that the halves balance. Direction 0 trains on half A and tests on half B; direction 1 reverses them. Within the training half, an inner validation set of 10% per stratum (5,346–5,364 cells; seed = outer seed + direction) was carved out and used only for early stopping; the remaining 48,143–48,160 cells were used for gradient updates. The generator asserts that train, validation and test are pairwise disjoint and that the test half is disjoint from the holdout and withheld sets. Each split file carries a SHA-256 of its membership in its metadata, and the manifest of those hashes is released. 

4.5 Omic Translator architecture 

Input tokens. Each accessible peak in a cell is one token. A token carries the peak’s vocabulary index, its chromosome (1–22, X, Y) and the integer midpoint of the peak in base pairs. The input embedding is the sum of a learned peak embedding (50 ,001 × 256, index 0 reserved for padding), a learned chromosome embedding (32 × 256) and a linear embedding of the accessibility value, which is constant because accessibility is binary. No positional embedding is added; coordinates enter only through the attention bias described below. Sequences are capped at 8,192 tokens. Cells with more accessible peaks (8.4% of cells) contribute a uniform random subset of 8,192 without replacement, drawn from a seeded hash; at this cap 94.9% of accessibility events are retained. The intended protocol redraws the training subset every epoch from a hash of (model seed, epoch, cell name). In the runs reported here the data-loader workers were persistent and did not receive the epoch update, so each run used one fixed subset per above-cap training cell, drawn with the epoch-1 seed; the released code recreates workers each epoch and a regression test covers it. At evaluation the subset is fixed from a hash of (split hash, cell name), identical across the five Omic Translator arms and across repeated evaluations of one checkpoint. 

Perceiver encoder. The encoder holds 256 learned latent vectors of width 256. Two blocks of cross-attention (4 heads, 64 dimensions per head, pre-LayerNorm) from the latents to the peak tokens, each followed by a feed-forward layer (256–1024–256, GELU, dropout 0.1), are followed by two blocks of latent self-attention with the same feed-forward layer [12, 20]. 

Genomic attention prior. Each latent is assigned a fixed genomic anchor before training: at least one latent per chromosome, the remainder allocated to chromosomes in proportion to their peak counts by largest remainder, and within a chromosome the k anchors placed at the ( i + 12 )/k 

quantiles of that chromosome’s peak midpoints, snapped to an observed peak. Anchors are buffers and are not trained. In the primary configuration the cross-attention logits from latent m to peak l

are set to −∞ if the two are on different chromosomes and otherwise reduced by sh |am − pl|/10 6,where am and pl are the anchor and peak midpoints in base pairs and sh is a fixed per-head slope of 1/2h+1 for heads h = 0 .. 3, that is 0.5, 0.25, 0.125 and 0.0625 per megabase, following the geometric 16 slope schedule of ALiBi [13]. A latent whose chromosome has no accessible peak in a cell receives no cross-attention update and passes through the residual unchanged. The chromosome mask only 

variant keeps the cross-chromosome block and drops the distance term; the no spatial prior variant masks only padding and is invariant to coordinates. The prior adds no parameters. 

Full-attention variant. The full attention arm replaces the Perceiver with a two-layer standard transformer encoder (4 heads, feed-forward 1024, GELU, pre-norm) applying self-attention over all 8,192 peak tokens with a padding mask and no coordinate bias, and the decoder cross-attends to all peak positions rather than to 256 latents. Attention is computed with PyTorch scaled-dot-product kernels so the 8 ,192 2 matrix is not materialised. Because model construction consumes random draws differently in the two encoders, a shared seed does not make the decoder initialisation identical between this arm and the Perceiver arms. 

Autoregressive decoder. A two-layer causal transformer decoder (4 heads, feed-forward 1024, GELU, pre-norm) attends to the encoder memory. Its vocabulary has 3,188 tokens: padding, beginning-of-sequence, end-of-sequence and the 3,185 genes. Three linear heads on each position predict the next gene token, a mean μ and a log-variance for the abundance of the gene at that position. Expression is continuous; there is no binning. The decoder has no positional embedding; order is conveyed by the causal mask and the previously emitted tokens. The training target for a cell is its non-zero vocabulary genes in a fixed order, truncated to 511 genes (affecting about 1% of cells), followed by end-of-sequence. In the primary configuration the order is a single random permutation of the 3,185 genes (seed 1234), identical for every cell, fold and arm; the genomic order 

variant uses chromosome-then-TSS order instead. The Perceiver configurations have 19.78 million parameters, 12.8 million of them in the peak embedding; the full-attention configuration has 18.13 million. 

Objective. With teacher forcing, the loss is 0 .3 LCE + LNLL , where LCE is token-averaged cross-entropy over the gene vocabulary (padding ignored, end-of-sequence supervised) and LNLL is the Gaussian negative log-likelihood 12 [log σ2 + ( y − μ)2/σ 2] of the LogNormalize target at gene positions, with log σ2 clamped to [ −10 , 10]. The same teacher-forced loss on the inner validation set is the early-stopping criterion. 

4.6 Training 

All five Omic Translator arms share one training recipe: AdamW [21] with learning rate 3 × 10 −4

and weight decay 0.01; a per-step schedule with linear warm-up over the first 3% of the nominal 100-epoch budget followed by cosine decay; batch size 32 without gradient accumulation; mixed precision with loss scaling; gradient norm clipping at 1.0; dropout 0.1 throughout. Validation loss is evaluated after every epoch on the complete inner validation set, and training stops after five epochs without improvement; the best checkpoint is reloaded before any held-out decoding. Runs stopped after 13–29 epochs. The model seed for fold ( r, d ) is 7000 + 10 r + d and is shared by the five Omic Translator arms and by Cisformer; cuDNN is set to deterministic mode and data-loader workers are seeded. Free-running validation decoding and the two correlation metrics were logged every epoch for the record but were not used for selection. Every run used one NVIDIA L40S (48 GB) under Slurm with a 36-hour limit. Peak reserved GPU memory in a separate synthetic probe at batch 32 and 8,192 tokens was 9.7 GB for the two 17 configurations with the distance prior, 8.9 GB for the two without, and 8.6 GB for full attention; these are probe measurements, not peaks of the training runs. The Omic Translator training timer covers the epoch loop including the teacher-forced validation loss and the free-running validation decode performed every epoch; it excludes data loading set-up and held-out inference. Mean training wall-clock per fold was 3.27 h for the primary configuration (23.5 epochs on average, about 500 s per epoch) and 6.48 h for full attention (16 epochs at about 1,440 s per epoch). Free-running decoding of a 53,506-cell test half took about 2,500 s for Perceiver arms and 9,400 s for full attention. 

4.7 Inference 

Decoding is support-free: the target RNA is dropped from the batch before the model is called. The encoder is run once per cell and the decoder generates greedily from the beginning-of-sequence token, taking the arg-max gene at each step with no sampling, beam search, temperature or repetition mask, until every cell in the batch has emitted end-of-sequence or 384 steps have elapsed. The prediction matrix is initialised to zero; for each emitted gene the predicted abundance is max(0 , μ ) at that step, and the first emission of a gene is kept if the same gene is emitted again. The implementation that produced the stored matrices tested whether a gene had been seen by whether its current prediction was zero, so a first emission clipped to zero could be overwritten by a later duplicate; the released code keeps an explicit seen-mask, and the stored decoder streams (tokens, means, log-variances, sequence lengths and end-of-sequence positions) allow the matrices to be rebuilt under the corrected rule without a GPU. Rebuilding all 50 cross-validation test-half matrices this way changed 0 entries in 0 cells; the 384-step cap was reached before end-of-sequence in 22.92% of cells and cells emitted 0.07 duplicate tokens on average. Because the cap was reached in a large fraction of cells, we re-decoded every checkpoint (50 runs) at a predetermined longer cap of 768 steps on both withheld sets and a seeded 5,000-cell subset of each test half. Lengthening the cap changed per-gene correlation by +0.0031 on podocytes (cap reached in 63% of cells at 384 steps, 0% at 768), by +0.0000 on Paneth cells and by +0.0108 on the test subset (mean over runs; largest absolute change on podocytes 0.0072). A determinism check confirmed that decoding the same checkpoint twice at batch 4 gives bitwise-identical predictions and that batch 7 with two workers gives identical token streams and means within 10 −4.

4.8 scButterfly-B baseline 

scButterfly [2] version 0.0.9 was run unmodified in its B configuration, that is, without the cell-type or cluster-based data augmentation of the T and C variants. Because the package expects raw counts, we recovered integer counts from the LogNormalize matrix as round(expm1( x) Ni/10 4) and supplied all 50,000 vocabulary peaks of the binary ATAC matrix in chromosome order, with the per-chromosome peak counts required by its chromosome-split ATAC encoder. Its own preprocessing was then applied to one object containing the training, validation and all held-out cells together: total-count normalisation over the 3,185-gene panel and log(1 + x) for RNA with no highly-variable-gene selection, and binarisation, low-frequency peak filtering ( fpeaks = 0.005), TF-IDF and normalisation for ATAC. The peak filter, inverse document frequencies and normalisation constants are therefore fitted on the pooled object including held-out cells; no held-out RNA target enters any gradient update, but this preprocessing is transductive. Training used the package defaults for architecture and optimisation (dual VAEs with a chromosome-split ATAC encoder, 128-dimensional translator, two discriminators, Adam at 10 −3 for the autoencoders and SGD at 5 × 10 −3 for the discriminators, MSE/BCE reconstruction and KL terms with the package’s loss weights) with batch size 64, 30 18 epochs of RNA and ATAC pre-training, up to 60 translator epochs, and early stopping on its own validation reconstruction loss with patience 5. The package resets all random generators to its internal seed 19193 in its constructor and training call, so the fold seed recorded in the run manifests was not the effective seed for scButterfly; the ten runs differ in their data, not in their initialisation seed. The released wrapper now re-seeds after construction and passes the fold seed explicitly. The job logs show that the integrative stage early-stopped in all ten runs, so the package’s best-validation state was restored in every reported run. The inner validation cells were placed at the head of the package’s test list so they were used only for checkpoint selection and never for gradient updates. Predictions for the test, holdout and withheld cells were taken from the ATAC-to-RNA path, which returns an n × 3,185 matrix in vocabulary order, and written to the shared prediction store. scButterfly’s output lives in its own normalised space, so mean-squared error is not reported for it. The scButterfly timer covers preprocessing and all three training stages and stops before the final decoded validation pass; the job required 256 GB of host memory for the TF-IDF step and the mean training time was 0.87 h per fold on the same L40S. 

4.9 Cisformer, support-free 

Cisformer [7] tokenises accessible peaks and decodes per-gene abundance classes with a transformer decoder. In the published ATAC-to-RNA protocol the decoder is queried only at the genes that are non-zero in the target cell, and the zero class is excluded from the loss. We kept the released architecture (model width 280, four decoder layers with self-attention, cross-attention and feed-forward sub-layers, 7 heads of dimension 140, feed-forward multiplier 4, dropout 0.1; a seven-digit decimal embedding of the peak index in place of an encoder; eight abundance classes, where class 0 is zero and classes 1–7 are septiles of the cell’s own maximum log(1 + count)) and changed the task. The decoder is queried on all 3,185 genes with an all-true decoder mask, which is asserted at inference, and the zero class is supervised with the same class-weighted cross-entropy as the other classes (inverse-frequency weights estimated from the first 200 training batches, zero-class weight doubled). A regression test asserts that no target information reaches the decoder mask. Peaks beyond the 8,192 cap are subsampled deterministically from a seeded hash of the cell and split; in the reported runs that hash used a Cisformer-specific namespace, so above-cap cells (8.4%) were evaluated on a different subset of their peaks than in Omic Translator; the released code uses the shared implementation and a test asserts identical subsets. Training used Adam with AMSGrad at 5 × 10 −4, a step schedule multiplying the rate by 0.6 every four epochs, mixed precision, batch size 8, at most 30 epochs, and early stopping on validation cross-entropy with patience 5 after three warm-up epochs. The checkpoint with the lowest validation loss was retained: the job logs of all ten runs record a checkpoint save at each improvement in validation loss, and the saved checkpoints carry the validation loss as their selection score and the epoch of minimum validation loss (Supplementary Table S4). The point prediction for each gene is the softmax expectation over the eight class indices; the arg-max class is stored as a native side output. Because every gene receives a value, coverage is 1 by construction. The Cisformer timer covers the epoch loop including the per-epoch validation loss and validation decode; the mean training time was 7.46 h per fold. 

4.10 Reference predictors 

Two reference predictors are evaluated on every fold through the same prediction-store and scoring path as the trained models. The training-mean profile is the per-gene mean of the LogNormalize matrix over the fold’s full training half (training plus inner validation cells), broadcast to every 19 test cell; it uses no test information and no chromatin. Its per-gene correlation is zero and its coverage is zero because every column is constant. The constant oracle-support diagnostic writes 1 at every position where the target is non-zero and 0 elsewhere; it uses target support and nothing else and is the appropriate reference for any protocol that conditions on expressed-gene identity. Oracle-support Cisformer results were run for diagnosis only and do not enter any ranking. 

4.11 Metrics 

One implementation scores every model. Ranks are tie-corrected average ranks and Spearman correlation is the Pearson correlation of ranks. A vector is treated as varying if and only if its maximum differs from its minimum, evaluated on the stored values; the original implementation used a standard-deviation threshold that float32 accumulation could exceed for genuinely constant columns, and every reported number was recomputed after the correction. For per-gene correlation, a gene is evaluable if its target varies across the scored cells; the correlation is computed across cells for every evaluable gene whose prediction also varies, and set to 0 for evaluable genes whose prediction is constant. The reported value is the mean over evaluable genes (3,145 of 3,185 on the cross-validation halves, 3,050 on the holdout, 2,048 on podocytes and 1,425 on Paneth cells). 

Coverage is the fraction of evaluable genes whose prediction varies, and the coverage-conditioned 

per-gene correlation is the mean over that subset only. For within-cell correlation, every cell whose target and prediction both vary across the 3,185 genes is scored across all 3,185 genes, emitted or not, and the mean over those cells is reported; all 53,506 cells of a test half are scored, with no subsampling. The mean number of non-zero predicted and target genes per cell are also recorded. Mean-squared error is reported only for models whose output is on the target scale. 

4.12 Prediction stores, provenance and verification 

Every run writes its dense prediction for each evaluation set to an HDF5 store (float32, gzip) with the cell identifiers, study, tissue and annotation of every row, the gene axis, native side outputs, and a manifest recording the command line, a SHA-256 of the source tree that executed, software environment, GPU, Slurm job, model seed, checkpoint SHA-256 and wall-clock times. Stores are written atomically and re-opened for validation before being accepted. The canonical target for each split and evaluation set is written once and verified to be byte-equal to the corresponding rows of the RNA matrix. A separate scoring script re-opens every store, joins it to the canonical target on cell identifier and recomputes all metrics; disagreements with the value computed in-process are recorded with their cause. The 400 stores total 24.5 GB and are checksummed. The cluster copy of the code is an rsync of the development tree without version control, so manifests record a content hash of the executed source rather than a commit. Two source-tree hashes appear across the 70 runs; neither corresponds exactly to a commit, because the tree was edited on the cluster during the campaign. For the Cisformer runs the executed selection rule was verified from the job logs and checkpoint metadata as described above; the Cisformer runner in the released repository had, at the time of the audit, a selection branch that could not have produced those runs, and it has been corrected and tested. The pre-audit cluster source tree is archived alongside the results. 20 4.13 Statistical inference 

For each prespecified contrast and metric, the fold-level difference is metric(A) − metric(B) on the same test half; the repetition-level difference is the mean of the two directions; the test statistic is the mean of the five repetition-level differences. Because the two directions of a repetition train on complementary halves and test on each other’s training data, and the five repetitions overlap randomly, we do not apply a t-test across folds. Inference treats the donor as the independent unit (16 units; Supplementary Table S1). The analysis reported in the tables was revised after the independent audit, which showed that the original procedure, exchanging the two models’ raw prediction rows within randomly chosen studies and recomputing the pooled statistic, depends on each model’s output scale: the pooled across-cell ranks of a hybrid matrix change under a per-model rescaling even though the observed Spearman statistic does not. The primary inference is now an exact sign-flip test on within-donor paired differences. For each contrast, metric and donor, the metric is computed on that donor’s cells alone in every fold, the paired difference A − B is averaged over the two directions and the five repetitions, and the test statistic is the mean of the 16 donor-level differences. Under the null hypothesis that neither pipeline is better, each donor’s difference is equally likely to carry either sign, so the null distribution is obtained by enumerating all 2 16 sign patterns exactly; the two-sided p-value is the fraction of patterns whose statistic is at least as extreme as the observed one, and it has no Monte Carlo floor. The assumptions are that donors are independent, that the sign of each donor’s difference is exchangeable under the null, and that the within-donor difference is the quantity of interest. The estimand differs from the pooled difference of performance scores: between-study rank structure is excluded, so within-donor effects are smaller in magnitude, and the two are reported separately throughout. Donors who contributed several tissues (one contributes colon, adrenal gland and fallopian tube, another muscle and bile duct) are single units whose cells are pooled; this respects their dependence but does not remove the between-study structure within such a unit, so their donor-level differences are averages over tissues. Confidence intervals are percentile intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of the 16 donor-level differences [22]; the released output records the bootstrap bias and leave-one-donor-out estimates for every contrast. As a sensitivity analysis the pooled statistic is also tested with a cluster permutation that exchanges per-gene rank-transformed prediction rows within donors, which is invariant to per-model monotone rescaling and leaves the observed pooled statistic unchanged (1,000 permutations and 1,000 cluster bootstraps per contrast); its results, and the original raw-row analysis kept for reproducibility, are released with the inference outputs. Monte Carlo p-values there are reported as (# {| T ∗| ≥ | T |} + 1) /(n + 1) together with an exact binomial upper bound on the tail probability. Holm’s step-down correction [23] is applied within each family and within each metric, never across. The ablations family contains the four contrasts of Table 1 and was fixed in the analysis plan committed to the repository on 5 August 2026 before any run of the matrix was launched; the model ranking family, scButterfly-B against each of the six transformer configurations, was added on 10 August 2026 after the ablation results were seen, and we call it post hoc throughout. No external registration was filed; the commit history is the record of prespecification. The plan also forbids attributing the primary versus full-attention difference to the encoder, since that pair differs in the spatial prior as well. 21 4.14 Software, development process and reproducibility 

Models were implemented in PyTorch 2.5.1 with CUDA 12.1 [24] under Python 3.9 on the cluster; the exact package versions of the environment that produced the results are recorded in the repository. Split generation, target writing, training, scoring, aggregation, permutation inference and figure generation are separate scripts that communicate only through the split files, prediction stores and their manifests. Every table and every numeric macro in this paper is generated from the aggregated metric table and the inference outputs by one script, and the prose claims that are not themselves generated (sign counts, rank orders, ranges) are pinned by assertions in the same script. The code and analyses were developed with an AI coding agent under the audited vibe coding approach described previously [25]: the agent wrote and ran the pipeline under human direction, every quantitative claim was traced back to a stored artefact, and the complete repository and manuscript were then subjected to an independent pre-submission audit whose findings, with the resulting code changes, regression tests, reanalyses and claim revisions, are recorded item by item in the repository.
