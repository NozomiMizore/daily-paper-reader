## Abstract 

Many high-throughput scientific experiments produce distributional outputs, re-quiring conditional generative models to interpret the data, and to predict outcomes at new inputs. Acquiring experimental data at new conditions (e.g., for a new antigen or a new cell type) is often expensive, motivating the need for automated experimental design methods. However, existing active learning frameworks are limited as they assume scalar or vector-valued outputs. We first show that the Wasserstein test risk of a conditional generative task decomposes into a mean term and a shape term, demonstrating why mean-based active learning is blind to distributional shape. To capture both, we propose the transport Neural Tangent Kernel (tNTK), a tractable kernel that measures how sensitive a generative model’s predicted distribution is with respect to its parameters. The tNTK recovers the standard NTK in the deterministic limit and upper-bounds the posterior Fréchet variance of the prediction under small parameter perturbations. It admits a closed-form expression for affine Gaussian transport and tractable Monte Carlo estimators otherwise. Treating the tNTK as the covariance function of a Gaussian process, we greedily select inputs that minimize the total GP posterior variance across the pool, yielding a distribution-aware active learning strategy. We evaluate this strategy across four classes of conditional generative models, including conditional flow matching and conditional Monge gap, on synthetic distributions, intent-labeled sentences, and single-cell perturbation responses. In these experiments, the tNTK outperforms the baseline NTK computed from the conditional mean, demonstrating the value of distribution-aware active learning for generative-modeling tasks. 

## 1 Introduction 

Decades after a logic-based “robot scientist” first autonomously characterized gene functions in yeast metabolism [1, 2], machine learning has become a standard tool for the automated design of scientific experiments. These approaches have demonstrated impact across a wide range of scientific fields, including inorganic materials synthesis [3], drug discovery [4–7], and single-cell perturbation biology [8–12]. They have also been applied to large-scale image classification [13] and to the alignment of large language models [14, 15]. We consider the setup of pool-based active learning (AL) [16, 17], in which the acquisition strategy iteratively assembles a training set Xtrain from a set of candidate inputs Xpool ⊆ X . For each selected input x ∈ X train , the corresponding output o ∈ O is acquired using the experimental system. The goal of an AL strategy is to assemble a training set with a fixed number of data points so that a surrogate

predictive model f (x) will generalize best to unseen inputs (i.e., a test set) — a problem extensively studied for classification [13, 18–22] ( O = { 1, . . . , K }) and regression [23–26] (single- or multi-task, 

O ⊆ Rd). In many scientific applications, however, the experimental output is a probability distribution: O = P(Y) for some underlying sample space Y ⊆ Rd, observed through a finite set of samples for each input. For example, chemical or genetic perturbation assays measure genomics data for hundreds of cells per perturbation, profiling entire distributions over cell states [8]. Regulatory genomics assays capture hundreds of thousands of hypersensitive DNA sequences in the genome for each cell type [27]. In both cases, the output mean E[y ∣ x] is a poor substitute for the entire output p(y ∣ x) as the distribution itself is of scientific interest. For this reason, existing AL paradigms are not suited to distributional outputs because they have no other option but to reduce the output to a pre-specified scalar functional (e.g., mean or an event probability [28, 29]). Conditional generative models such as conditional optimal transport [30, 31], flow matching [32], and diffusion models [33] are compelling options for predictive modeling of distributions. They demonstrated impressive performance for image and text generation [34, 35], as well as perturbation effect prediction [31, 36–39] and conditional DNA sequence generation [40]. Despite their adoption in these domains, no existing acquisition strategies directly leverage these generative models within an active learning framework for experimental design. To address this gap, we develop a framework for active learning of conditional generative models that operates natively on distributional outputs. Specifically, we introduce the transport Neural Tangent Kernel (tNTK), a tractable kernel that measures the sensitivity of the predicted distribution pθ (y ∣ x)

to perturbations of the model parameters θ. The tNTK admits two complementary interpretations: it upper-bounds the Fréchet posterior variance under a Laplace approximation to the parameter posterior, and it controls the metric derivative of the predicted distribution on the Wasserstein manifold. We characterize the geometric gap and show the bound is tight in dimension one. To propose the first distribution-aware active learning strategy, we treat the tNTK as a Gaussian Process (GP) covariance function, and greedily select inputs that minimize the total posterior variance across the pool, following existing kernel-based active learning frameworks [25, 26]. After introducing the necessary background (Section 2), we decompose the risk of a conditional generative prediction task into a mean and a shape term, and illustrate on a tractable 1D example that mean-based acquisition is suboptimal (Section 3). We then introduce the transport Neural Tangent Kernel (tNTK) as well as our distribution-aware AL strategy (Section 4). Finally, we conduct an empirical evaluation across four classes of conditional generative models — affine Gaussian transport, conditional quantile prediction, conditional flow matching, and conditional Monge gap — on simulated data where ground truth is available, as well as on real-world data from intent-labeled sentences and single-cell perturbation datasets (Section 5). Across 26 out of 30 settings, the tNTK outperforms baselines that operate on the conditional mean alone.

## 4 Transport Kernels for Active Learning of Conditional Distributions 

For most applications of interest, neither the test risk nor the shape term from (6) is tractable. Most fundamentally, the ground-truth distribution μ(x) is unknown at unseen inputs Xpool . Even if it were known, the 2-Wasserstein distance remains intractable for neural transport maps. Those represent significant challenges for distribution-aware active learning. We circumvent these issues by considering the posterior Fréchet variance as a measure of uncertainty in a generative model’s predictions μθ (x). We bound this quantity by linearizing the transport map Tθ (x) around its parameters θ, which yields the transport Neural Tangent Kernel (tNTK). ## 5 Experiments 

We investigate the empirical performance of our active learning strategy based on the tNTK, following Algorithm 1, across four conditional generative modeling tasks. We consider as baseline the selection rule in (18) applied to the NTK computed on a neural network fitting the mean of the distribution (“NTK”) and a random sampling baseline (see Appendix D.2 for more details). The NTK baseline matches BAIT [26] as defined in Holzmüller et al. [25], and is among the best active learning methods for regression tasks in Holzmüller et al. [25]. Unless mentioned otherwise, we use the Monte Carlo estimate of the last layer tNTK for all experiments with finite data (using M = 4, 096 samples). Full experimental details can be found in Appendix D.

## 6 Discussion 

We introduced an active learning framework for conditional distributions based on the transport neural tangent kernel . The tNTK captures the sensitivity of a conditional generative model’s predicted distribution to perturbations of its parameters, and generalizes the NTK to distributional outputs. We derived the tNTK as an upper bound on the posterior Fréchet variance (Theorem 1) and provided a geometric interpretation on the Wasserstein manifold (Appendix B). Across four conditional generative model classes and on simulated and real datasets, total-posterior-variance-minimizing selection with the tNTK as GP covariance function outperforms the same strategy applied to the NTK of the conditional mean, as well as random sampling. Sequential greedy total posterior variance minimization, while a principled choice, may be outper-formed by selection methods explicitly designed for batch diversity [25], especially in large-batch regimes. However, the tNTK can be paired with any kernel-based selection method, so combining it with existing or novel selection strategies offers a natural extension of our framework to large-batch settings. The framework extends naturally to other model classes, including diffusion models: in their probability-flow ODE formulation [65], the model is deterministic and the same Jacobian-integration argument as in CFM applies. Conditional normalizing flows [66] provide direct access to the inverse map and pushforward density, and score-based kernels with theoretical guarantees in the regression setting [67, 19, 25] could be extended to the distributional case as an alternative to the tNTK. In dimensions greater than one, neither the Fréchet variance bound (Theorem 1) nor the metric derivative bound (Lemma 4) is generally tight. While we have characterized the gap and tightness conditions for the metric derivative, our experiments evaluate output dimensions only up to d = 50 .How the tNTK behaves at substantially higher dimensions remains an open question. 10 Acknowledgments and Disclosure of Funding

D.2 Baselines 

The NTK baseline benchmarked throughout Section 5 is the standard finite-width NTK (4) for last-layer parameters, applied to the mean-fitting network that is trained to predict the sample mean. The size of the mean-fitting network is set as identical to the transport map trained. The NTK kernels are paired with the same acquisition rule (18) as tNTK. For the conditional quantile prediction, we have two additional baselines that use NTK of networks that each predict mean and variance, and quartiles. We also apply BALSA EMD from Werner and 31 Schmidt-Thieme [56], which is the only work that takes the distribution into account to the best of our knowledge. BALSA EMD was benchmarked only for conditional quantile prediction, as it is only designed for 1-dimensional distributions. 

D.3 Affine transport of 2D Gaussian
