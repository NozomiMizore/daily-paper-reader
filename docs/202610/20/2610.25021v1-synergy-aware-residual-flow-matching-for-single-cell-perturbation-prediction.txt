# Synergy-Aware Residual Flow Matching for Single-Cell Perturbation Prediction

Abstract —Predicting single-cell responses to genetic and chem-ical perturbations is important for understanding regulatory mechanisms and prioritizing therapeutic interventions. Exist-ing methods have made progress by modeling perturbation-conditioned expression shifts with neural networks, graph priors, foundation-model representations, or distributional generative models. However, combinatorial perturbation prediction remains challenging because much of the response can be explained by additive single-perturbation effects, while the biologically infor-mative signal often lies in a smaller non-additive residual induced by perturbation interactions. Directly generating the full post-perturbation expression profile may therefore allocate substantial capacity to the dominant additive component and underempha-size synergy-specific structure. To address this issue, we propose SynGeneFlow, a synergy-aware flow matching framework that anchors the source distribution at an additive prior and learns a residual flow toward the true perturbed state. SynGeneFlow further incorporates perturbation-conditioned gene interaction modeling, where a static gene-gene mask provides biological structure and a perturbation-dependent deformation bias dy-namically modulates gene-gene coupling. To preserve population-level response structure, we align both endpoint distributions and biological residual distributions with MMD-based regularization. We evaluate SynGeneFlow on genetic and drug perturbation benchmarks under observed-single, held-out, and cross-modality settings. The results demonstrate that residualizing flow matching around additive perturbation effects provides a biologically grounded and robust framework for single-cell perturbation response prediction. 

Index Terms —Single-cell perturbation prediction, flow match-ing, combinatorial perturbation synergy. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Predicting cellular responses to genetic or chemical pertur-bations at single-cell resolution is a central problem in func-tional genomics, drug discovery, and therapeutic design [1], [2]. Accurate in silico perturbation models can help uncover regulatory mechanisms, identify candidate intervention targets, and reduce the cost of large-scale experimental screening [3], [4]. This need becomes particularly urgent for combinatorial perturbations, where the number of possible gene or drug combinations grows rapidly as the perturbation space ex-pands [5], [6]. Since exhaustive profiling of all combinations is experimentally infeasible, a useful model must generalize from observed perturbations to unseen combinations while preserving the biological structure of cellular responses. A key difficulty is that the response to multiple perturbations is not simply the sum of individual effects. Recent benchmarks show that simple mean-based or additive baselines can perform surprisingly well against more complex neural models [7], [8], [4]. This suggests that a large part of the observed response can be approximated by additive components inherited from single perturbations. However, the scientifically important sig-nal often lies in the remaining non-additive residual, which reflects interactions between perturbations and is commonly interpreted as combinatorial synergy. Such synergy is crucial for understanding genetic interactions, drug combinations, and context-dependent regulatory mechanisms [9], [5]. Therefore, a model that only reconstructs the full perturbed expression profile may spend most of its capacity on the dominant additive signal while underrepresenting the smaller but more informative non-additive response. Single-cell perturbation data further make this problem challenging. Because RNA sequencing is destructive, the same cell cannot be observed before and after perturbation, making the data inherently unpaired and preventing direct cell-level supervision. In addition, single-cell expression profiles are sparse, zero-inflated, and affected by technical noise. Dropout can create missing values that do not correspond to true biological absence [10], while batch effects and sequencing-depth variation can distort gene-level dependencies [11]. These issues make it difficult to distinguish true perturbation-induced changes from technical artifacts. Moreover, perturbation ef-fects are nonlinear and context-dependent [12], [13]. A static gene co-expression graph can provide useful biological prior knowledge, but it only describes an average dependency pattern. Under different perturbation contexts, the effective coupling between the same pair of genes may be strengthened, weakened, or redirected. Models that treat genes as indepen-dent features, or rely only on fixed gene-gene priors, may therefore fail to capture the dynamic regulatory rewiring that gives rise to non-additive combinatorial responses. We propose SynGeneFlow , a synergy-aware generative framework for single-cell perturbation prediction based on conditional flow matching. The central idea of SynGeneFlow is to model non-additive synergy explicitly rather than treating it as an implicit by-product of full-profile generation. At the representation level, SynGeneFlow introduces a perturbation-conditioned cell encoder. Each gene is represented as a token, and a gene-gene co-expression mask restricts attention to biologically plausible interactions. Instead of using this mask as a fixed graph, the encoder uses the perturbation embedding to generate a dynamic deformation bias for each allowed gene arXiv:2610.25021v1 [cs.LG] 18 Oct 2026 pair. This bias modulates gene-gene attention under the current perturbation, allowing the same gene network to produce different cell representations across perturbation contexts. In this way, SynGeneFlow preserves the denoising benefit of biological structure while capturing perturbation-dependent gene network rewiring. At the generative level, SynGeneFlow introduces synergy-decomposed flow matching. Rather than transporting noise directly to the full perturbed expression profile, we first construct an additive prior from the control expression and estimated single-perturbation effects. The flow then starts from this additive prediction and learns the residual between the additive prior and the true perturbed expression, which corre-sponds to the non-additive synergy that cannot be explained by independent perturbation effects. To preserve distributional fidelity, we combine the flow matching objective [14] with Maximum Mean Discrepancy [15]. Beyond aligning the fi-nal generated expression distribution, we further align the predicted and true synergy-residual distributions, encouraging the model to capture population-level statistics of the non-additive response. We evaluate SynGeneFlow on the Norman combinatorial genetic perturbation dataset [9] and the Com-boSciPlex drug perturbation dataset [6]. Across additive and holdout generalization settings, SynGeneFlow improves the accuracy and robustness of in silico perturbation prediction, demonstrating the benefit of jointly modeling perturbation-conditioned gene rewiring and synergy-residual distributions. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Foundation Models for Single-Cell Biology. 

Large-scale pretraining has become an increasingly im-portant direction for learning transferable representations of single-cell transcriptomes. Instead of training task-specific models from scratch, foundation-style models aim to learn reusable cell and gene embeddings from large collections of expression profiles. Representative examples include Gene-former, scBERT, scGPT, UCE, and related transformer-based single-cell models [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]. These models typically use masked prediction, tokenized gene representa-tions, or large-scale self-supervised objectives to capture broad transcriptomic structure. As a result, they have shown promise in tasks such as cell-type annotation, representation learning, transfer learning, and biological network prediction. Despite their broad applicability, the role of foundation models in perturbation-response prediction remains less set-tled. Most of these models are pretrained on unperturbed or weakly conditioned expression profiles, where the learning objective mainly encourages the recovery of general tran-scriptomic patterns rather than intervention-specific changes. This mismatch becomes important when the downstream task requires predicting how a cell actively moves from one state to another after a genetic or chemical perturbation. Recent evaluations suggest that foundation models do not consistently outperform simpler baselines in post-perturbation prediction, especially in zero-shot or distribution-shifted settings [7], [8], [21]. Broader efforts toward virtual cells and context-aware perturbation models further highlight that transferable representations alone are insufficient unless the model also captures the causal or functional effect of interventions [22], [23]. These observations motivate methods that go beyond generic cell embeddings and explicitly model perturbation-conditioned response dynamics. 

B. Models Tailored to Perturbation Effects. 

A second line of work directly targets perturbation-response prediction by incorporating perturbation identities, biological priors, uncertainty modeling, or generative transition mecha-nisms. CPA models perturbation composition by embedding drugs, genes, dosages, and cellular contexts in a shared latent space, enabling extrapolation to unseen combinations through learned condition embeddings [2]. GEARS instead injects biological structure into the architecture by using gene-gene prior knowledge, including co-expression and Gene Ontology relationships, to improve generalization to unseen genetic perturbations [5]. PerturbNet and GPerturb further explore probabilistic formulations for predicting responses under un-seen chemical or genetic conditions, aiming to better quan-tify uncertainty and distributional variation [24], [12]. Other works study large-scale chemical or combinatorial perturbation profiling, providing important datasets and benchmarks for evaluating whether models can generalize beyond observed interventions [25], [6], [3]. More recently, generative models have been used to de-scribe perturbation response as a transition between cellular distributions. Methods such as scDiffusion, CellFlow, and UNLASTING formulate this problem using diffusion, flow matching, or bridge-based generative modeling, allowing the model to learn continuous transformations from control states to perturbed states [1], [26], [14], [27], [28]. These approaches move beyond deterministic mean prediction and are better aligned with the unpaired and distributional nature of single-cell perturbation data. At the same time, recent studies have emphasized that evaluation should not only reward mean recovery, since perturbations can change variance, hetero-geneity, and subpopulation structure [29], [30], [4], [31]. In combinatorial settings, an additional difficulty is that many methods compose perturbations implicitly or approximately additively. Such a design can explain the dominant response but may underemphasize the smaller non-additive component produced by interactions between perturbations. SynGeneFlow is designed around this gap: it dynamically modulates gene-gene interactions according to the perturbation context and uses an additive prior only as a starting point, forcing the flow model to focus on the synergy residual rather than reconstructing the entire response from scratch. We evaluate SynGeneFlow under three perturbation predic-tion regimes. The first tests whether the model can predict unseen double perturbations when the corresponding single perturbations are observed during training. The second evalu-ates a harder holdout setting, where selected single perturba-tions and all combinations involving them are excluded from training. The third tests transfer from genetic perturbations to drug-induced transcriptional responses. Across these settings, we examine whether additive-prior residual flow matching improves reconstruction accuracy and perturbation-response recovery while preserving population-level structure. 

A. Evaluation Design 

We use two single-cell perturbation benchmarks. The Nor-man dataset contains CRISPR-based gene overexpression pro-files in K562 cells, including both single and double per-turbations [9]. We evaluate two splits on Norman. In the additive split, test double perturbations are composed of single perturbations observed during training, which tests whether a model can recover non-additive interaction effects beyond simple composition. In the holdout split, selected single per-turbations and all combinations involving them are removed from training, creating a more difficult extrapolation setting. We further evaluate on ComboSciPlex to test whether the same framework transfers from genetic perturbations to drug perturbations [25], [2]. All methods are evaluated under the same preprocessing pipeline, gene set, and train/validation/test splits. For Norman, we follow the same additive and holdout protocols used by the direct flow-matching baseline, and for ComboSciPlex we follow the held-out drug-combination evaluation setting. We compare SynGeneFlow with statistical baselines, task-specific perturbation models, foundation-model baselines, and generative transition baselines. These include the con-trol response, additive composition, CPA [2], GEARS [5], CellFlow [26], STATE [23], Geneformer [16], scGPT [18], and scDFM. The comparison with scDFM is particularly important because it separates the effect of the proposed additive-prior residual formulation from the general benefit of using flow matching. Performance is evaluated using reconstruction metrics, including L2, MSE, and MAE; perturbation-response metrics, including Pearson ∆, Pearson ˆ∆, Pearson ˆ∆20 , and DE-Spearman ρ; and the Discrimination Score (DS), which measures whether predicted perturbation populations remain separable [22]. 

B. Observed-Single Combinatorial Prediction 

We first evaluate the Norman additive split, where the single perturbations composing each test double perturbation are already observed during training. This setting focuses on whether a model can recover non-additive residual effects beyond independent single-perturbation composition. This confirms that a large part of the combi-natorial response can be explained by first-order composition. Among neural baselines, CellFlow gives strong reconstruction performance, while GEARS achieves the strongest non-flow baseline DE-Spearman ρ. scDFM further improves recon-struction, DE ranking, and distributional separation, indicating that direct flow matching is already a strong baseline for combinatorial perturbation prediction. D. Non-additive Synergy Prediction 

To directly evaluate whether SynGeneFlow captures interaction-induced effects rather than only improving full-profile reconstruction, we further assess non-additive synergy prediction on Norman double perturbations. For each dou-ble perturbation, we decompose the transcriptional response into an additive component estimated from single-perturbation effects and a residual synergy component that captures non-additive interactions. F. Ablation Study 

We perform ablation experiments on the Norman holdout double-perturbation split to examine the main components of SynGeneFlow. This setting stresses both unseen pertur-bation extrapolation and combinatorial response modeling. Each variant removes or simplifies one component, covering residual flow design, additive-effect supervision, distribution alignment, perturbation-conditioned gene interaction, and ve-locity backbone design. As shown in Figure 3, the full model achieves the best MAE, DE-Spearman ρ, Pearson ∆, and Pearson ˆ∆20 . Removing the additive prior increases MAE from 0.0224 to 0.0236 and reduces Pearson ˆ∆20 from 0.8794 to 0.8653, showing that direct full-profile transport is weaker than additive-prior resid-ual transport. The additive-prior-only variant further degrades DE-Spearman ρ and Pearson-based metrics, indicating that additive composition captures the dominant response but can-not replace residual velocity modeling. Removing Lsingle also hurts performance, suggesting that single-effect supervision constrains the additive operator and prevents it from absorbing higher-order interactions. Distribution and gene-interaction ablations show similar trends. Removing endpoint MMD or synergy-residual MMD reduces response recovery, with synergy-residual MMD caus-ing a larger drop in DE-Spearman ρ and Pearson ˆ∆20 .This suggests that residual alignment preserves perturbation-sensitive structure beyond endpoint fidelity. Removing ∆pij or the gene mask weakens performance, supporting perturbation-aware gene rewiring within a constrained biological interaction space. Replacing differential attention with standard attention also reduces the main response metrics. Although the variant without the additive prior obtains the highest DS, the full model gives the best overall balance between reconstruction accuracy, perturbation-response recovery, and population-level fidelity. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented SynGeneFlow , a flow matching framework that anchors generation at an additive prior to focus on non-additive perturbation effects. By combining residual flow matching with perturbation-conditioned gene interaction mod-eling and residual distribution alignment, SynGeneFlow targets interaction-driven responses. Experiments across observed-single, held-out, and drug perturbation settings show that this formulation improves reconstruction and response-direction recovery while preserving distributional fidelity.
