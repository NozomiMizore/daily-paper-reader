ABSTRACT 

Gene perturbation models aim to predict transcriptional responses to unseen ge-netic interventions, but comparisons rely on heterogeneous evaluation metrics and differentially-expressed (DE) gene subsets. We investigate the consequences of these choices using eight methods across six single-gene perturbation datasets under a unified evaluation protocol. Varying the number of top DE genes re-veals substantial instability in Precision-based rankings, with the winning method changing 86 .7% of the time, while rankings based on the rest of the metrics re-main comparatively stable. Investigating this discrepancy exposes that repeat-ing a single predicted expression profile across cell-level rows, a standard prac-tice for many methods, can inflate significance estimates and produce excessively large predicted significant-DE sets. Specifically we found that 67 .1% of tested perturbation–gene pairs pass the significance threshold despite some of them hav-ing minuscule effects. We derive an inflation factor showing that replicated predic-tions can amplify the standardized Mann–Whitney statistic even after correction for tied ranks, without adding independent information. This artifact changes Pre-cision mainly as the number of DE genes increases because for smaller cutoffs, most of the genes are already significant due to the effect-based ordering. Tak-ing this pitfall into account in our benchmark comparison, we find that a random forest using Gene Ontology features achieves the best overall results. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large-scale single-cell perturbation screens provide a promising means of studying gene function and identifying potential therapeutic targets. However, experimentally measuring every possible genetic intervention is prohibitively expensive. Gene perturbation models (GPMs) aim to address this limitation by predicting the transcriptional response to previously unseen interventions, typically represented as the change in gene expression relative to non-targeting controls. Recent methods span linear predictors, biologically informed models, graph neural networks, and pretrained foundation models, yet there is still limited agreement on which modeling paradigms perform best. A major obstacle to reaching consensus is the absence of a common evaluation protocol. Most studies quantify numerical reconstruction, but apply the same families of metrics to substantially different gene set sizes. Error-based metrics such as MSE, MAE, and RMSE are computed on the top-20 observed differentially expressed (DE) genes (Roohani et al., 2024; Wenteler et al., 2025), over complete profiles (Roohani et al., 2025; Adduri et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Miladinovic et al., 2025), over the 1,000 most highly expressed genes (Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025), or over combinations of highly variable and DE genes on different thresholds N (Wenteler et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026). Pearson correlation is similarly reported over all genes (Roohani et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026), over both all genes and the top-20 DE genes (Miladinovic et al., 2025), or just in top DE genes (Csendes et al., 2025). Accurate detection of affected genes or perturbations is equally important, but detection metrics also differ in both definition and gene scope. The 2025 Virtual Cell Challenge evaluates recovery of the complete perturbation-specific significant-DE set through its differential-expression score, while the 1arXiv:2610.26267v1 [stat.AP] 19 Oct 2026 associated Cell-Eval framework includes Precision@ k, Overlap@ k, and AUPRC (Roohani et al., 2025; Adduri et al., 2025). Jaccard similarity, Precision@10, and precision–recall analysis are used in Roohani et al. (2024), true-positive rate and false-discovery proportion in Ahlmann-Eltze et al. (2025), and AUC and false omission rate in Miladinovic et al. (2025). PerturBench combines perturbation-rank metrics with recall of the top-20 DE genes (Wu et al., 2026). Li et al. (2026) combine reconstruction metrics computed over all genes and top-20, top-50, and top-100 subsets with top-50 DE gene overlap and direction accuracy. Finally, Radig et al. (2026) use top-20 DE gene overlap and Wei et al. (2026) combine MSE and PCC-delta with a Common-DE-genes metric, reporting results over all genes and the top-100 DE genes. These divergent protocols render method rankings difficult to discern. In this work, we treat the evaluation protocol itself as the object of study. We compare eight methods and baselines span-ning structured regression, Gene Ontology-derived features, frozen foundation-model representa-tions, graph neural networks, and pretrained perturbation models across six single-gene perturbation datasets. All methods are evaluated using common perturbation-disjoint five-fold splits in unseen perturbation prediction. We systematically vary the number N of evaluated DE genes and we focus on @N versions of MSE and Pearson for accuracy and Overlap and Precision for detection because they are the most representative. We find that Precision-based rankings are particularly sensitive to the number of evaluated DE genes and identify inflated predicted significance as a contributing mechanism. To be specific, ranking based on Precision through different N produces a mean absolute rank displacement of 2.58 posi-tions and changes the top-ranked method in 86 .7% of dataset–fold evaluations. In contrast, MSE, Pearson, and Overlap exhibit mean displacements below one position and winner-change rates no greater than 33 .3% . Exact dataset-level sign-flip tests identify systematic reordering only for Preci-sion after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Investigating this divergence reveals an artifact in DE testing. The evaluation wrapper repeats a single predicted expression profile across multiple rows to match the ground truth expression matrix, creating a predicted group with no within-condition variation. Mann–Whitney U (MWU) testing against control cells can then assign very small p-values to weak predicted shifts. We further derive the replication-induced inflation factor of the standardized Mann–Whitney statistic, showing that correction for tied ranks does not account for the dependence among copied predictions. Under the assumptions of our analysis, 100 copies compared with 100 control cells multiply the standardized statistic by approximately 7.62 , despite leaving the predicted expression unchanged and adding no independent information. In practice we find that across 620 .9 million tested perturbation–gene pairs, 67 .1% pass the nominal FDR < 0.05 threshold, while 8.7% of these significant calls have minuscule predicted effect magnitudes of | log 2 FC | < 0.1. The resulting predicted significant-DE lists are typically much larger than their ground truth counterparts: only approximately 22% 

of perturbations have at least 1000 observed significant genes, and fewer than 1% have at least 

4000 . This asymmetry interacts with the metric definitions. Overlap caps its effective cutoff at the observed significant-DE count, so the predicted p-values do not enlarge it. Precision, however, uses the predicted significant-DE count and can therefore continue evaluating inflated predicted lists. Since genes are ordered by absolute log-fold change, smaller N emphasize the strongest predicted effects which are indeed significant, hence the artifact, despite being there, has a limited impact on Precision. However as N increases, weaker effects are admitted by the inflated significance estimates, as proven by our analysis and experiments. Taking this pitfall into account, we create a benchmark comparison that aggregates results for small cutoffs of N . In this manner, we aim to account for the aforementioned variability between N but reduce the influence of the artifact on Precision. Overall the results agree with the literature that greater architectural complexity does not consistently improve perturbation prediction, but they are not in favor of simple linear models either. A Random Forest with gene ontology features (Csendes et al., 2025) has the best performance overall, followed by the same model with pretrained embed-dings from scFoundation (Liu et al., 2026). This shows that informative biological or pretrained representations can be highly effective when combined with comparatively simple predictors. 22 RELATED WORK 

Known evaluation artifacts in GPMs. Recent studies show that GPM evaluations can be dis-torted by transcriptional shifts shared across perturbations (Vi˜ nas Torn´ e et al., 2025), correlated metrics (Radig et al., 2026), and shared control references (Nicol et al., 2026). Other concerns in-clude the inclusion of the directly perturbed gene (Csendes et al., 2025), sensitivity to perturbation strength (Li et al., 2026), and incentives induced by composite scores (Roohani et al., 2025). Although recent scRNA-seq datasets contain millions of cells, larger cell counts do not necessarily provide more independent biological replicates. Correlated cells from the same biological sam-ple can produce pseudoreplication when treated as independent observations in MWU-based DE testing. Using matched bulk and single-cell datasets, Squair et al. (2021) found that pseudobulk methods preserving biological replicate structure improved agreement with bulk DE results and re-duced false discoveries. In perturbation prediction, Bhattacharya et al. (2026) show that evaluation metrics respond differently to deterministic and stochastic outputs, while Sch¨ afer et al. (2026) em-phasize that cell-count-dependent statistical power complicates comparisons of significance-defined DE gene sets. As datasets and benchmarks proliferate, frameworks such as cell-eval (Adduri et al., 2025) and DE-gene-recovery metrics such as AUPRC (Zhu et al., 2025) make careful justification of significance-based evaluation increasingly important. These concerns motivate us to theoretically and quantitatively examine prediction replication used to match observed cell counts in evaluation and examine its consequences for DE gene selection and Precision-based rankings as N increases. 

GPM benchmarks. Several recent studies have broadened the empirical evaluation of cellu-lar perturbation models. PerturBench (Wu et al., 2026) provides standardized datasets, task def-initions, model implementations, and metrics for genetic and chemical perturbations, including covariate-transfer and combinatorial-prediction settings. Foundation-model-focused evaluations in-clude PertEval-scFM (Wenteler et al., 2025), which examines whether frozen single-cell foundation-model representations improve zero-shot genetic perturbation prediction, and Csendes et al. (2025), who compare scGPT and scFoundation with train-mean and biologically informed classical base-lines across four Perturb-seq datasets. The latter study finds that simple baselines can outperform foundation models and identifies low perturbation-specific variance as a limitation of commonly used benchmark datasets. In a broader biomedical setting, El Kanbi et al. (2026) introduce a transcriptomic foundation-model benchmark comprising 35 tasks across eight immunological and inflammatory diseases, spanning target discovery, preclinical translation, treatment-response pre-diction, and patient stratification; perturbation prediction is one component of this wider drug-development benchmark. Broader comparisons of gene perturbation models have also rapidly ex-panded in scale. Wei et al. (2026) compare 27 methods across 29 datasets under perturbation- and cellular-context generalization settings while Li et al. (2026) conduct a task-spanning comparison of 13 methods on 25 datasets using 24 metrics covering expression accuracy, differential-expression recovery, and distributional similarity. Radig et al. (2026) evaluate the transfer of non-genetic per-turbation responses across cell types and individuals rather than unseen genetic interventions.

4.1.1 PRECISION -BASED RANKINGS CHANGE WITH N

Figure 1a shows that the evaluated gene-set size can alter the ordering of competing methods. Rank-ings based on numerical accuracy are comparatively stable after the smallest values of N , whereas Precision exhibits frequent rank crossings across datasets. Overlap presents an intermediate pattern, with substantial changes in some datasets but greater stability than Precision. To understand why Precision becomes unstable while the rest remain comparatively robust, we will examine its definition and how the predicted significant-DE lists evolve with N . As defined in Sec. 3.1, Precision uses KP = min( N, |P |), where P is the list of predicted significant genes. This changes based on the model, in contrast to Overlap, where it is determined by the observed significant-DE list and is therefore shared by all models for the same perturbation. In our evaluation, the real significant genes |R| ≥ N account for 35% of the overall cases, whereas the predicted significant genes |P | ≥ N are approximately 95% . The real significant gene set size |R| starts at 

70% for N = 50 and drops to 21% for N = 1000 and 6% for N = 4000 while the predicted |P | go from 97% to 95% and 94% respectively. Consequently, in our experiments, while Overlap saturates due to the finite size of the observed DE set, Precision continues to evaluate longer prefixes because the predicted significant set is typically much larger than the observed one. This excess of predicted significant genes does not necessarily imply that the predicted effects are larger. In fact, the results indicate that predicted significance can be inflated by an evaluation artifact. To be specific, of the 620 .9 million tested perturbation–gene pairs, 416 .5 million ( 67 .1% ) passed the predicted F DR < 0.05 threshold. The pattern was consistent across methods, and even the AVG Ctrl baseline, which contains no perturbation-specific information, classified 69 .2% of pairs as significant. However, 36 .3 million significant calls ( 8.7% ) had a minuscule effect, i.e., | log 2 FC | <

0.1, including some effects arbitrarily close to zero. These results show that predicted significance is not determined by effect magnitude alone. We hypothesize that replication of perturbation-level predictions contributes to this excess signifi-cance. During evaluation, each predicted expression profile is repeated to match the observed cell count, adding no independent information while increasing the nominal sample size supplied to the MWU test. More-over, Precision and Overlap are identical whenever both the observed and predicted significant-DE 8lists contain at least N genes, a condition that is more readily satisfied at smaller cutoffs. As N

increases beyond the observed significant-DE count, Precision can continue evaluating longer pre-dicted prefixes while Overlap stops expanding, exposing the asymmetry between the two lists, as indicated also by the percentages in Sec. 4.1.2. 4.2 BENCHMARK 

For the benchmark comparison, we restrict the evaluation to N ∈ { 50 , 100 , 200 } as a pragmatic choice based on the observed ranking stability. This restriction does not correct the underlying significance estimates but limits evaluation to the strongest retained effects and hence reduces the influence of the artifact on Precision. We aggregate scores separately for each method and metric in three stages. First, within each dataset, we compute a mean score for each cutoff by averaging over the folds in which that cutoff was evaluated. Missing scores are excluded, rather than replaced by scores from smaller cutoffs. Second, we average these cutoff-specific means with equal weight to obtain one dataset-level score, excluding cutoffs with no available results. Thus, cutoffs evaluated in more folds do not receive greater weight in the dataset-level average. Finally, we average the dataset-level scores across datasets with available results, giving each dataset equal weight. Overall the benchmark shows that greater architectural complexity does not consistently translate into better performance. RF-GO achieves the highest Pearson, Precision, and Overlap, while RF-scf obtains the lowest MSE and ranks second on the remaining metrics (Table 3). Notably, AVG Train ranks third in MSE, Pearson, and Overlap, demonstrating that even a simple averaging baseline re-mains competitive as indicated in the literature. These findings agree with part of the literature on simpler methods (Csendes et al., 2025; Vi˜ nas Torn´ e et al., 2025); however, they disagree that simple regression models such as Bilinear are highly competitive (Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025). We hypoth-esize that this is because that work’s conclusions rely partly on top-1000 expressed, not DE, genes, which is a very specific evaluation not used extensively in the literature. Finally, more complex models such as GEARS and LPM also underperformed compared to RF. Results disaggregated by dataset and cutoff are provided in App. C. 5 CONCLUSION 

We benchmarked eight methods and baselines across six single-gene perturbation datasets in order to discern the role of different cutoffs for DE genes in the results of current methods. We found that Precision changes significantly as N increases and we identified an evaluation artifact that contributes to this instability. Repeating predictions across cell-level rows to match the ground truth expression matrix can inflate predicted DE significance, which leads to admitting weak effects into the predicted significant-DE lists. Since Precision determines its effective cutoff from the predicted rather than observed significant-DE count, this effect increasingly influences evaluation as N grows. Restricting the benchmark comparison to smaller cutoffs does not alleviate the effect, but since the majority of genes at these cutoffs are significant, it diminishes it. To this end, we take an aggregate of the results for N = {50 , 100 , 200 }. Under this regime, random forests using gene ontology features or scFoundation representations achieve the strongest results, while a training-mean baseline remains competitive. 9Future work will develop and validate evaluation procedures suited to perturbation-level point pre-dictions, rather than treating duplicated profiles as independent cellular observations. For small numbers of independent cell-level predictions, particularly for deterministic models, the exact MWU test should be used, so as to avoid artificially replicating predictions to increase the nominal sample size. Finally, we plan to extend the benchmark to other advanced methods (Cui et al., 2024; Adduri et al., 2025; Wenkel et al., 2026), and add finetuning versions of the large models.

A.1 DATA PROCESSING 

For each dataset we load the provided AnnData object and harmonize it to a common schema. Gene identifiers are mapped to gene symbols and a target gene field is added to every cell, recording either the perturbed gene or the control label non-targeting ; control-cell labels from heterogeneous source naming conventions are unified to this single label. Duplicated gene names are removed, and any perturbation whose target gene is not itself among the measured/profiled genes is dropped, since such perturbations cannot be scored against a matching expression readout. For the Adamson dataset specifically, the provided expression matrix is supplied in natural-log1p-normalized form rather than raw UMI counts; we invert this transform ( exp( x) − 1) to recover linear-normalized expression values, consistent with the scale used by the other five datasets, while preserving matrix sparsity. Non-targeting control cells are separated from perturbed cells and held out as a dedicated control set, used both for baseline predictors and as a reference profile during evaluation. An overview of the dataset characteristics and comparison can be seen in Fig. 2. A.2 METHODS

GEARS (Roohani et al., 2024): The neural network predicts an expression change and adds it to an input control cell’s expression. At inference, it uses sampled control cells and averages their predicted post-perturbation profiles to obtain one expression profile per perturbation. 

Ridge/RF-scf (Liu et al., 2026): Contextual scFoundation embeddings describe the perturbed gene. A ridge regression or a random forest model learns the difference between mean perturbed expres-sion and mean control expression. Average train (Vi˜ nas Torn´ e et al., 2025): Every perturbation receives the mean expression pro-file across all perturbed training cells (not batch based). The prediction is identical for every test perturbation. Before evaluation, the wrappers clip negative predicted expression to zero and apply log1p. Methods producing one profile per perturbation repeat that profile across the required prediction rows. For methods evaluation we use 5-fold cross-validation performed at the perturbation level rather than the cell level: unique target genes are randomly partitioned into five folds, so that all cells belonging to a given perturbation fall entirely within either the training or the test split. This tests each model’s ability to generalize to genes it has not seen perturbed during training, rather than merely interpolat-ing between cells of an already-observed perturbation. Depending on configuration, non-targeting control cells are either excluded from both splits and reserved solely for control-based baselines 13 and evaluation, or retained in the training split only, never appearing in the test set. All splits use a fixed random seed for reproducibility. Beyond this common pipeline, a subset of models require additional, model-specific dataset adaptations applied at run time (e.g., GEARS and LPM expect the control label ctrl rather than non-targeting , among other format requirements); these adaptations are not part of the shared preprocessing described above.
