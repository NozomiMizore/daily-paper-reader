Abstract
Comprehensively characterizing cellular diversity is critical for understanding
tissue development, identifying disease-associated populations and investigating
gene programmes linked to specialized functions. Advances in single-cell profiling
reveal additional cell-specific transcripts and molecular associations, yet many
foundation models represent only a restricted portion of this increasingly rich
molecular information. Here we present CellNet, which enables panoramic mod-
elling of single-cell transcriptomes by integrating DNA-derived gene identities
and measured RNA expression in a shared image representation. Pretrained on
231 million cell profiles spanning 40 species, CellNet improves rare-population
and condition-associated state recognition in evaluated cohorts while retaining
competitive general cell representations. It predicts withheld coding-gene expres-
sion and selected non-coding targets in total-RNA data, and recovers lineage-
specific expression patterns in spatial measurements. Prediction also extends
across species, including to targets without expression-pretraining exposure. Its
gene representations retrieve known non-coding modules and developmental or
neural programmes, including from sparsely detected RNA queries. Together,
these results establish CellNet as a scalable framework for interpreting cellular
diversity through increasingly comprehensive transcriptomic measurements.

1 Introduction
Understanding the diversity of cellular identities and states remains a fundamental
challenge in biology [1–4]. Resolving this diversity helps illuminate tissue develop-
ment, identify cell populations associated with disease and investigate gene-regulatory
programmes linked to specialized functions [5–7]. Single-cell transcriptomic atlases
have made these questions approachable across tissues and species [8–10]. However,
the biological information that can be extracted from these atlases depends on which
molecules are measured. Conventional poly(A)-based profiling underrepresents many
RNA classes, potentially obscuring cell-specific molecular signals and relationships.
Recent total-RNA technologies, including VASA-seq and TotalX, have revealed addi-
tional cell-type markers, developmental expression patterns and coordinated modules
spanning coding and non-coding RNAs [9, 11]. These advances create an opportunity
to study cellular diversity using a more complete view of the genes expressed within
each cell.
Foundation models have begun to translate large transcriptomic collections into
transferable representations, supporting cell identification, integration and molecular
analysis [12]. Geneformer [13] uses large-scale pretraining for network-biology pre-
diction, while scGPT [14] applies generative pretraining to single-cell multi-omics.
UCE [15] and TranscriptFormer [16] extend cellular representations across tissues
and species. However, even as sequencing assays capture additional transcripts, the
molecular information accessible to models remains shaped by gene identity and
input design. Learned gene identifiers depend on predefined vocabularies, while
protein-derived identities do not directly describe genes without protein products [13–
16]. Gene-token architectures further make broader coverage more computationally
demanding; UCE, for example, samples 1,024 expressed genes per cell [15]. Thus, even
when an assay measures additional RNAs, those molecules may remain unavailable
for model-based expression prediction or gene-relationship analysis.
A representation that keeps pace with these measurements must accommodate
diverse gene identities without making cellular context prohibitively expensive to
model. Genomic sequence supplies an identity for both coding and non-coding genes,
independent of a protein product [17–20]. Image-based organization offers a comple-
mentary way to bring many genes into a structured cellular representation [21, 22]. At
the same time, the sparsity and noise of single-cell measurements motivate learning
strategies that capture stable cellular information without solely discarding gene-
level expression variation [23, 24]. Together, these principles provide a route towards
modelling an expanded transcriptomic space at both cellular and molecular levels.
Here we present CellNet, which enables panoramic modelling of single-cell tran-
scriptomes by bringing DNA-derived gene identities and cellular expression into a
common image representation. Sequence-derived gene features define species-specific
layouts for gene expression, while multiview latent learning promotes consistent
representations under noisy readouts. Gene-conditioned expression prediction is pro-
gressively introduced to retain gene-level information. Alongside the pretrained model,
we publicly release a collection of 231 million transcriptomic images spanning 40
species, establishing a large-scale, reusable resource for image-based single-cell mod-
elling. CellNet improves identification of rare cell populations and condition-associated

2 Results
2.1 Panoramic transcriptome modelling with DNA-informed
images
To move beyond the restricted gene coverage of conventional gene-token repre-
sentations, we developed CellNet, which organizes single-cell transcriptomes into
DNA-informed images (Fig. 1a,b). Gene identities were derived from Evo 2 nucleotide
sequence representations, providing a common basis for modelling protein-coding and
non-coding genes[17]. For each gene, nucleotide features were pooled into a 1,920-
dimensional vector and reduced to 256 dimensions by principal-component projection.
These features were further used to construct species-specific two-dimensional gene
layouts through dimensionality reduction and balanced spatial assignment[21]. Each
gene occupied a distinct pixel on a 384×384 canvas, with positions fixed across cells of
the same species. Expression measurements were mapped to these positions alongside
assay-specific masks distinguishing measured zeros from unmeasured genes.
Each transcriptome image was divided into non-overlapping patches of 16 × 16
pixels, yielding up to 576 patch tokens (Fig. 1b). Within each patch, aggregated
DNA-derived gene identities were combined with local expression information before
entering the DINOv3 backbone [25]. This image-based organization allows many genes
to contribute to a shared cellular context without allocating a separate global token to
every gene. The encoder learns a cell-level representation while retaining contextual
features that can be queried for individual gene predictions.
To learn from sparse and noisy single-cell measurements, we adopted multiview
latent-space learning rather than relying exclusively on direct expression reconstruc-
tion (Fig. 1c). Two lightly masked global views and six more extensively masked
local views were generated from each cell. Their latent representations were encour-
aged to agree, while Gaussian distribution regularization prevented representation
collapse[26]. An auxiliary species-balanced prototype objective incorporated coarse
cell-type information to support cross-species alignment.
Latent consistency alone, however, does not ensure that individual gene-expression
patterns are retained. We therefore coupled representation learning with a gene-
conditioned expression decoder (Fig. 1c). Using a target gene’s DNA-derived identity
as a query, the decoder attends to the cell representation and visible patch context
to predict expression detection and magnitude. Expression prediction was activated
after a brief warm-up and gradually increased in weight. Together, latent learning
and gene-conditioned decoding enable CellNet to learn consistent cellular represen-
tations while preserving the molecular information needed to predict individual gene
expression.
We pretrained CellNet on approximately 231 million cell profiles spanning 40
species, encompassing more than one million gene identities across the training cor-
pus. Alongside the pretrained model, we publicly released the corresponding collection
4


of 231 million transcriptomic images spanning these 40 species, providing a reusable
resource for image-based single-cell modelling. The main pretraining run used eight
NVIDIA A800 GPUs for approximately two weeks. By comparison, UCE was trained
using 24 A100 GPUs for 40 days[15], while scLong reported 35 GPU days on a cluster
of 96 A100 GPUs[27]. TranscriptFormer reported training on a CZI computing cluster
equipped with 1,000 H100 GPUs[16]. By combining broad molecular coverage with
eﬀicient image-based encoding and complementary pretraining objectives, CellNet
provides a practical approach to panoramic transcriptome modelling at scale, sup-
porting cellular representation, gene-expression prediction and molecular association
analysis within a shared framework (Fig. 1d).
2.2 Cellular representations across tissues and species
A broader view of the transcriptome is most useful when molecular information
can be interpreted in the context of distinct cellular identities. We therefore eval-
uated whether CellNet retained these identities across tissues and donors. Using
549,018 annotated cells from 18 tissues in Tabula Sapiens v2[28], we compared seven
pipelines based on frozen foundation-model representations under donor-held-out
evaluation[13–16, 27, 29]. CellNet achieved a tissue-equally weighted Macro-F1 of
0.621, exceeding the strongest comparison pipeline, TranscriptFormer (0.589; Fig. 2a).
Improvements were observed across multiple organs, including bone marrow, liver and
subcutaneous adipose tissue (Supplementary Fig. S.1a).
Reliable cellular representations must also preserve biological differences across
experiments while accommodating donor-associated variation. We assessed this
balance across 26 multi-donor tissues comprising 682,741 cells using the scIB
framework[30]. CellNet achieved the highest biological-conservation score (AvgBIO =
0.684, compared with 0.680 for UCE), while maintaining a batch-mixing score of 0.886
(Fig. 2c). Cell-type organization was also preserved across the 13 tissues not used for
configuration selection (Supplementary Fig. S.1b,c).
We further examined whether CellNet could represent cellular identities across
species with different gene repertoires. Evaluations covered immune, germline and
plant references, including within-species identification and directional cross-species
label transfer[10, 15, 16] (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Fig. S.2). Within-species recogni-
tion was particularly strong across seven immune species, with a mean Macro-F1 of
0.871 versus 0.809 for TranscriptFormer. Cross-species transfer showed task-dependent
performance: CellNet led the evaluated plant settings, whereas UCE and Transcript-
Former performed better in several animal tasks. Together, these results establish
that CellNet retains useful cellular identity information across diverse datasets and
organisms while extending the molecular scope available for modelling.

and lung atlases[31, 32], comprising 107,344 and 69,650 cells, respectively. Under

We next examined transcriptional differences within shared cellular identi-
ties using CD4 T-cell polarization[33] and WI-38 fibroblast senescence-induction
experiments[34]. In donor-held-out CD4 T cells, CellNet distinguished Th0, Th2 and
Th17 conditions with a Macro-F1 of 0.936, compared with 0.911 for TranscriptFormer
(Fig. 2f,g). Complete-library holdouts in WI-38 cells yielded a Macro-F1 of 0.919 ver-
sus 0.891 across three induction conditions (Fig. 2h). These advantages were observed
across individual conditions and biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. S.5a–c).

sion differences across CD4 donors, illustrating a molecular feature of these states
(Supplementary Fig. S.5d).
Together, these results show that CellNet preserves both broad cellular identities
and finer distinctions among rare populations and condition-associated states, pro-
viding a cellular representation for the subsequent analysis of gene expression and
molecular relationships.
2.4 Expression recovery across coding and non-coding genes
Panoramic transcriptome modelling aims to capture molecular information beyond the
genes observed in any individual experiment. By learning how gene identities relate to
cellular context, CellNet offers a means to infer the expression of genes absent from its
input. Such predictions could extend the molecular information accessible from exist-
ing single-cell datasets, enabling researchers to investigate transcripts beyond their
original measurement panels. We evaluated this capability against directly measured
expression by withholding target genes from TotalX profiles of human peripheral blood
mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and developing brain [11], using 5,000 query cells from
each dataset.
We first examined protein-coding gene recovery under fivefold gene-wise with-
holding (Fig. 3a,b). Among targets detected in at least 20% of cells, frozen CellNet
achieved mean gene-wise Pearson correlations (PCCs) of 0.365 in PBMCs and 0.241
in developing brain, exceeding all four evaluated pretrained comparators. By com-
parison, scPRINT-2 [29] achieved PCCs of 0.198 and 0.094, respectively (Fig. 3a).
Predicted immune-marker profiles also reproduced measured expression differences
among lymphoid, myeloid and platelet populations (Fig. 3b). Detection-stratified
analyses and additional lineage markers further characterized prediction performance
across expression prevalence (Supplementary Fig. S.6).

We next asked whether this predictive capacity extended beyond protein-coding
genes. With all non-coding targets withheld simultaneously, CellNet inferred their
expression using only the observed coding transcriptome as cellular context (Fig. 3c).
For non-coding targets detected in at least 20% of cells, mean all-cell PCCs reached
0.285 in PBMCs and 0.265 in developing brain. Among cells with detected target
expression, conditional intensity PCCs reached 0.716 and 0.577, respectively, exceed-
ing scGPT, scLong and CellFM [35] on the corresponding targets (Fig. 3d). Recovered
expression profiles retained population-associated patterns for selected non-coding
transcripts, including LINC00861, LINC00926 and NEAT1 in PBMCs, and MEG3,
MIAT and SOX2-OT in developing brain (Fig. 3e). Beyond the shared accuracy bench-
marks, DNA-derived identities enabled predictions for 18,502 PBMC and 26,113 brain
non-coding RNA targets, substantially extending the molecular scope of the decoder
(Supplementary Fig. S.7).
We further investigated whether limited reference measurements could refine these
predictions. In a separate reference-adaptation evaluation using fixed sets of 108
PBMC and 323 brain non-coding targets, adapting the native decoder with 500 ref-
erence cells increased mean all-cell PCC from 0.190 to 0.255 and from 0.174 to
0.206, respectively (Fig. 3f). Conditional intensity prediction also exceeded matched-
reference Ridge regression and nearest-neighbour baselines, with PCCs of 0.669 and
0.642 (Fig. 3g). Increasing reference size yielded further improvements (Supplementary
Fig. S.8). These results demonstrate that CellNet can use observed cellular expres-
sion to predict coding and non-coding targets, with limited reference data providing
a means to further refine gene-level inference.
2.5 Extending expression inference across species and assays
The genes available for molecular analysis vary across organisms [36], while experi-
mental technologies measure different portions of the transcriptome [37]. We therefore
investigated whether CellNet could extend gene-level prediction across species, includ-
ing genes absent from expression pretraining, and recover expression patterns under
distinct measurement conditions.
Across six nonhuman species from the spermatogenesis atlas [38], frozen CellNet
achieved measured-positive expression-intensity PCCs of 0.672–0.767, compared with
0.093–0.179 for scPRINT-2 (Fig. 3h). Adaptation with 500 within-species reference
cells yielded conditional intensity PCCs of 0.681–0.772. We further evaluated genes
without expression-pretraining exposure in bonobo, mouse and chicken, obtaining
frozen conditional intensity PCCs of 0.696, 0.718 and 0.671, respectively (Fig. 3i).
These targets retained DNA-derived identities despite lacking expression-pretraining
exposure, allowing CellNet to generate predictions beyond the gene targets used in its
expression training. Complete-expression correlations, target coverage and exposure-
stratified results were evaluated separately (Supplementary Fig. S.9).
Targeted spatial transcriptomics offers an additional test of expression predic-
tion [39]. Because these assays use limited gene panels [40], accurate inference of genes
of interest is particularly valuable [41]. We examined whether CellNet could recover
such molecular information using a Xenium mouse-brain section [42], jointly with-
holding 20 predefined genes from 12,000 cells while retaining the remaining measured
9


genes as input. Without spatial coordinates, frozen CellNet reproduced characteris-
tic spatial expression patterns for Slc17a7, Gad1, Aqp4 and Mog, corresponding to
major neuronal and glial populations (Fig. 3j). Across all 20 targets, mean all-cell
PCC reached 0.478 and increased to 0.665 following same-section reference adapta-
tion (Supplementary Fig. S.10). Even when the observed panel was reduced to 1,000
genes, adapted predictions retained a mean PCC of 0.606 (Supplementary Fig. S.11a).
Measured-positive comparisons and additional marker maps supported recovery across
panel sizes and neuronal, glial, myeloid and vascular populations (Supplementary
Fig. S.11b,c).
Together, these evaluations demonstrate the potential of CellNet to infer gene
expression from incomplete molecular observations across diverse gene repertoires and
experimental assays. By extending prediction beyond the transcripts supplied as input,
panoramic modelling provides a means to investigate additional molecular features in
existing single-cell datasets.
2.6 Distinct RNA inputs inform coding and non-coding gene
predictions
Predicting expression beyond the observed transcriptome provides additional molec-
ular information, but does not reveal which measured RNAs support individual
predictions. Identifying these dependencies could help connect gene-level predictions
to coordinated expression programmes within cells [7, 43]. We therefore asked whether
CellNet draws on distinct RNA inputs when predicting coding and non-coding targets
from the same cellular context.
We first examined the biological organization of the learned gene representations.
A network constructed from 3,279 genes in 126 monocytes contained eight com-
munities enriched for processes including translation, RNA splicing and chromatin
modification (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. S.12). The network also linked the

2.7 Panoramic gene representations recover relationships
across RNA classes
Cellular specialization involves coordinated molecular programmes that can span
both coding and non-coding RNAs [9, 11]. Some single-cell foundation models rep-
resent genes using protein-sequence-derived identities and therefore lack direct gene
representations for transcripts without protein products [15]. By representing genes
through DNA-derived identities, CellNet brings both RNA classes into a shared, cell-
contextual gene space. We investigated whether this expanded representation could
recover established molecular modules and gene associations.
Using 30 reference RNAs from a published TotalX non-coding coexpression
module[11], CellNet recovered 15 known members among the top 20 of 343 candidates
in naive B cells, compared with four using RNA correlation (Fig. 5a,b). Mean aver-
age precision (AP) across naive B cells, naive T cells and CD14-positive monocytes
was 0.612 versus 0.314 (Fig. 5c). Individual queries also retrieved known partners
across RNA classes. NEAT1 ranked MALAT1 highly across supported cell popula-
tions, whereas HLA-DRA retrieved the antigen-presentation genes CD74, HLA-DPB1
and HLA-DPA1 at ranks 2, 3 and 6 (Fig. 5d,e). Prior studies provide context for
these retrievals: NEAT1 and MALAT1 associate with active chromatin, CD74 inter-
acts with HLA-DR, and HLA-DPA1/DPB1 form the HLA-DP heterodimer [49–51].
In an external PBMC dataset [52], 15 and nine of the fixed top-20 neighbours of
HLA-DRA and CD3D, respectively, were recovered (Fig. 5f).
We next examined how cellular context influenced these relationships. MALAT1
retrieved PF4 in platelets, LYZ in monocytes and AFF3 in naive B cells, whereas
LINC00486 retrieved ITGA2B in platelets (Fig. 5g,h). Prior work reports MALAT1
activity in platelets, AFF3 function in B-cell class switching, and PF4/ITGA2B
expression in megakaryocytes [53–55]. These selected pairs ranked above their posi-
tions under RNA correlation and static gene-identity similarity. All four associations
remained among the top 20 in both cell halves, while shuffling input expression reduced
their similarities (Supplementary Fig. S.20e,f). At the programme level, six host-
response programmes in DENV-exposed Huh7 cells showed greater within-programme
similarity than size-matched random gene sets (Fig. 5i).
Together, these results demonstrate that CellNet’s shared gene representation sup-
ports retrieval of established molecular relationships across RNA classes and cellular
contexts.

3 Discussion
CellNet demonstrates that broader molecular coverage can support a more compre-
hensive analysis of cellular diversity, rather than simply expanding the number of
input genes. By combining DNA-derived gene identities with image-based transcrip-
tome representations, CellNet brings diverse RNA molecules into a shared cellular
context while retaining access to their individual gene representations. The model
improves recognition of rare cell populations and condition-associated states while
maintaining competitive performance in dataset integration and cross-species eval-
uations. These findings show that extending molecular coverage is compatible with
the general-purpose cellular representations needed to study cellular diversity across
tissues and biological conditions.
An important consequence of panoramic transcriptome modelling is the ability to
investigate genes beyond those observed in individual experiments. CellNet predicts
withheld expression from partial transcriptomes, extending from protein-coding genes
to selected non-coding RNAs and targets without expression-pretraining exposure.
Its ability to recover spatially patterned expression further illustrates the potential
to investigate transcripts outside targeted measurement panels. Beyond individual
expression predictions, the learned gene representations recover established molec-
ular modules and retrieve coding-gene programmes using non-coding RNA queries.
Notably, the sparsely detected SERTAD4-AS1 retrieved genes associated with a
14


published neural stem-cell programme. These results illustrate how panoramic mod-
elling can make previously underrepresented RNAs accessible to biological analysis,
providing hypotheses about their expression and associations with cellular functions.
The shared representation also provides opportunities to investigate the molecu-
lar information underlying model predictions. Input-attribution and targeted-removal
analyses identified distinct sets of measured RNAs contributing to predictions of
different coding and non-coding targets. Cell-contextual gene representations fur-
ther recovered known partners and coordinated programmes across diverse cellular
backgrounds.
Computational tractability is essential for extending molecular coverage at scale.
By organizing multiple genes within image patches, CellNet avoids assigning a sepa-
rate global token to every gene. Multiview latent learning captures shared information
across incomplete observations, while gene-conditioned expression prediction retains
gene-level information. This design supported pretraining on 231 million cell pro-
files spanning 40 species using eight NVIDIA A800 GPUs over approximately two
weeks. Prediction accuracy nevertheless varies across molecular classes, particularly
for sparsely detected non-coding transcripts, and inferred associations require inde-
pendent functional validation. As single-cell profiling continues to expand, more
comprehensive RNA measurements and perturbation datasets will offer opportuni-
ties to refine these predictions and investigate their regulatory significance. CellNet
provides a scalable framework for leveraging this growing molecular information
to characterize cellular diversity and explore the gene programmes associated with
specialized functions.
4 Methods
4.1 Overview and pretraining collection
CellNet combines DNA-derived gene identities with image-based expression encod-
ing to learn cellular representations and gene-level predictions within a shared
model. Each transcriptome is represented by an expression image and a measurement
mask. A transformer encodes these inputs into cellular context, which a gene-
conditioned decoder uses to predict specified transcripts and generate contextual gene
representations.
The main pretraining collection comprised 231,190,557 cell profiles from 26,467
datasets spanning 40 species. Genes were indexed using species-aware identifiers, and
the model’s molecular representation contained 1,015,163 genes. Expression process-
ing, sampling and optimization are described in Supplementary Methods S.2.4-S.2.8.
4.2 DNA-informed transcriptome images
We represented gene identity using nucleotide sequence features from Evo 2 [17]. For
gene g, sequence features were averaged over nucleotide positions, yielding a 1,920-
dimensional vector eg. A frozen principal-component transform provided the 256-
dimensional representation ag used for trainable adaptation:

ag = PT(eg −µ).

Here, sg denotes the gene-associated nucleotide sequence, fDNA the pretrained
sequence encoder, and P and µ the fixed projection basis and centring vector.
The sequence features remained frozen during transcriptome pretraining, while
downstream identity projections were learned jointly with the cellular encoder.
A fixed, species-specific layout was derived from the DNA sequence features by
principal-component projection followed by t-SNE. The resulting coordinates were
scaled to the 384×384 canvas and assigned to distinct pixels using balanced greedy k-
d tree assignment [22]. The assignment distributed genes across 16 × 16 patches while
retaining one gene per pixel. For cell c, normalized expression xcg was placed at the
pixel assigned to gene g. A corresponding indicator mcg recorded whether the gene
was measured by the assay. The same layout was used across cells, retaining measured
zero-expression genes and accommodating protein-coding and non-coding identities
through the sequence-based interface.
The image was divided into non-overlapping 16 × 16 patches. Image organization
allowed multiple genes to contribute to each contextual token, providing a bounded
patch grid as molecular coverage increased.

4.7 Cellular and molecular readouts
The pretrained encoder remained frozen for cellular classification, integration and ref-
erence mapping. Molecular association analyses used the frozen gene decoder. Expres-
sion completion compared frozen predictions with separately specified reference-based
decoder adaptation (Supplementary Methods S.2.10). Cellular classification used layer
normalization of the sum of the final eight raw cellular summary states. Shrinkage
linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was the default cellular classification readout for
CellNet, motivated by the isotropic Gaussian regularization used during representa-
tion learning. The six comparison models used logistic regression, a conventional linear
probing approach in single-cell representation evaluation [61]. These heads were fit-
ted using training labels, with feature standardization restricted to the corresponding
training partition. The Gaussian regularizer acted in the pretraining projection space;
downstream LDA estimated class means and a shared covariance from the encoder
features.
Integration used the raw cellular summary from the sixth block counted from the
encoder output. Reference mapping used the eighth block counted from the output and
a shared distance-weighted nearest-neighbour classifier. These readouts and evaluation
partitions are specified in Supplementary Methods S.2.9.
Expression completion queried the gene decoder using context assembled exclu-
sively from observed genes. Withheld targets were absent from encoder expression,
gene identities and measurement masks. Complete reconstruction used the expected-
expression output, pcgMcg, across all query cells. Conditional intensity used Mcg and
was scored separately on measured-positive observations. Total-RNA, multispecies
and spatial protocols are described in Supplementary Methods S.2.10.

S.1.12 Biological organization of gene representations
The network used 126 CD14-positive monocytes with observed RNA available. Its
3,279 query genes were selected before network analysis. The preparation cohort
contained 126 platelets and 300 cells each from naive B, naive T and monocyte pop-
ulations. We combined the 2,048 protein-coding and 512 other annotated genes with
the highest detection rates across these 1,026 cells, all 1,625 supported coding genes
detected in at least seven platelets, and 83 prespecified platelet-reference and query
genes. Removing duplicates and retaining supported, unambiguous gene mappings
yielded the 3,279-gene panel. The 512-gene annotated group was not restricted to
non-coding RNA. The network queries did not restrict the full measured-RNA input.
Gene vectors were averaged across monocytes, centred across the query panel and
normalized to unit length. Connecting each gene to its 15 highest positive-cosine neigh-
bours, retaining a link selected in either direction, produced 36,638 links and eight
weighted Leiden communities. All eight communities showed Reactome enrichment
after global Benjamini–Hochberg correction over 10,720 tests against the 3,279-gene
background (Supplementary Fig. S.12). Representative enrichments included trans-
lation elongation (q = 6.28 × 10−79) and mRNA splicing (q = 2.64 × 10−9). The
HLA-DRA–HLA-DPA1 link joined curated MHC-II pathway members with measured
RNA r = 0.552. Across the network, 3.96% of links had RNA r ≥0.30, compared
with 0.38% of random non-edge partners matched to the source gene. These analy-
ses characterize biological organization of the representations. Prediction attribution
used the separate hidden-target design below.
S.1.13 Target-specific input evidence and removal controls
The prediction analysis used 24 cells from one SSTX PBMC library, six each from
monocyte, T-cell, B-cell and natural killer-cell groups. Within each population, cells
were selected at equally spaced ranks spanning the 55th–90th percentiles of summed
original CST3 and NEAT1 predictions. The entire 7,302-gene target partition, includ-
ing both targets, was withheld from input expression, measurement indicators and
input gene identities; 29,197 measured genes supplied the image. Expression was
log-transformed after normalization to 10,000 observed-gene counts per cell. The
monocyte displayed in Fig. 4c was subsequently selected for measured targets and
the strongest combined two-target removal contrast among the selected monocytes.
The four NEAT1 examples in Supplementary Fig. S.13 required detected targets and
absolute prediction errors no greater than 0.4 log units, and were selected by the
largest removal contrast within each group, excluding the main monocyte. All 24 cells
contributed to the summaries and complete map collections.
Integrated gradients[44] followed a 24-step path from zero to observed expression,
with measurement indicators, gene identities and detection information fixed. Signed
gene contributions were summed within 16 × 16 image patches. In Supplementary
Fig. S.13, full signed gene-contribution vectors were more similar within than between
monocyte, B-cell and natural killer-cell groups; the T-cell contrast was approximately
zero. The complete CST3 and NEAT1 collections use a common scale across all 48
cell/target maps (Supplementary Figs. S.14 and S.15).
40


Expression-only removal set selected input values to zero while retaining mea-
surement information, gene identity and the original normalization. Eight random
sets were averaged within each cell before comparison. Complete hiding addition-
ally removed measurement, identity and detection information. For the 32-gene sets,
target-selected removal exceeded random removal in every cell under complete hiding;
patch-removal contrasts were positive in 23 or 24 of 24 cells, depending on target and
removal protocol (Supplementary Fig. S.17). Summed integrated gradients approxi-
mated the prediction change from the zero-expression reference with a median relative
residual of 1.18%. The maximum residual was 98.15%, with large relative errors in
low-contrast CST3 cases. The figure retains all residuals and expression-matching
diagnostics.
The complete named-set comparison gave extra decreases of 0.0963, −0.0018,
−0.0002 and 0.0293 log units for the antigen-presentation set on CST3 and NEAT1,
and the response-associated set on CST3 and NEAT1, respectively (Supplementary
Fig. S.16). In 2,273 reference monocytes excluding attribution cells, the response-set
expression score correlated negatively with measured NEAT1 (r = −0.138), although
removing that set lowered its prediction relative to random removal. Thus, mea-
sured expression covariation and the model’s use of an input set conveyed different
information in this cohort.
S.1.14 Cell-state evidence across donors and
expression-associated features
For the Th17–Th2 analysis, a linear discriminant classifier was trained on three donors
and applied to the fourth, rotating across four donors. Attribution explained each held-
out cell’s Th17-minus-Th2 log-posterior score. Gradient multiplied by input expression
gave signed contributions; positive values favoured Th17. The 96 attribution cells com-
prised 12 cells per state and donor. Within-state donor-mean gene contributions had
Spearman correlations of 0.717–0.752 (Supplementary Fig. S.18). The rotating clas-
sifiers shared training donors. Two displayed examples were selected retrospectively
from correctly classified cells by the largest existing patch-removal contrasts.
Removing the 10% strongest supporting patches was compared with random
removal of the same patch count. Of six random sets per cell, only sets match-
ing removed expression mass and detected-gene count within 10% were averaged.
This strict matching yielded 38 eligible cells, all Th17, and each showed a larger
score decrease after targeted removal. Ribosomal genes were prominent among the
classifier’s contributing inputs.
Separately, six-component sparse nonnegative matrix factorization was fitted to
cell representations from 2,700 cells sampled across donors D1–D3 and Th0, Th17
and Th2 conditions, then applied to all 9,777 cells. D4 was held out from fitting and
gene ranking. Four selected features were associated with DNA replication, mitosis,
endoplasmic-reticulum stress and interferon programmes (Supplementary Fig. S.19).
The eight genes per feature with the highest within-donor expression correlations in
D1–D3 were drawn from genes detected in at least 488 cells. All 32 selected correlations
remained positive in D4, and the training-defined high/low state polarity persisted
41


across all four donors. Feature activations also covaried with total RNA counts, as
shown for each feature.
S.1.15 Module and partner retrieval across RNA classes
Module recovery used a 1,020-gene query panel whose expression and measurement
indicators were withheld from the input. Thirty reference genes were excluded from the
343 ranked non-coding candidates, which contained 117 additional published members
and 226 candidates outside the member list. Scores averaged candidate-to-reference
model cosine similarities or absolute RNA Pearson correlations. Average precision
sums precision over the complete ranking, weighted by each increase in reference-
member recall. Across naive B, naive T and monocyte populations, it was 0.601, 0.611
and 0.624 for scImagene, compared with 0.275, 0.341 and 0.325 for RNA correlation.
Module membership was defined by the published TotalX coexpression analysis from
the same resource[11].
Other neighbourhood and programme analyses retained measured query RNA.
Population-mean gene vectors were centred across the analysed gene panel and nor-
malized before cosine scoring; query self-matches were excluded. RNA comparators
used absolute Pearson correlation of log-transformed expression in the same cells,
including measured zeros. These rankings measured associations between representa-
tions. TCP1 retrieved CCT4, CCT5, CCT2, CCT6A and CCT3 at ranks 1–5 in both
HEK293T and untreated Huh7, with CCT7 at rank 17 or 9, respectively (Supple-
mentary Fig. S.20a,b). RNA correlation retrieved no CCT partners in either top 20.
In the external PBMC cohort, HLA-DRA’s partners CD74, HLA-DPB1 and HLA-
DPA1 retained ranks 4, 7 and 11. The complete discovery lists include recurrent and
non-recurrent neighbours (Supplementary Fig. S.20c,d).
The four cell-context examples were selected after an exploratory screen, including
comparison with baseline ranks. Each primary population contained 126 cells with
matched protocol composition: eight 10X-dUTSO, 74 SSTX and 44 SSTX-pA+ cells.
All used the same 2,736 coding candidates. Their observed similarities ranged from
0.211 to 0.369, compared with −0.088 to 0.034 after a single within-cell expression-
value shuffle (Supplementary Fig. S.20e). Paired, protocol-stratified cell bootstrap
intervals used 1,000 resamples of the observed and shuffled populations and describe
that cohort and shuffle realization. All four partners remained within the top 20 in
both disjoint 63-cell halves. Support across sequencing protocols varied: MALAT1–
AFF3 ranked 27 in SSTX-pA+, while its half-cell ranks were 17 and 15 and its
expanded-population rank was 15 (Supplementary Fig. S.20f).
S.1.16 Host programme organization and non-coding entry
points
The Huh7 analysis contained 500 untreated and 500 DENV-exposed cells. Host-
expression scores assigned 267 exposed cells to an active-like group and 233 to a
quiescent-like group (Supplementary Fig. S.21a,b). Scores averaged gene-wise stan-
dardized log-expression within predefined signatures; standardization used all 1,000
cells. Five scores defined the exposed-cell annotations, and the stress-granule score
42


supplied additional expression context. Each displayed programme had a higher mean
within-programme cosine than the mean of 200 size-matched random gene sets. The
gene cosine and RNA Pearson scores in Fig. 5i summarize programme organization
within their respective association spaces.
Developmental references came from the published VASA-seq combined-stage
marker table[9]. Coding markers with adjusted P < 0.01 and log fold change greater
than 1 were ranked by the published marker score, retaining up to 60 genes before
measurement-support filtering. The Foxl2os, C130021I20Rik and Gm50321 analyses
contained 60 of 2,149, 60 of 2,152 and 46 of 2,117 reference members/candidates,
respectively. Coding candidates required detection in at least max(5, ⌈0.05N⌉) of the
context’s N cells; non-coding queries required at least five RNA-positive cells. All
cells contributed, including those with zero measured query expression. VASA-seq
contributed to pretraining; these analyses evaluated recovery of known programmes
in the specified developmental contexts.
The complete developmental top-20 lists contained eight, seven and three refer-
ence members, compared with six, four and zero for RNA correlation (Supplementary
Fig. S.21c). Each method used identical cells and fixed full-population candidate and
reference sets in the disjoint half-population comparisons. scImagene retained higher
average precision in all six halves (Supplementary Fig. S.21d).
The human neural analysis used the published neural stem-cell state-12 marker
programme[57]. Coding markers with adjusted P < 0.01 and average log2 fold change
greater than 0.25 were ranked by the published fold change, retaining the top 60
before coverage filtering. The eligible reference contained 59 genes among 2,082
coding candidates. All 3,423 verified non-targeting controls contributed to both meth-
ods’ associations; 13 had measured SERTAD4-AS1. WNT5B, DLK1 and SERPINF1
were recovered at ranks 7, 10 and 17. The original perturbation study supplied the
programme reference; this retrieval analysis used its control cells.

S.2 Supplementary Methods
S.2.1 Datasets
We used a public pretraining collection and fifteen public datasets or dataset col-
lections spanning human tissues, cell lines, vertebrate and invertebrate cell atlases,
plant tissues and spatial transcriptomics. Table S.1 summarizes the datasets and
adopted cohort sizes; their sources, biological material and measurement details are
described below. Species-level composition of the multispecies atlases is provided in
Table S.2. Original donor, sample, cell-type and experimental-condition annotations
were retained. Data partitioning, analysis-specific sampling and evaluation procedures
are described in S.2.9–S.2.13 and S.2.3.
Main pretraining collection. The main collection contained 231,190,557 cell
or nucleus profiles from 26,467 constituent expression-matrix entries, represent-
ing 40 source species designations, 1,015,163 species-qualified gene identities and
2,233 harmonized cell-type categories. Data were obtained from GEO, ArrayExpress,
BioProject-associated repositories and CELLxGENE. The collection included 189
CELLxGENE entries comprising 44,489,429 profiles within these totals. Constituent
entries denote expression matrices rather than independent publications or biologi-
cal replicates. Source quality control and measured-gene annotations were retained;
expression normalization and species-aware gene harmonization are described in
S.2.4–S.2.6.
Tabula Sapiens 2.0. We used the Tabula Sapiens 2.0 human multi-organ atlas,
retaining source tissue, donor and cell-type annotations. The dataset is available from
Figshare. The adopted collections comprised a 692,102-profile matrix and a 549,018-
profile labelled collection from nine donors; nine profiles without valid labels were
excluded from the latter. These numbers describe the supplied subsets used here rather
than the complete atlas. Eligibility of tissues and labelled cells is specified in S.2.9.
Human kidney atlas. The atlas of healthy and injured human kidney was
obtained from the corresponding CELLxGENE collection. The adopted cohort con-
tained 107,344 profiles from 45 donors and 57 author-provided cell labels. Donor
identities and injury annotations were retained. Proximal-tubule subsets were drawn
from this same atlas.
Human lung atlas. We used the Travaglini et al. human lung atlas, available
from its CELLxGENE collection, combining its 10x and Smart-seq2 lung transcrip-
tomes. After excluding blood samples, the adopted cohort comprised 69,650 cells from
three donors with 59 author-provided labels. The original donor identities and cell
annotations were retained.
Cano-Gamez CD4 T-cell dataset. The Cano-Gamez et al. CD4 T-cell dataset
was obtained from BioStudies accession S-BSST2978. We retained the author-defined
Naive starting population in 10x technical batch 1 under Th0, Th2 and Th17 con-
ditions. The resulting collection contained 9,777 cells from four donors and 20,953
measured genes. Condition labels were taken from the experimental metadata.
WI-38 senescence dataset. The WI-38 fibroblast transcriptomes were obtained
from GSE226225. Six 10x libraries, GSM7068355–GSM7068360, represented replica-
tive senescence, irradiation-associated senescence and etoposide-associated senescence,
54


with two libraries per condition. The adopted matrix contained 14,457 cells and 36,601
measured genes after the quality-control criteria described in S.2.9. Library replicates
were derived from the same human cell line.
Circulating immune-cell atlas. Author-annotated circulating immune-cell
matrices were obtained from the seven-species Figshare collection 28008611. Species
comprised human, rhesus macaque, Chinese softshell turtle, Pacific white shrimp,
Ciona intestinalis, half-smooth tongue sole and whitespotted bamboo shark. The
adopted labelled cohort contained 19,727 profiles in total (Table S.2). The human
matrix was supplied as counts per million without raw counts and retained its source
expression convention.
Spermatogenesis atlas. We used the single-nucleus transcriptomes from [38],
The molecular evolution of spermatogenesis across mammals, with data available
through the author portal. The adopted atlas encompassed human, chimpanzee,
bonobo, gorilla, gibbon, rhesus macaque, common marmoset, mouse, opossum, platy-
pus and chicken, with 96,464 labelled nuclei in total (Table S.2). Original cell
annotations were retained. Gibbon is reported using the established common species
designation for the analysed collection.
Brassicaceae multi-tissue atlas. The adopted author-annotated matrices,
deposited under GEO accession GSE297576, contained 86,015 profiles from Arabidop-
sis thaliana, Arabidopsis lyrata, Brassica oleracea and Capsella rubella (Table S.2).
Source tissue and sample annotations were retained, together with the species-specific
cell labels and shared coarse labels supplied with the matrices.
Brassicaceae root atlas. Root transcriptomes were obtained from GSE268881.
The adopted collection contained 184,951 profiles from Arabidopsis thaliana,
Camelina sativa, Eutrema salsugineum, Schrenkiella parvula and Sisymbrium irio,
spanning control and author-defined stress conditions. Source condition, replicate and
cell-type annotations were retained. The root atlas and its accession refer to the same
underlying collection.

S.2.2 Third-party comparison methods
Model releases and task scope. Released pretrained models and their origi-
nal inference procedures supplied frozen cell representations or native expression
predictions. Gene mapping followed each model’s supported identities and species
conventions. Unsupported species or genes had unavailable predictions rather than
zero scores. Geneformer V2, scGPT, scLong, scPRINT-2, TranscriptFormer and UCE
formed the six cellular comparators in Fig. 2. Expression comparisons in Fig. 3 addi-
tionally included CellFM. The model subset varied with the task and native output
support.
Geneformer V2. The V2-104M release [? ] used Ensembl-mapped expression and
the authors’ rank-value tokenization. Its frozen 768-dimensional CLS representation
used the oﬀicial second-to-last-layer extraction setting. Downstream labels trained
only the classifier described in S.2.9.
scGPT. The whole-human release [14] used its supported vocabulary and oﬀi-
cial binning. Cell analysis used 512-dimensional CLS features and maximum sequence
length 1,200. Completion used 51 expression bins and at most 600 nonzero visible-
context genes. Hidden expression was excluded from bin construction. Native predic-
tions were inverse-binned through visible-expression boundaries to log1p counts per
10,000 without query-truth calibration.
scLong. The released model [27] retained the fixed 27,000-gene axis used for these
analyses. Cell features mean-pooled merged gene decodings, excluding the terminal
padding position. Completion retained the frozen native expression-regression head.
Missing expression on the fixed gene axis followed the authors’ input convention;
target measurements were excluded from visible-context normalization and input.
scPRINT-2. The small-v2 release [29] used oﬀicial gene encoding, positional
encoding, expression preprocessing and generative inference. Cell analysis used the
256-dimensional base summary representation, at most 1,200 context genes and the
released expression-dependent sampling procedure. Expression inference used visi-
ble raw counts and explicit gene queries. Human comparisons jointly normalized
generated target counts and observed counts. Current multispecies comparisons nor-
malized predictions to observed-input library size only, with depth multiplier one.
With native intensity µ and zero-inflation logit z, complete output before normal-
ization was µ[1 −sigmoid(z)] and the intensity endpoint used µ. The latter was not
interpreted as the strict mean conditional on a positive observed count.
Gorilla, platypus and legacy chicken identities were added through the oﬀicial
frozen gene-encoding and positional-encoding extension without updating pretrained
weights. Bonobo assay identifiers followed the chimpanzee namespace used by the
58


comparison. Exact gene support fixed multispecies target membership before reading
new prediction scores; identifier conventions did not change the biological species of
the cells.
TranscriptFormer. TF-Metazoa [16] used raw counts, supported protein-
sequence-derived identities and oﬀicial inference. Its 2,048-dimensional cell represen-
tations supplied annotation, integration and reference mapping without task-specific
representation fitting. Species and gene support were retained for each atlas.
UCE. The released 33-layer model [15] used oﬀicial raw-expression preprocessing
and protein-sequence-derived identities. Inference sampled 1,024 gene tokens with
padded length 1,536 and returned 1,280-dimensional cell representations. Additional
cell filtering was disabled to preserve the common cohort; gene eligibility followed
native vocabulary support. Features supplied classification, integration and reference
mapping.
CellFM. Completion used the released 800-million-parameter model [35] and its
original scalar cell-wise expression decoder. Weights were frozen, with no new head or
query-truth recalibration. Human vocabulary support defined eligible targets; human
identities were not substituted for unsupported nonhuman identities. CellFM therefore
entered human completion comparisons without fabricated nonhuman scores.
Classification and mapping. Third-party cell representations used training-fold
standardization and logistic regression with C = 1, L-BFGS, at most 2,000 itera-
tions, no class weighting. scImagene used automatically regularized LDA, as in S.2.9.
Reference mapping used a common cosine kNN classifier for scImagene, scPRINT-2,
TranscriptFormer and UCE, with identical partitions, class-frequency correction and
validation-selected settings.
Reference-supervised expression. Ridge used at most 512 high-variance visi-
ble genes selected from reference cells, reference-fitted standardization and α = 100,
with the target mean as intercept. Cosine kNN used the same feature selection and
ten neighbours. Expression predictions were weighted averages of measured reference
expression, with weights proportional to nonnegative cosine similarity, normalized to
sum to one; zero total similarity gave uniform weights. Predictions were constrained
to nonnegative log-expression values. Reference membership matched the adapted
decoder for each budget. These methods received target-expression supervision from
reference cells and were distinguished from frozen pretrained prediction.
Molecular associations. Expression comparison used absolute Pearson cor-
relation over the same selected cells, candidate genes and measured zeros. Static
identity comparison used cosine similarity between learned 64-dimensional gene-
identity vectors. Published reference sets and full eligible candidate rankings were
shared. These comparisons measured co-expression or context-independent identity
similarity, respectively.
S.2.3 Evaluation metrics and statistical summaries
Classification. Accuracy was the fraction of correctly labelled query cells. For class
k, precision was Pk = TPk/(TPk + FPk) and recall was Rk = TPk/(TPk + FNk).
Class F1 was 2PkRk/(Pk + Rk), with undefined class contributions assigned zero.
Macro-F1 averaged class F1 equally. Tabula Sapiens tissue-conditioned evaluation
59


used biological labels present in each true test fold, first averaging donor folds within
tissue and then weighting eighteen tissues equally. Global all-class scores used their
predefined union of true and predicted labels; seen-class results were reported sepa-
rately. Balanced accuracy averaged recall over true test classes. Experimental-state
classification reported macro-F1 and balanced accuracy averaged equally over held-out
donor/library folds; per-state scores, predicted probabilities and confusion matrices
were retained.
Rare-cell F1 used labels with training prevalence at most 1%, at least twenty cohort
cells across at least three donors, and positive training and test support in the fold.
Eligible labels could differ by fold. Eligible-class F1 was averaged within each fold,
then across the three donor-held-out folds equally rather than pooling predictions.
The separately reported 5% minority threshold did not define the primary rare-cell
endpoint. Cell counts and donor- or library-held-out scores described distinct sampling
and evaluation units.
Reference mapping. Within-species F1 used predefined fine labels; cross-species
F1 used shared coarse labels. Coverage was the fraction of all target-test cells with
a true label present in the source reference. F1 was evaluated on this eligible sub-
set. Empty subsets retained coverage but undefined F1. Within-species family means
weighted species equally; cross-species means weighted eligible ordered directions
equally. Shared cross-species kNN settings were selected by validation macro-F1,
weighting the four comparison methods and five development task families equally. SD
across five overlapping splits described partition sensitivity rather than independent
biological replication.
Integration. Metrics followed scIB definitions [30]. NMI normalized mutual infor-
mation between graph clusters and cell labels by the arithmetic mean of their
entropies. ARI corrected pairwise cluster–label agreement for chance. Cell-label silhou-
ette width was rescaled as (s+1)/2. The equal-weight mean of NMI, ARI and rescaled
cell-type silhouette defined AvgBIO. Batch silhouette used 1 −|sbatch| within labels,
averaged within each label and then across eligible labels. Graph connectivity aver-
aged each label’s fraction of cells in its largest connected neighbourhood component.
Their arithmetic mean defined batch performance. Higher values indicated better con-
servation or mixing. Aggregate scores weighted eligible tissues equally. Metrics used
high-dimensional representations and graphs; UMAP supplied visualization [62].

S.2.5 Sequence features, image mapping and augmentation
Gene-associated DNA sequences were matched to species-aware gene identifiers before
feature extraction. Sequence features were extracted from the 1-billion-parameter
Evo 2 base model, using the output of the third linear projection in the multilayer
perceptron of block 20. Features at valid nucleotide positions were mean-pooled;
long sequences were encoded as overlapping chunks, whose pooled features were
then averaged to obtain one 1,920-dimensional vector per gene. A frozen incre-
mental principal-component transform reduced these vectors to 256 dimensions.
Independent learned projections generated the 64-dimensional mean-identity and
expression-modulated identity features in equation (2).
For image mapping, DNA-derived gene vectors were first reduced to 64 dimen-
sions by PCA and then projected to two dimensions by t-SNE, using cosine distance,
perplexity 30, PCA initialization and an automatic learning rate [63]. The coordi-
nates were scaled to the image grid. Balanced greedy k-d tree assignment placed genes
at nearby available pixels while favouring less occupied patches, producing a fixed,
species-specific mapping with at most one gene per pixel. For each cell, normalized
expression and the assay measurement indicator were written at each gene’s assigned
pixel. Thus, measured zero-expression genes remained distinguishable from genes not
measured by the assay.
The expression branch used two channels, expression and visibility. The expression-
modulated branch used 64 identity channels, with δ = 0.02. Each branch projected
to 768 dimensions. During training, adjacent horizontal positions could be swapped
within a patch. Patches were selected with probability 0.5 and neighbouring pairs
with probability 0.3. Gene identities and their expression values moved together. The
permutations were disabled at inference.
Each cell generated two global views with patch-mask fractions sampled from
[0, 0.1], and six local views with fractions from [0.35, 0.8]. Additional global/local
augmentation parameters are summarized in Table S.3.

S.2.12 Host-cell states and programme coherence
The host-cell analysis used 500 untreated and 500 DENV-exposed Huh7 profiles,
with host RNA providing the expression context. Frozen CLS features supplied cel-
lular representations for UMAP visualization. Programme scores were constructed
by standardizing each measured host gene across the 1,000 selected cells and averag-
ing the relevant signature members; the quiescent-lncRNA score subtracted the mean
down-signature score from the mean up-signature score.
Within the 500 exposed profiles, the five scores for active UPR stress, antiviral
inflammation, quiescent lncRNA, MAPK signalling and chromatin regulation were
standardized across cells and clustered using k-means with two clusters, 10 initializa-
tions. The cluster with the higher active-UPR-stress score was labelled active-like; the
other was labelled quiescent-like, yielding 267 and 233 cells, respectively. Because the
host-only matrix lacked viral-load measurements, these groups approximate the pub-
lished infection states using host expression signatures. Separation was measured in
the original high-dimensional CLS space, with UMAP used for display. This analysis
did not fit the LDA state-recognition head described in S.2.9.
Frozen final interaction features were extracted separately for each cell group.
