Generalizable single-cell perturbation response prediction
using energy-guided flow matching
Jianan Wei1,∗, Jiajun Hong1,3,∗, Guikun Chen1, Ning Yang2, Lifeng Fan3, Wenguan Wang1,†
1Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China
2Peking University Chengdu Academy for Advanced Interdisciplinary Biotechnologies, Chengdu, Sichuan,

Abstract
Predicting phenotypic and transcriptional responses to perturbations at single-cell resolution
provides a powerful tool for probing biological systems. However, existing methods typically
rely on fixed mappings learned during training, making it challenging to calibrate distribution
shifts or adapt to novel perturbation conditions during inference. Here, we present scEGFlow,
an energy-guided flow matching framework that dynamically bridges control and perturbed cel-
lular states. scEGFlow models continuous transitions from control cell populations to perturbed
states using conditional flow matching. It then applies condition-specific energy gradients to cor-
rect and steer these predictions, enabling flexible adjustments without retraining the flow model.
Evaluations across benchmarks spanning imaging phenotypes and transcriptomic profiles show
that scEGFlow outperforms existing methods in reconstructing response distributions under
both seen and unseen perturbation conditions, faithfully preserving cellular manifold geometry
and population heterogeneity. This advantage is notable when adapting to new conditions with
only a few measured cells, consistently improving prediction accuracy. Furthermore, scEGFlow
accurately recapitulates perturbation-induced up- and down-regulation patterns across consen-
sus gene expression signatures, where energy guidance improves the agreement between predicted
and observed regulatory directions. Ultimately, these findings demonstrate that scEGFlow pro-
vides a modular, generalizable, and steerable solution for single-cell perturbation modeling. By
grounding generative flows in learned biological landscapes, this architecture establishes a new
computational paradigm for navigating and manipulating cellular behavior in silico.

Introduction
Single-cell perturbation technology provides an efficient tool to investigate complex and het-
erogeneous systems by profiling cellular responses to genetic, chemical, or environmental pertur-
bations [1, 2]. However, high-throughput perturbation screens face substantial challenges due to
the complexity of biological systems and the vast combinatorial space of possible perturbations, of
which only a small fraction can be experimentally measured [2–4]. To circumvent this combinatorial
sparsity, diverse computational methods have been developed to learn from experimental cellular
profiles collected under distinct perturbation conditions and generalize to unseen perturbations. Ex-
emplified by scGen [5] and CPA [6], latent-variable approaches leverage vector arithmetic in latent

space to predict out-of-distribution cellular responses. Another line of work improves extrapolation
by incorporating biological priors. chemCPA [7] replaces a fixed drug embedding dictionary with a
molecular structure encoder, thereby extending predictions to unseen compounds, while GEARS [8]
exploits prior knowledge of gene-gene relationships to model unseen genetic perturbations. From
a data-driven perspective, foundation models, including scGPT [9] and scFoundation [10], leverage
large-scale pretraining to obtain transferable representations for downstream perturbation predic-
tion. Conditional generators, such as PRNet [1], learn to generate post-perturbation transcriptional
profiles from cellular states and perturbation descriptors. More recently, optimal transport has been
introduced to model perturbation-induced shifts between unpaired cell populations. CellOT [11]
learns a neural optimal-transport map from the control distribution to the perturbed distribution,
whereas CellFlow [12] combines OT-based cell pairing with conditional flow matching. However,
recent benchmarking studies have raised concerns about the robustness of such generalization. They
show that on small-scale datasets, these methods tend to underperform simple baselines (e.g., mean-
based predictors), particularly in unseen experimental settings [13–15].
In this study, we introduce scEGFlow, a flexible and scalable framework that captures single-cell
perturbation effects across diverse, complex, and heterogeneous systems. It combines a conditional
flow-based predictor with decoupled energy-based endpoint refinement, enabling low-cost and effi-
cient extension to novel perturbation scenarios. First, to address the unpaired nature of pre- and
post-perturbation cellular profiles [11, 16, 17], scEGFlow constructs cell-level paired data via op-
timal transport, which provides fine-grained supervision for model training. It then employs flow
matching [18, 19] to model cellular perturbation responses as a continuous dynamical process, by
learning velocity fields that characterize state transitions along pseudo-paired trajectories. Building
on this, our method incorporates multimodal condition inputs spanning cell type, drug identity and
drug dosage to condition the flow model. However, when generalized to perturbation conditions
absent from the training data, such as unseen drug-dose conditions, this OT-guided conditional
flow-matching backbone may produce biased initial predictions, a limitation also observed in exist-
ing methods. To mitigate this bias, scEGFlow introduces energy-guided refinement by augmenting
each Euler update with a correction derived from the gradient of a condition-specific energy func-
tion. This strategy can be naturally extended to multiple conditions through a weighted sum of
energy functions defined for individual independent conditions.
We evaluate scEGFlow across four distinct phenotypic and transcriptomic benchmarks: the
imaging-based 4i dataset [11], the transcriptomic SciPlex3 dataset [3], the combinatorial ComboSci-
Plex dataset [6], and the large-scale LINCS L1000 compendium [20].
Our evaluation follows a
progressively challenging paradigm. We first validate the model’s capacity to reconstruct observed
perturbation effects. On 4i [11] and SciPlex3 [3], scEGFlow achieves low distributional discrepan-
cies while preserving marker distributions, cell-state geometry, and condition-specific heterogeneity.
We then evaluate zero-shot generalization in out-of-distribution (OOD) settings. scEGFlow yields
superior distribution-level fidelity on held-out drug-dose conditions, unseen cellular contexts, and
novel drug combinations. Next, we assess scEGFlow when only a limited number of cells from the
target perturbation condition are available for training. Given only three target-condition cells in
4i, scEGFlow substantially outperforms PRNet [1] and scDFM [21] across all evaluated correlation
and distributional metrics. Finally, we use L1000 to assess whether these statistical gains reflect
biologically meaningful regulation [20]. We show that energy guidance improves directional concor-
dance with L1000 consensus signatures for the most responsive genes, with gains observed in most
evaluated contexts. Across these scenarios, scEGFlow accurately predicts mean responses, models
population heterogeneity, and reproduces biologically meaningful perturbation signatures in diverse
experimental settings.

Experiment
2.1
scEGFlow predicts perturbation responses with energy-guided flow match-
ing
Single-cell perturbation data contain independently sampled control and treated populations
rather than matched pre- and post-perturbation cells.
scEGFlow therefore formulates response
prediction as conditional population transport: given control cells and a perturbation context, it
generates a treated population that recovers both the mean response and cell-to-cell heterogeneity.
scEGFlow first constructs geometry-aware pseudo-pairs between control and treated cells using op-
timal transport. These pairs provide structured supervision for a conditional flow-matching model,
which learns a continuous velocity field from baseline to perturbed states while conditioning on the
perturbation and cellular context. The resulting flow captures the principal response modes and the
global organization of the target population. scEGFlow further calibrates the flow-matching tra-
jectories through energy-guided refinement. At each Euler step, the gradient of a condition-specific
energy function shifts the generated cell state toward response profiles that are more compatible
with the queried perturbation. This formulation extends naturally to perturbations specified by
multiple attributes: guidance for drug identity, dosage, and other independent conditions can be
combined through their respective energy terms. This decouples population transport from endpoint
3


calibration and permits condition-specific refinement without retraining the flow backbone.
We evaluate scEGFlow on four complementary benchmarks: the imaging-based 4i dataset [11],
the large-scale transcriptomic SciPlex3 dataset [3], the combinatorial perturbation dataset Com-
boSciPlex [6], and the LINCS L1000 perturbational transcriptomics resource [20]. The evaluation
first examines the reconstruction of observed perturbation responses on 4i and SciPlex3. It then
assesses generalization to previously unseen drug-dose conditions and cellular contexts in SciPlex3,
as well as unseen drug combinations in ComboSciPlex. We further evaluate response prediction
on 4i when only a small number of cells from each target perturbation are available for training.
Finally, L1000 consensus signatures are used as an external reference to evaluate whether the pre-
dicted responses recover perturbation-induced directions of gene regulation. Across these settings,
scEGFlow remains competitive in mean-response prediction and shows consistently strong distribu-
tional recovery, while also preserving biologically meaningful transcriptional response directions.
2.2
Predicting single-cell perturbation under various and complex scenarios
In single-cell perturbation experiments, cellular responses vary with treatment, cell type, baseline
cellular state, and other factors, resulting in diverse and complex response patterns. To evaluate
whether our method can accurately predict post-perturbation cell populations under such settings,
we conduct experiments on two single-cell perturbation benchmarks, 4i [11] and SciPlex3 [3], which
differ in readout modality and condition space.
We first evaluate our method on the 4i benchmark, which contains imaging-based phenotypic
profiles from a co-culture of two patient-derived melanoma cell lines, M130219 and M130429, under
diverse drug perturbations. Fig. 2a compares the measured and predicted marginal distributions of
three representative markers with distinct distributional characteristics [11]. Although all methods
capture the overall response trends of these features, scDFM and PRNet exhibit noticeable distri-
butional shifts or over-smoothing, while scEGFlow more closely follows the measured distributions,
preserving heterogeneous and accurate population responses. Fig. 2b-e compare scEGFlow with
other methods in terms of MMD2, Sinkhorn divergence, Pearson correlation, and energy distance.
Together with Table 1, these results show that scEGFlow achieves the lowest MMD2 and Sinkhorn
divergence on the 4i [11] benchmark, while maintaining competitive energy distance and Pearson
correlation. We further test whether scEGFlow captures drug-specific responses. Motivated by
recent findings that simple mean-based baselines can be difficult to outperform in single-cell per-
turbation prediction [14], we compare scEGFlow with MeanExp across six drug perturbations from
the 4i [11] dataset. As shown in Fig. 2f, scEGFlow achieves a consistent improvement in energy dis-
tance for all six drugs, demonstrating that it better preserves drug-dependent population variation.
Fig. 2g visualizes the reconstructed 4i populations by embedding predicted and measured cells in a
shared UMAP space and coloring them by pERK intensity. The cell distributions predicted by our
method show qualitative overlap with the measured population in the shared UMAP embedding,
together with a similar spatial organization of low- and high-pERK states across the embedding.
This agreement indicates that scEGFlow preserves both the global arrangement of phenotypic states
and the signaling-associated variation within the treated population.
We then extend the evaluation to SciPlex3 [3], a substantially larger transcriptomic benchmark
spanning multiple cancer cell lines, compounds, and dosage. As shown in Table 1, scEGFlow achieves
the lowest MMD2, energy distance and Sinkhorn divergence while maintaining high Pearson corre-
lation. Thus, the reconstruction advantage observed in the imaging-based 4i [11] setting remains
evident in a higher-dimensional gene-expression space with more diverse cellular and perturbation
contexts. Figure 2h provides a complementary view of the SciPlex3 results by jointly embedding
predicted and measured cells across aggregated conditions.

2.3
Generalizing cellular responses across unseen chemical and cellular contexts
We next assess whether scEGFlow can extrapolate from observed patterns to previously unseen
combinations of experimental conditions, i.e., out-of-distribution (OOD) settings. This capability is
evaluated on SciPlex3 [3] and ComboSciPlex [6]. SciPlex3 exhibits substantial heterogeneity across
cell types and single-drug dose conditions (Fig. 3a,b), whereas ComboSciPlex [6] includes both
monotherapy and combination-drug treatments. Specifically, we consider three held-out settings:
unseen drug-dose conditions, unseen cellular contexts, and unseen drug combinations. Together,
these settings evaluate scEGFlow’s generalization across chemical, cellular, and combinatorial con-
texts.
Unseen drug-dose conditions. We construct the test data by withholding all perturbed cells
from three drug-dose conditions (i.e., ENMD-2076 at 0.1 µM, WP1066 at 0.01 µM, and Mesna
at 0.1 µM) from training on SciPlex3 [3]. scEGFlow is used to predict responses to these unseen
conditions, which are evaluated against the corresponding measured cell populations. As shown
in Fig. 3c and Table 2, scEGFlow achieves the lowest energy distance, Sinkhorn divergence, and
MMD2 in this split, while maintaining a high Pearson correlation, indicating superior population-
level agreement with the measured responses. This result is further supported by the gene-level
distributions in Fig. 3d, where scEGFlow faithfully captures both the expression shifts relative to
the control and the specific distributional shapes of the five most strongly perturbed genes across
the three held-out conditions. Fig. 3e provides a detailed analysis of the held-out Mesna at 0.1 µM
condition by comparing the covariance structures of the measured and predicted populations over
the first 10 principal components. scEGFlow successfully captures the dominant covariance patterns

Adapting to target conditions with limited observations
Although generalization to entirely unseen perturbations is appealing, existing methods, includ-
ing scEGFlow, still fall short of fully supervised performance. A practical and efficient compromise
is to adapt the model using a small number of target-condition observations.
To evaluate this

setting on the 4i [11] benchmark, we select five target drugs (i.e., cisplatin, crizotinib, dabrafenib,

for training, while reserving the remaining cells for evaluation.
As illustrated in Fig. 4d, scEGFlow consistently achieves the best performance across different

The benefit becomes substantially more evident

This improvement suggests that providing scEGFlow with even a

patterns and better recover response heterogeneity.
2.5
Recapitulating directional concordance with L1000 perturbational signa-
tures
In perturbational transcriptomics, cellular responses are conventionally characterized by the
direction of gene-expression changes, where up- and down-regulated genes together form a signed
signature [20, 25, 26]. Building on this concept, we investigate whether scEGFlow can accurately
recover the directionality of these induced responses.
To this end, we evaluate scEGFlow on LINCS L1000 [20], a large-scale transcriptomics dataset
profiling cellular responses to chemical and genetic perturbations. L1000 measurements are pro-
cessed through five data levels, ranging from raw measurements to consensus signatures. Here, we
utilize Levels 3 and 5 to construct our training and evaluation setup. Level 3 provides normal-
ized gene-expression profiles for individual control and compound-treated samples, whereas Level
5 aggregates replicate profiles to represent the consensus perturbation signatures induced by each
perturbation. Consequently, we train scEGFlow on the Level 3 expression profiles and compare its
predicted response directions against the corresponding Level 5 signatures.
Given the scale of the full L1000 compendium [20], we construct a focused subset of compound
perturbations by pairing Level 3 profiles with their corresponding Level 5 signatures. The resulting
dataset contains 24,104 Level 3 expression profiles over 978 landmark genes, spanning 21 cell lines
and 79 compounds. We hold out six drug–dose conditions for OOD evaluation, ultimately yielding
111 evaluation contexts after pairing with the corresponding Level 5 signatures.
For each context, we rank the 978 landmark genes by the magnitude of their Level 5 expression
changes, retaining the top 10% to yield 98 genes with the strongest perturbation effects. We quantify
directional response concordance as the fraction of these top genes for which the predicted outcome
and the corresponding Level 5 signature agree on the direction of regulation.

the direction of gene-expression changes in the evaluated settings and show that energy guidance
improves concordance with L1000 consensus signatures.
3
Conclusion and discussion
In this work, we present scEGFlow to predict single-cell perturbation responses as conditional
transport between unpaired control and treated populations.
scEGFlow combines optimal-
transport-based soft coupling with conditional flow matching to learn population transitions, and
leverages condition-specific energy gradients to refine the generated trajectories. This decoupling of
transport learning from condition-specific calibration is the core design principle of the framework:
the flow model captures the global dynamics of the response distribution, whereas energy guidance
steers predictions toward states that are most compatible with the queried perturbation context.
Moreover, energy terms representing independent attributes can be compositionally combined,
providing a modular mechanism to incorporate drug, dose, and cellular context into the refinement
process.
Evaluation across 4i [11], SciPlex3 [3], ComboSciPlex [6], and LINCS L1000 [20] supports three
main conclusions. First, scEGFlow accurately reconstructs observed perturbation responses across
11


phenotypic and transcriptomic modalities, capturing both mean effects and within-condition het-
erogeneity. Second, its distributional performance remains favorable in the evaluated held-out sce-
narios, encompassing held-out drug–dose conditions, an unseen cellular context, and novel drug
combinations. In data-scarce regimes, scEGFlow readily leverages limited target observations to
refine its predictions. Third, driven by energy guidance, scEGFlow faithfully reproduces the up-
and down-regulation patterns recorded in L1000 consensus signatures, demonstrating that its effec-
tiveness stems from genuine biological concordance. Together, these results show that scEGFlow
successfully bridges statistical distributional alignment with biological fidelity in single-cell response
modeling.
Several limitations define the scope of the present study. First, our method relies on an iterative
optimization procedure, in which each step requires velocity prediction from the flow model and a
derivative operation for the energy function. As a result, our method incurs a higher computational
cost than one-shot approaches. Second, our flow model adopts a simple linear interpolation path
between each source cell and its perturbed counterpart, which simplifies biological transitions that
may otherwise be nonlinear; furthermore, the OT-derived pseudo-pairs depend on feature-space
distances and may not always reflect true cellular trajectories. Third, energy guidance with multiple
conditions assumes that all conditions are independent; when correlations exist between conditions,
this assumption may lead to suboptimal results.
Looking ahead, several important directions could further advance scEGFlow for single-cell
perturbation response prediction. The modular design of scEGFlow offers the potential to reduce
the computational complexity of extending prediction to additional perturbation conditions, which
could open the door to the scalable exploration of larger and more diverse biological datasets.
Building upon this efficiency, future work could focus on extending the framework to more diverse
perturbation types, cellular contexts, and experimental platforms. Furthermore, future work could
explore whether additional energy functions can extend scEGFlow to other perturbation types, such
as gene knockouts [27] and cytokine stimulation [28], without retraining the flow backbone. Thus,
another promising direction is to improve the design of the energy function, for example by using
off-the-shelf pretrained models to encode conditions and provide stronger priors.

4.1
Problem setup
To streamline model training and optimization, we predefine a set of cellular attributes, denoted
as G = {g1, g2, . . . , gG}, consisting of profiled genes as well as cellular and nuclear morphological
measurements. The state of a cell x is characterized by its profile across this set, represented as
a vector x = [cx(g1), . . . , cx(gD)]T ∈RD, where cx(gi) signifies the quantified value of the i-th
attribute. Our task is to predict the perturbed cell state xp from an unperturbed baseline state xu,
conditioned on a specific perturbation and a biological context. Here, the perturbation is represented
by a vector p ∈RMd, where Md is the total number of unique perturbations in the vocabulary; the
element pm specifies the dosage of the m-th perturbation, falling to zero (pm = 0) in its absence.
The biological context is represented by a context embedding c derived from the corresponding
unperturbed source population. In our implementation, c is defined as the mean control-cell state
for each cell line. We denote the complete condition by ξ = (p, c). Formally, we aim to learn a
conditional distributional predictor fθ(xp | xu, ξ) that captures the distribution of perturbed cell
states conditioned on baseline expression, perturbation, and biological context.

4.4
Energy guidance for perturbation response refinement
Predicting cellular perturbations has long suffered from poor generalization, primarily due to
the diverse spectrum of drug types and dosages, as well as the combinatorial complexity of joint
perturbations [7, 31]. Current models [6, 7, 12] typically use continuous vectors with biological or
chemical structural meaning as conditional embeddings, aiming to extrapolate learned patterns to
unseen perturbations. In contrast, we alleviate this issue through a post-processing step termed
energy relaxation, which iteratively adjusts the predicted response to reduce its condition-specific
energy, thereby encouraging greater compatibility with the perturbed distribution [32].
Conditional energy guidance. Here, we define an energy function Eϕ(xt, ξ) that measures
the compatibility between the condition ξ and the intermediate state xt interpolated between xu
and xp, where a lower energy indicates stronger consistency with ξ.

distribution, we assume that the conditional probability p(ξ|xt) is exponentially inversely related
to the energy function Eϕ(xt, ξ) [32, 33]:

Distance function construction. Based on the above analysis, the distance function should

Experimental setup
Evaluation settings. We evaluate models under three settings: 1) In the independent and
identically distributed (IID) setting, each perturbation condition appears in training, with cells
split into 80% training and 20% test cells within each condition. This setting measures interpo-
lation within the observed perturbation space. 2) In the out-of-distribution (OOD) setting, the
perturbation conditions used for testing are absent from the training set. For SciPlex3 [3], we hold
out three drug-dose conditions, and for ComboSciPlex [6], we hold out five drug-combination condi-
tions. This setting tests whether models can generalize to unseen dosage or combination conditions
rather than interpolate among observed conditions. 3) In the few-shot setting, we evaluate a related
but less stringent scenario in which the target perturbations are no longer completely unseen: only
a small number of cells from each target condition is included during training, and prediction is
evaluated on the remaining held-out cells from the same conditions. We use this setting on the
4i [11] dataset for five target perturbations under a leave-one-condition protocol. This setting tests
adaptation when only limited condition-specific supervision is available. See Appendix for more
details.
Baselines.
Our method is benchmarked against leading single-cell perturbation prediction
models, including CellOT [11], biolord [22], CPA [6], PRNet [1], CellFlow [12], and scDFM [21]. We
also include MeanExp as a statistical baseline [31]. In the IID setting, MeanExp predicts the mean
expression profile of training samples from the queried condition. In the OOD setting, it predicts the
mean expression of all perturbed training cells with the same dosage as the target condition. The
official implementations of all baseline methods are used with their recommended hyperparameters
17


and dataset-specific adjustments. For a fair comparison, all models are evaluated using the same
data protocol.
Implementation details.
Our model is implemented in PyTorch [37] and optimized with
AdamW using a batch size of 256 for 4i, SciPlex3, and ComboSciPlex. For 4i, we train the model
for 100,000 iterations with a learning rate of 1 × 10−6, Sinkhorn regularization εOT = 0.2, and
100 Sinkhorn iterations. For SciPlex3 [3], we use 50,000 training iterations, a learning rate of
1 × 10−7, εOT = 0.1, and 100 Sinkhorn iterations. ComboSciPlex [6] uses the same optimization
and mini-batch OT settings as SciPlex3. For LINCS L1000 [20], we use a batch size of 128, train the
conditional flow-matching model for 30,000 iterations with a learning rate of 1×10−7, and train the
energy model for 300 iterations with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4. During inference, energy-guided
refinement is performed for 10 steps with guidance coefficient λ = 0.05.

S1.2
4i preprocessing
The 4i dataset [11] contains single-cell imaging profiles under chemical perturbations. Following
CellOT [11], the preprocessed representation is retained without additional feature filtering, with
each cell characterized by 48 morphological and protein-marker intensity measurements. The dataset
contains 77,743 cells, including 10,995 untreated cells and 66,748 treated cells across 27 chemical
perturbations. Untreated cells serve as the source population, and cells treated with each compound
form the corresponding target population.
For the held-out and few-shot experiments, five drugs are designated as target perturbations:
cisplatin, crizotinib, dabrafenib, dacarbazine, and dasatinib. Treated cells from these con-
ditions are either excluded from training or included in limited numbers, following the evaluation
protocols in Supplementary Section S2.

SciPlex3 preprocessing
SciPlex3 [3] profiles transcriptional responses to chemical perturbations across three cancer cell
lines: A549, K562, and MCF7.
Each cell line defines a distinct cellular context, with vehicle-

identity and dose. Data splits are defined before feature selection. The three drug-dose conditions,
i.e., ENMD-2076 at 0.1 µM, WP1066 at 0.01 µM, and Mesna at 0.1 µM, are entirely withheld
from training for OOD evaluation. Gene-expression counts are normalized and log-transformed.

scEGFlow training, these expression profiles are projected onto 200 principal components. The PCA
transformation is fitted exclusively on the training cells and then applied unchanged to the IID and
OOD test sets. The model predicts perturbed states in this PCA space, and these predictions are
subsequently transformed back to the 2,000-gene expression space for gene-level evaluation.

S1.5
LINCS L1000 preprocessing and external validation
The LINCS L1000 compendium [20] profiles transcriptional responses to perturbations across

S2
Data Splits and Evaluation Protocols
S2.1
IID experimental setup
For both 4i [11] and SciPlex3 [3], we randomly split the cells within each perturbation condition
into a training set (80%) and a held-out test set (20%). Every evaluated perturbation condition is
therefore represented during training, but the individual test cells are excluded from training.
S2.2
OOD experimental setup
For OOD evaluation, treated samples from the target conditions are excluded from training. We
consider three settings: unseen drug-dose conditions, transfer to an unseen cellular context, and
unseen drug combinations.
S2.2.1
Drug-dose conditions
For SciPlex3 [3], we exclude all treated cells from three drug-dose conditions from training:
ENMD-2076 at 0.1 µM, WP1066 at 0.01 µM, and Mesna at 0.1 µM. Vehicle-control cells serve as
the source population, and predictions are evaluated against the held-out treated cells.
24


For LINCS L1000 [20], we hold out six drug-dose conditions and train models on Level 3 ex-
pression profiles excluding these conditions. Predicted response directions are evaluated against the
matched Level 5 consensus signatures.
S2.2.2
Cellular-context transfer
For SciPlex3 [3], we exclude all treated A549 cells from training while retaining treated cells
from K562 and MCF7. Unperturbed A549 cells serve as the source population, and predictions are
evaluated against the held-out treated A549 cells.
S2.2.3
Combinatorial perturbations
For ComboSciPlex [6], we exclude all cells from five drug combinations from training: Dacino-
stat with Danusertib, Givinostat with Cediranib, Panobinostat with Alvespimycin, Panobinostat
with SRT2104, and SRT2104 with Alvespimycin. The remaining single-drug treatments and drug
combinations are retained for training. Predictions are generated from unperturbed source cells and
evaluated against the held-out combination-treated cells.
S2.3
Few-shot adaptation
Few-shot adaptation is evaluated on five target perturbations in the 4i benchmark [11]: cisplatin,
crizotinib, dabrafenib, dacarbazine, and dasatinib.
For each target, we start from a zero-shot
training set that excludes its treated cells and add k ∈{1, 3, 5, 20, 60, 100, 200} measured target
cells.
A separate model is trained for each target and each k, with all remaining target cells
reserved exclusively for evaluation. As k increases, previously selected target cells are retained, and
additional cells are added to the supervision set. For each k, all methods are trained with the same
k target-condition cells and evaluated on all remaining cells from that condition.
S2.4
Subsampling evaluation protocol
Following the subsampling strategy of [11], we evaluate single-cell distributions using equally
sized subsets of control and treated cells to limit computational cost.
For each condition, we
select the largest N ∈{100, 250, 500, 1000, 1500} that does not exceed either the available control-
cell count or the held-out treated-cell count. We randomly sample N control cells to generate N
predicted treated cells and compare them with N randomly sampled held-out treated cells.
To reduce variability due to random subsampling, we repeat this procedure 10 times per condi-
tion and average each metric across repetitions. Overall performance is then computed as the mean
of the condition-level scores.
S3
More Implementation Details
S3.1
Perturbation representation p.
Small-molecule identity is represented by a 1024-bit Morgan fingerprint. For SciPlex3 [3], raw
doses are recorded in nanomolar (nM). The dose feature is computed as log10(d+1), where d denotes
the numeric dose value in nM and d ∈{10, 100, 1000, 10000} for treated cells. It is incorporated
into the drug representation to encode the drug-dose condition. For combination perturbations,
each constituent drug-dose component is encoded separately, and the resulting representations are
combined into a joint perturbation representation.
25


S3.2
Cellular-context representation c.
When cellular-context conditioning is used, each context is represented by the mean unperturbed
cellular state computed from the corresponding training source cells. This mean state is encoded
and combined with the perturbation representation to condition the velocity network.
S3.3
Energy model construction
An energy model assigns a scalar compatibility score to a cellular state under a queried per-
turbation condition, with lower energy indicating stronger agreement between the cellular state
and the condition. Here, we describe the architecture of the energy models used in the reported
experiments.
Given the cellular state x, biological context c, and perturbation vector p (from which the
molecular fingerprint m is derived via drug identity), each input is encoded into a latent space.
These representations are then fused to yield a term-specific energy:
Dϕ(x, ξ) = hE(hx(x), hm(m), hp(p), hc(c)),
where hx, hm, hp, and hc map the cellular state, molecular fingerprint, perturbation vector, and
biological context into their respective hidden representations, and hE denotes the network that
maps these representations to a scalar energy value. In the reported experiments, we instantiate
this architecture as drug and dose energy models, Ddrug
ϕ
and Ddose
ϕ
, trained with drug- and dose-
negative samples, respectively.

Condition-wise OOD effects of energy guidance
We first examine the effect of energy guidance at the level of individual held-out conditions rather
than only through aggregate performance. For SciPlex3 [3], we consider the three OOD drug-dose
conditions ENMD-2076 at 0.1 µM, WP1066 at 0.01 µM, and Mesna at 0.1 µM. The unguided
model uses the same trained flow backbone while disabling the energy-guided correction during
inference. The guided model additionally applies the final energy model with the paper-default
guidance strength.
As shown in Table S4, energy guidance consistently improves population-level agreement across
all three held-out drug-dose conditions. Averaged across conditions, energy distance decreases from
0.2810 to 0.2438, Sinkhorn divergence from 0.1615 to 0.1391, and MMD2 from 0.0045 to 0.0039,
while Pearson correlation increases from 0.9932 to 0.9945. These results indicate that the energy-
based correction provides condition-specific refinement beyond the predictions generated by the flow
backbone alone.
We further examine the effect of energy guidance on the five held-out ComboSciPlex [6] drug
combinations defined in Supplementary Section S2.2.
As shown in Table S5, energy guidance
consistently reduces energy distance and Sinkhorn divergence across all five combinations, while
improving MMD2 and Pearson correlation in four of the five conditions. The only exception is
Givinostat + Cediranib, for which a slight degradation in MMD2 and Pearson correlation is observed.
Overall, energy guidance reduces the average energy distance from 29.9767 to 24.8578 and
Sinkhorn divergence from 111.6462 to 93.0152, while improving MMD2 from 0.0618 to 0.0527 and
Pearson correlation from 0.9597 to 0.9667. These results demonstrate that energy-guided refinement
provides consistent improvements in the recovery of responses to unseen combinatorial perturba-
tions.

S4.2
Sensitivity to energy-guidance strength
We investigate the sensitivity of scEGFlow to the inference-time guidance coefficient λ by sweep-
ing across λ∈{0, 0.0125, 0.025, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20} while keeping all other settings fixed. Here, setting
λ = 0 disables the energy correction and reduces the framework to the conditional flow-matching
model.
As shown in Table S6 for SciPlex3 [3], scaling the guidance coefficient from zero up to the
default λ = 0.05 steadily optimizes all four evaluation metrics, achieving the lowest values for
energy distance (0.2438), Sinkhorn divergence (0.1391), and MMD2 (0.0039), together with the
highest Pearson correlation (0.9945). Although performance moderately degrades when λ is further
increased to 0.10 and 0.20, all four metrics remain better than those of the conditional flow-matching
model.
28


A similar trend is observed on ComboSciPlex [6], with λ = 0.05 yielding the best performance
across all four metrics among the tested coefficients (Table S7). Together, the results on SciPlex3 [3]
and ComboSciPlex [6] show that the gains from energy guidance are robust across a reasonable
parametric neighborhood rather than contingent on brittle hyperparameter tuning.
