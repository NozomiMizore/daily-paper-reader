## Abstract 

Predicting cellular responses to genetic perturbations is a central capability for vir-tual cells and a key step toward computational modeling of biological interventions. Most existing models directly map an unperturbed molecular profile and perturba-tion to the resulting observation without explicitly representing the latent cellular transition induced by the intervention. We introduce a mechanism-centric virtual cell world model that represents cellular state as a set of Latent Mechanism Units (LMUs) and treats genetic perturbations as actions on these latent states. Each LMU combines a reusable identity grounded in multimodal biological evidence with an observation-specific state, allowing a perturbation to induce mechanism-specific stochastic transitions before decoding the resulting transcriptional response. We train VCLMU through two-stage pretraining, first on around 200K pseudo-bulk perturbation profiles and then on gene-aligned single-cell perturbation data. Across six perturbation-disjoint benchmarks, VCLMU consistently improves perturbation-specific response recovery over strong baselines while maintaining competitive global response accuracy. We further analyze learned LMUs through enrichment between perturbation responses and LMU gene sets and show that they capture structured biological response programs. These results support mechanism-level latent state transition as a useful formulation for virtual cell models that aim to predict and interpret cellular responses to biological interventions. 

## 1 Introduction 

Predicting how cells respond to genetic perturbations is a foundational capability for virtual cell 

models and an important step toward computational evaluation of biological interventions [6]. High-throughput single-cell perturbation screens [1, 10, 22, 25], now consolidated into harmonized re-sources [24], provide increasingly large datasets for learning intervention-response relationships. A rapid progression of models has followed, including scGen [20], CPA [21], and GEARS [26]; pretrained transcriptome models [7, 15, 30]; and large-scale virtual cell models trained across diverse perturbations and cellular contexts [2, 34], alongside standardized benchmarks [27]. Most existing perturbation predictors remain primarily observation-centric : they map an unperturbed profile and intervention to the resulting transcriptional response while leaving the latent cellular transition induced by that intervention implicit. Scaling this map has so far yielded limited returns. A recent systematic evaluation finds that deep perturbation predictors still do not outperform simple linear or condition-agnostic baselines [3], suggesting that improved modeling of intervention-induced state changes may be as important as additional model capacity. xci is encoded and routed to a sparse active LMU set with control mechanism states Hci , while the perturbation action ai is encoded as ψi. Each active LMU undergoes an action-conditioned stochastic transition to produce predicted perturbed mechanism states, which are decoded into the response distribution p(∆ xi | xci , a i) and the predicted profile bxpi = xci + bμi. During training, the observed perturbed profile is encoded through the same active LMUs to provide transition targets and support inverse prediction of the perturbation, without entering the forward prediction path. CPA [21] and SAMS-VAE [5] introduce structured latent effects, but a perturbation still enters primarily as an additive or conditioning term rather than as an action that transforms cellular state. We instead adopt a single-step world-model formulation that encodes an observation into latent state, transitions that state under a perturbation, and decodes the resulting response [11–14]. Because concurrent cellular programs can respond differently to the same intervention, we factorize state into mechanism-specific units, following the general principle of object-centric world models [8, 9, 19, 33]. We therefore introduce VCLMU, which represents a cell as a sparse set of Latent Mechanism Units (LMUs) selected from a bank of K units by a control-conditioned top-Ka router (Fig. 1). Each LMU separates a reusable identity from an observation-specific state: the identity represents which mechanism the unit captures and combines a learnable query with a set-encoder summary of a periodically refreshed candidate gene set grounded in multimodal gene evidence, while the state reflects the current activity of that mechanism from the expression of the same genes. Perturbations are encoded from the target gene and its regulatory neighborhood and applied independently to each active LMU, allowing heterogeneous responses across mechanisms. State transitions are residual and stochastic, with each unit drawing an action-conditioned Gaussian latent variable to represent variability not determined by the control state and perturbation. The transitioned states are then decoded into the perturbed expression profile. We explicitly supervise these latent transitions rather than only the final reconstruction: the observed perturbed profile is encoded through the same control-selected LMUs, and predicted state changes are aligned with the observed changes using stop-gradient targets, while the target branch is constrained to reconstruct the observed profile. The forward dynamics depend only on the control state and perturbation, preventing information leakage from the observed outcome. Our contributions are (i) a formulation of perturbation response as action-conditioned latent-state transition in addition to direct observation regression; (ii) LMUs, a factorized cellular state whose units carry separate stochastic dynamics under a shared action; and (iii) supervision of the latent transition itself, with stop-gradient targets and observation grounding that keep the learned target from collapsing, together with a two-stage curriculum that pretrains on pseudo-bulk perturbation profiles and continues on gene-aligned single-cell data. ## 2 Method 

Problem formulation. Let xci , xpi ∈ RG denote a control reference and an associated perturbed expression profile, and let ai index the perturbed gene. We predict the response ∆xi = xpi − xci

from (xci , a i). Each gene g carries a multimodal evidence embedding eg ∈ Rd built from functional annotation, pathway, interaction network, protein sequence, and tissue-expression resources, together with a gene-regulatory-network (GRN) neighborhood [16]. These features provide biological evidence for gene representation, while the response model itself is learned end-to-end. Details of the evidence representations are provided in Appendix C. Rather than fitting (xc, a ) 7 → ∆x directly, we factor the map through a latent state in the manner of a world model [11, 13]: encode the control cell into a 3structured latent state, transition that state under the perturbation, and decode the transitioned state into the transcriptional response. 

Cellular state as a set of Latent Mechanism Units. A cell is represented by a set of K Latent Mechanism Units (LMUs), of which only a sparse subset Ai of size Ka is active per cell, in the spirit of slot-based object-centric encoders [19] and sparsely-gated expert routing [29]. Each LMU factorizes into an identity and a state, which differ in scope. The identity ℓk is shared across all cells and grounded in biological evidence: LMU k owns a small, periodically refreshed set of candidate genes Ck, and its identity blends a free learnable query qk with a permutation-invariant summary 

SetEnc [18] of that set’s evidence embeddings, 

ℓk = LayerNorm( αqk + (1 − α) SetEnc {eg : g ∈ C k}) , (1) where α ∈ (0 , 1) is learned. The free query allows each LMU to adapt to programs supported by the data, while the evidence term anchors its identity to biologically informed gene representations, yield-ing mechanism-level representations that are both biologically coherent and interpretable (Fig. 2d,e). Perturbation as an action on mechanism states. A perturbation is encoded once, into an action embedding ψi = fact (ai, {eg }, GRN) that summarizes the target gene together with its GRN neighborhood, and is then applied separately to each active LMU. Because every unit receives the same action but occupies a different state, one perturbation can strongly move some mechanisms and leave others untouched, a behavior that is difficult to represent with a single global latent state. Transitions are residual and stochastic, with a per-unit latent zik drawn from a learned conditional distribution q(zik | hcik , ψi) and regularized toward the prior p(zik ) = N (0, I) through a KL penalty [17], allowing the transition to represent variability not determined by the control state and the action: bhpik = hcik + fdyn (hcik , ψi, zik ) , zik ∼ q(zik | hcik , ψi) . (3) The residual form means the model predicts a change to each mechanism rather than re-deriving the cell from scratch. Critically, the dynamics see only the control state and the action, so the observed outcome cannot leak into the forward path. We pretrain on perturbation-disjoint pseudo-bulk profiles and then continue on gene-aligned single-cell data, with dynamics alignment and target-state grounding disabled during Stage 2 (Appendix A). 

## 3 Results 

Setup. We evaluate on held-out single-gene perturbations under perturbation-disjoint downstream splits on widely used genetic perturbation datasets, so no perturbation in the downstream fine-tuning set appears at test time (see more details in Appendix D). VCLMU-FT denotes the Stage-2 pretrained model further fine-tuned on the downstream training split of each evaluation dataset. We report Jaccard@20 together with Pearson correlation on the full perturbation response ( ∆PCC) and the top responsive genes ( ∆PCC@20). 

Held-out perturbation prediction. VCLMU-FT attains the best Jaccard@20 on all six datasets (Fig. 2a). The margin is small on Adamson [1] and Norman [22], where the condition-agnostic Perturbed Mean is already competitive, consistent with reports that simple baselines remain hard to beat [3]. The gains are largest on Replogle K562 and RPE1 [25] and Tian CRISPRi [31], where baselines recover few true response genes. Using the Stage 2 pretrained checkpoint for downstream fine-tuning improves Jaccard@20 on five of six datasets, with the largest gains on Adamson, Norman, and Tian CRISPRa, while preserving performance on Replogle RPE1 (Fig. 2b). This indicates that pseudo-bulk pretraining captures transferable perturbation structure that subsequent single-cell pretraining further sharpens. VCLMU also occupies the upper-right region of the ∆PCC/ ∆PCC@20 plane (Fig. 2c): accuracy on responsive genes does not come at the cost of the global response. 

LMU candidate gene sets are associated with distinct perturbation-response programs. To assess the biological coherence of learned LMUs, we analyzed enrichment between perturbation-specific response genes and LMU candidate gene sets. Among 1,101 held-out perturbations with at least ten observations, after excluding ubiquitous response genes and subtracting the shared response, 86.6% are significantly associated with at least one LMU (BH-FDR < 0.05 ) [4]. In total, 56 distinct LMUs appear as the best match, with the most frequent accounting for only 20.9% of perturbations. The median best-LMU overlap is seven genes, corresponding to a 19.9-fold enrichment over the random-overlap expectation. Fig. 2(d,e) shows representative LMU associations whose overlapping response genes form biologically coherent programs spanning interferon response, mitotic regulation, T-cell signaling and activation, oxidative phosphorylation, and RNA processing. LMUs are also largely non-redundant: across 50,000 randomly sampled pairs, the median candidate-gene Jaccard is 

0.0079 , with 47.9% sharing no genes and 80.1% having Jaccard < 0.05 .

## 4 Conclusion 

We introduced a mechanism-centric virtual cell world model that represents perturbation response as action-conditioned transitions over Latent Mechanism Units. Across held-out perturbations, VCLMU achieves strong response-gene recovery while maintaining global prediction accuracy, and the learned LMUs capture distinct and biologically coherent response programs. These results support mechanism-level latent state transition as a useful and interpretable formulation for virtual cell modeling. The current study focuses primarily on single-gene transcriptomic perturbations, and future work will extend LMU validation to multimodal and combinatorial perturbation settings. 5References

A.7 Two-stage training and inference 

We first form perturbation-disjoint data splits before any response-derived preprocessing or model fitting. All response-derived preprocessing, control statistics, GRN construction, and evidence fitting are performed using training data only. 

Stage 1: pseudo-bulk pretraining. For each perturbed pseudo-bulk profile, xci is the training control mean for its context. The model is optimized end-to-end, including evidence projections, set encoder, LMU queries, dynamics, and decoder. Candidate memberships are refreshed periodically from synchronized EMA prototypes. The current configuration jointly uses forward prediction, dynamics alignment, target-state grounding, inverse prediction, KL regularization, and LMU regular-ization. 

Stage 2: single-cell pretraining. The stage-1 checkpoint is restored before recomputing evidence-derived quantities. Single-cell profiles are log-transformed and aligned one-to-one to the stage-1 gene order; only shared genes contribute to observation losses. The control reference is estimated from training control cells. Evidence projections are then frozen, and the remaining model continues pretraining at a lower learning rate. In the current configuration, the dynamics and target-state-grounding terms are disabled, while the forward and inverse objectives remain active. At inference, the observed perturbed profile is absent: the model selects LMUs from xci , encodes 

ai, sets zik = μik , predicts bHpi , and returns bxpi = xci + bμi. The target-state, state-grounding, and inverse branches are not required. 

## B Data 

Pseudo-bulk corpus. Stage-1 training uses approximately 200K pseudo-bulk genetic perturbation profiles constructed from publicly available datasets comprising more than 10 million single-cell perturbation profiles, including X-Atlas-Orion [43], Marson [44], and the Virtual Cell Challenge 2025 collection [27]. We use only gene perturbation experiments (e.g., CRISPR knockout, CRISPR interference, and CRISPR activation) and exclude chemical perturbation data. Pseudo-bulk profiles are generated by randomly aggregating 200 cells from the same perturbation condition within each cellular context, improving the signal-to-noise ratio while preserving perturbation-specific and context-specific biological responses. Expression profiles are normalized and log-transformed over a shared gene vocabulary. For each perturbed pseudo-bulk profile, the control reference xci is defined as the mean expression profile of the corresponding training control cells from the same cellular context. 12 Single-cell data. Stage 2 continues pretraining on gene-aligned single-cell genetic perturbation data derived from the same resources, including X-Atlas-Orion [43], Marson [44], and the Virtual Cell Challenge 2025 collection [27]. Profiles are library-size normalized and log-transformed. Only genes shared between Stage 1 and Stage 2 contribute to the observation losses. For each dataset, the control reference is estimated as the mean expression profile of the corresponding training control cells. 

Evaluation Datasets Evaluation datasets are Adamson [1], Norman [22], Replogle K562 and RPE1 [25], and Tian CRISPRa and CRISPRi [31], spanning multiple genetic perturbation screens and cellular contexts. 

Gene evidence. Each gene carries a multimodal evidence embedding built from co-expression, Gene Ontology, MSigDB Hallmark, PROGENy, Gene2Vec, STRING Mashup, Reactome, ESM2, and GTEx representations (Appendix C). Features estimated from perturbational expression are fitted using the training split only.

D.1 Perturbation-disjoint Data Splits 

The perturbation-disjoint constraint is applied to downstream fine-tuning and evaluation splits. Because Stage 1 and Stage 2 pretraining use separate large-scale perturbation corpora, a downstream test perturbation may still appear during pretraining in a different dataset or cellular context. Following the perturbation-disjoint evaluation setting used in prior perturbation-response benchmarks, we split each evaluation dataset by perturbation identity rather than by individual cells. For each random seed, 25% of the genetic perturbations are held out for testing, and all cells associated with a held-out perturbation are assigned to the test set. The remaining perturbations are used for downstream model development, ensuring that no test perturbation appears as an intervention during downstream model fitting. This setting evaluates generalization to held-out perturbation identities rather than to new cells from perturbations already observed during training. We repeat the perturbation-level split across three random seeds and report performance aggregated over the resulting test sets. For downstream evaluation, VCLMU-FT initializes from the Stage-2 pretrained checkpoint and is further fine-tuned using only the downstream training split of each evaluation dataset. Held-out perturbations are never used during downstream fine-tuning. 

D.2 Baseline Implementation Details 

All baselines are evaluated under the same perturbation-disjoint splits described in Appendix D.1. For each dataset and random seed, models are trained using only the downstream training split and evaluated on held-out perturbation identities. For each held-out perturbation, we compare the observed condition-level mean expression profile with the corresponding mean predicted profile. 

GEARS. We use the official cell-gears implementation [26]. GEARS propagates perturbation effects over a gene graph constructed from biological relationships. The model is trained indepen-dently for each dataset and random split with hidden size 64 , batch size 32 , and 50 training epochs using Adam with learning rate 10 −3. Predictions for each held-out perturbation are obtained using the model’s standard perturbation-conditioned prediction procedure. 

CPA. We use the cpa-tools implementation [21]. The model uses a 64 -dimensional latent representation with a Gaussian reconstruction objective. The encoder contains four layers of width 

256 with batch normalization and dropout 0.2, while the decoder contains two layers of width 256 .Perturbation identity, dosage, and cell type are represented as covariates, together with the adversarial training objective of CPA. Models are trained with Adam at learning rate 10 −3 for up to 50 epochs with early stopping. At inference, control cells are provided together with the target perturbation, and predicted profiles are averaged to obtain the condition-level prediction. 

scGPT and scGPT-FT. We use the TransformerGenerator architecture from scGPT [7], with embedding dimension 512 , feed-forward dimension 512 , 12 Transformer layers, 8 attention heads, and dropout 0.2. Perturbation identity is represented as a binary per-gene perturbation indicator together with gene expression values. scGPT is trained from random initialization, whereas scGPT-FT initializes the encoder, value encoder, and Transformer encoder from the pretrained scGPT_human 

checkpoint before downstream fine-tuning. Both variants are trained with batch size 8 using Adam with learning rate 10 −4 for up to 15 epochs and early stopping on validation masked MSE. At inference, predictions are generated from sampled control cells paired with the target perturbation and averaged to obtain the condition-level prediction. 

Perturbed Mean. We include a condition-agnostic reference that predicts the same perturbation response for every held-out perturbation. Specifically, the prediction is the mean expression profile pooled over all non-control cells in the downstream training split, without using the identity of the test perturbation. 

D.3 LMU Interpretability Analysis 

We evaluate the interpretability of the learned LMUs from two complementary perspectives: whether different LMUs form non-redundant gene modules, and whether these modules align with 14 perturbation-specific transcriptional responses. Each background LMU k is associated with a learned candidate gene set Ck, defined by the candidate gene indices retained by the trained model. 

LMU non-redundancy. We first assess whether the learned LMU library collapses to highly overlapping gene modules. For two LMUs k and l, we compute the Jaccard similarity between their candidate gene sets, 

J(k, l ) = |C k ∩ C l||C k ∪ C l| .

We evaluate the distribution of Jaccard similarities over 50,000 randomly sampled pairs of distinct LMUs. We report the median Jaccard similarity, the fraction of sampled pairs with zero candidate-gene overlap, and the fraction with Jaccard similarity below 0.05 . These statistics quantify whether the learned LMU library contains distinct rather than highly redundant gene modules.
