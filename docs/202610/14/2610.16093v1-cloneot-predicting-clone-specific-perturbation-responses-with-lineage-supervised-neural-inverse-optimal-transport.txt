Abstract. Predicting single-cell perturbation responses requires mapping between two unpaired pop-ulations of cells measured before and after the perturbation. Optimal transport (OT) provides a prin-cipled framework for constructing such mappings by minimizing the transport cost, and neural OT methods ( e.g. , CellOT) have been employed for this prediction task. However, these methods rely on a fixed transport cost, typically the squared Euclidean distance. This cost may connect cells with the wrong responses. Lineage tracing provides the information needed to learn the cost, as heritable bar-codes link the cells of the same clone before and after the perturbation. Here, we propose CloneOT, a neural inverse OT method that learns both the transport cost and the transport map from partially lineage-traced single-cell datasets. CloneOT represents the cost as a neural network. It learns the cost by minimizing the extra cost that the lineage adds to the optimal transport, so that the cheapest transport keeps the cells of each clone together. Our experiments demonstrate that CloneOT recovers the correct transport on 2D datasets, while the squared Euclidean cost fails. Moreover, CloneOT out-performs five baselines on 14 lineage-tracing perturbation scenarios of cancer cells, for both seen and unseen clones. Furthermore, we show that CloneOT reveals the clone-specific transcriptional programs of drug-tolerant persister cells and forecasts the fate of each clone from its cells before treatment. Our implementation is available at https://github.com/poseidonchan/CloneOT .

Single-cell perturbation experiments measure how drugs, genetic interventions and other stimuli change the molecular states of individual cells. They have important implications for cancer research [1] and drug development [2]. Predicting these responses computationally is also a key task of recent efforts toward virtual cell models [3,4,5]. In particular, predicting the response of a cell from its state before the perturbation can explain why cells of the same population respond differently. For example, a small fraction of cancer cells persists under targeted therapy [6,7]. However, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) destroys the cells it measures. It is therefore impossible to observe the same cell before and after the perturbation, and a model must learn a mapping between two unpaired populations. Since no cell is observed twice, a model has to decide which cell before the perturbation becomes which cell after it. Previous models make this decision implicitly, by making different assumptions during modeling. scGen assumes that the perturbation shifts every cell by the same vector in a learned latent space [8], and SquidDiff, a conditional diffusion model, applies the same shift to its conditioning vector [9]. chemCPA and bioLORD assume that the basal state of a cell and the effect of the perturbation can be disentangled, so the perturbation changes the effect while the cell keeps its basal state [10,11]. Optimal transport (OT) methods such as CellOT assume that all features of a cell are equally important under the perturbation and they move cells onto the target population at the minimal total cost measured by the squared Euclidean distance [12,13,14]. This assumption does not match the observation that a perturbation changes some features much more than others. However, unpaired data produced by conventional scRNA-seq cannot identify the correct cost. For example, if two equally abundant groups of cells exchanged their responses, the treated population would look exactly the same. Hence, an optimal transport model with the Euclidean cost would then assign both groups the wrong response. Lineage tracing provides the missing link. Heritable barcodes mark the descendants of a common ancestor. The cells of a clone can therefore be identified before and after treatment, even though their states and numbers change [6,15,16,7]. A clone links a set of source cells to a set of target cells without pairing individual cells. Previous methods used lineage information to constrain the couplings of OT-based trajectory inference [17,18] and to supervise transition maps [19,20,21,22]. Nevertheless, these methods relate the cells under a fixed cost or transition model. They do not learn the cost itself. Learning a cost from observed transport is the subject of inverse OT [23,24,25]. When matched pairs are observed, the cost can be estimated by maximizing their likelihood under the entropic coupling [26]. OT-SI is the closest method to lineage tracing, as it learns a cost from correspondences between subsets of two point clouds [27]. However, these methods are discrete OT solvers, so they do not learn a map that predicts the responses of new cells. To address these limitations, we present CloneOT, a neural inverse OT method that learns both the trans-port cost and the transport map from partially lineage-traced single-cell datasets (Fig. 1). Unlike previous methods, CloneOT uses the available lineage information to learn the transport cost and the corresponding neural optimal transport map to predict the response of a new cell from its expression alone. CloneOT therefore uses lineage tracing to learn how the response differs between clones. We demonstrate that CloneOT is capable of 1) recovering the correct transport on two 2D datasets, while the squared Euclidean cost fails; 2) predicting the responses of lineage-traced cancer cells in 14 scenarios more accurately than five baselines, for both seen and unseen clones; and 3) revealing the clone-specific programs of drug-tolerant persister cells, such as the interferon and antioxidant programs of non-cycling and cycling persisters, and forecasting the fate of each clone from its cells before treatment. 

## 2 Methods 

2.1 Overview 

Single-cell perturbation experiments measure one population of cells before a perturbation, the source cells, and another population after it, the target cells. Sequencing destroys the cells it measures, so we never observe how an individual source cell responds. The task is to learn a map that predicts the response of any source cell from its expression (Fig. 1a). Optimal transport (OT) addresses this task by moving the source population onto the target population at the smallest total cost. Most OT methods fix the cost to the squared Euclidean distance. This choice assumes that cells change as little as possible in every direction of the expression space. The assumption fails when a perturbation changes some directions much more than others, and unpaired data alone cannot reveal the correct cost. Lineage tracing provides the information needed to learn the cost. Heritable barcodes mark the descen-dants of each ancestor cell. For a fraction of the clones, we therefore know which source cells and which target cells belong together, although we do not know which source cell gave rise to which target cell (Fig. 1a). CloneOT uses this information with a simple idea. Under a good cost, the cheapest transport of the source population onto the target population should already keep the cells of each clone together. CloneOT there-fore solves two transport problems with the same cost, one without and one with the lineage as a constraint, and measures the extra cost that the lineage adds (Fig. 1b). If the lineage-consistent transport is much more expensive than the best transport, the cost disagrees with the lineage, and CloneOT changes the cost to reduce this gap. The cost is a neural network, so the cost of a change can depend on the state of a cell. Both transport problems are also solved with neural networks, so the cost is learned without differentiating through an iterative solver. After training, a neural map learns the expected response of each cell under the learned transport. The map predicts the response of a new source cell from its expression alone, without its barcode (Fig. 1c). 22.2 CloneOT Problem formulation The training data consist of source cells {xi}Ni=1 and target cells {yj }Mj=1 , with empirical distributions μ and ν. Each cell is represented by the posterior mean of a variational autoencoder (VAE) [28]. For each labeled clone k = 1 , . . . , K , we know its source cells Sk and its target cells Yk. All other cells, including the clones observed on only one side, form the unlabeled sets S0 and Y0. The goal is a map that predicts the response of a new source cell from its expression alone.

2.3 Implementation and baselines 

The potentials and the map are multilayer perceptrons with three hidden layers of width 128. The velocity fields of the flows and the ICNN have two hidden layers of width 64. All networks are trained with Adam. The temperature is ε = 0 .1 m, where m is the median of 12 ∥y − x∥2 over random pairs of training cells. The same settings are used for the 2D datasets and all single-cell tasks (Supplementary Table ?? ). On single-cell data, the map checkpoint is selected on the validation clones. The selection criterion is the mean of ℓ2, 1 − r2

4and the energy score over the validation clones, each relative to the prediction of every clone by the mean of the training target cells. All models were trained on one NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU, and CloneOT trains in a median of 1.6 minutes per model (Supplementary Table ?? ). We compare CloneOT with five published methods. They are the neural OT method CellOT [12], the diffusion model SquidDiff [9] and three generative perturbation models, scGen [8], chemCPA [10] and bi-oLORD [11]. Each method is trained per task with its recommended settings on the same cells and returns one predicted expression profile per source cell. Deterministic methods return their output, chemCPA and bioLORD return the mean of their predicted distribution, and SquidDiff returns one generated profile. Each method keeps the checkpoint with the best energy score on the validation clones, and none of them uses lineage information in training. As in CellOT [12,13], we also report two naive baselines. Identity predicts every source cell by its own expression, and Observed predicts every clone by the training target cells. Ob-served is not an upper bound in the clone-level evaluation, since its clone means are those of the whole target population. On the 2D datasets, we also compare with OT-SI [27], the inverse OT method closest to CloneOT, and with neural W2OT, the solver underlying CellOT [12,38]. OT-SI is a discrete OT solver without a map for new points, so we extend its plan to new points through its dual potential and predict them by the barycentric projection. Supplementary Section ?? lists the settings of all baselines. 

2.4 Datasets and evaluation 

The 2D datasets are the T-shape dataset and the rotated-moons dataset. Methods are evaluated on 400 new source points by the fraction of predictions on the correct class and by the mean distance to the true target position (Supplementary Section ?? ). The single-cell data comprise 14 scenarios from three studies. Twelve time and sorting scenarios come from Watermelon lineage tracing of PC9 lung cancer cells treated with osimertinib [6]. One scenario comes from doxorubicin treatment of MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells with ClonMapper barcodes [15], and one from olaparib treatment of Kuramochi ovarian cancer cells in ReSisTrace [16]. Expression is normalized and log-transformed, and 2,000 highly variable genes are selected on the training cells [40,41]. A VAE with the architecture of W1OT [13], hidden layers of widths 256 and 128 and a 50-dimensional latent space is then trained on the source and target cells of the training set. In the setting of seen clones (IID), about 20% of the cells of every training clone with at least two cells on each side are held out. These evaluation clones are split at random into a validation half for model selection and a test half for evaluation. In the setting of unseen clones (OOD), entire clones are held out, so the test clones never appear in training. In each setting, 10%, 20%, 40% or 80% of the training clones are labeled, in nested sets. Following CellOT [12,13], we evaluate predictions on the top 50 differentially expressed genes of the training cells, separately for every evaluation clone. The metrics are (i) the ℓ2 distance between the predicted and observed clone-mean expression, (ii) the squared Pearson correlation r2 between the two clone means, and (iii) the energy score [42]. 3.3 CloneOT improves with lineage supervision and transfers it to unlabeled clones 

Next, we examine how the amount of lineage information affects CloneOT. The results show that lineage improves every metric for both seen and unseen clones, and that the accuracy generally increases with the fraction of labeled clones (Fig. 4). Without lineage, CloneOT is entropic W2 OT. Like the other OT methods, it then predicts clone means less accurately than Observed. Learning the cost from only 10% of the clones already improves every metric. This indicates that a few labeled clones are enough to improve the cost, so CloneOT suits experiments in which only some clones are identified on both sides. With 80% labeled clones, CloneOT reduces ℓ2 in every scenario for both seen and unseen clones, and it has a significantly lower ℓ2 than the same solver with the fixed squared Euclidean cost in almost every scenario (Supplementary Table ?? ). Removing the flows or replacing the ICNN by a quadratic function also reduces the accuracy (Supplementary Section ?? ). This indicates that a flexible neural transport cost is necessary. Interestingly, the gains extend to the clones without labels. At every fraction of labeled clones, ℓ2 decreases at least as much on unlabeled as on labeled test clones. 3.4 CloneOT reveals the clone-specific programs and fates of drug-tolerant persister cells 

CloneOT predicts the differences between clones. We therefore ask what these differences mean biologically. In the Watermelon study, most clones gave rise to only cycling or only non-cycling persister cells [6]. Cy-cling persisters had higher NRF2 and glutathione antioxidant signatures. Non-cycling persisters had higher interferon-α, cholesterol homeostasis and Notch signatures. None of these programs was upregulated before treatment. We therefore ask which programs CloneOT predicts better with lineage, and whether CloneOT forecasts the fate of a clone and its programs from the cells before treatment. To answer these questions, we decompose the post-treatment cells of the 12 Watermelon scenarios into ten transcriptional programs. The programs are the leading principal components of the training target cells, pooled over the scenarios. We annotate each program by the MSigDB Hallmark gene sets [43,44] enriched among its most positive and most negative genes (Supplementary Table ?? ). For every test clone of the seen-clone setting, we score each program on its observed target cells, on the predictions for its source cells and on its source cells before the perturbation. We then measure how well each model predicts the program scores by the Pearson correlation across test clones, after standardizing the scores within each scenario (Fig. 5a). The confidence intervals and the comparison with CloneOT without lineage come from a paired bootstrap over clones (Supplementary Section ?? ). Lineage does not improve the prediction of the two leading programs, the E2F and G2/M cell-cycle programs. This is consistent with the cell-cycle phase being a transient state rather than a clonal one. In contrast, lineage significantly improves the prediction of the other eight programs, in most scenarios (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. ?? ). Consistent with the original study, these programs include the ones that separate the persister fates. They include an interferon-response program (PC10) and a program that opposes interferon-response genes to MYC targets, glycolysis and antioxidant genes (PC6). They also include 9an EMT program with the NRF2 targets AKR1C2 , AKR1C3 and GSTM4 (PC4) and a p53 stress program (PC5). Scoring every MSigDB gene set separately gives the same pattern (Supplementary Table ?? ). We next forecast the fate of each clone, the central question of the original study. A day-0 clone is cycling if its day-14 descendants appear only in the mCherry-low persister fraction, and non-cycling if they appear only in the mCherry-high fraction [6]. The mCherry-low fraction contains the cells that kept dividing. We describe each seen and unseen evaluation clone by nine fate-related MSigDB gene sets scored on its predicted response, including the NRF2, glutathione, interferon-α and cholesterol signatures. We then classify the two fates by logistic regression with five-fold cross-validation, repeated 20 times (Supplementary Section ?? ). The results show that CloneOT forecasts the fate clearly better than without lineage, from both its day-3 and its day-7 predictions (Fig. 5b). In contrast, the same gene sets scored on the cells before treatment perform at the level of chance. This agrees with the original finding that the two fates arise from distinct lineages, and that their programs are not upregulated before treatment [6]. This indicates that the fate of a clone is hidden in its pre-perturbation state, and CloneOT reveals it by predicting the response of the clone. To test whether CloneOT also forecasts these programs before treatment, we split the test clones of each scenario into thirds by their predicted program score (Fig. 5c). The interferon program (PC10) is led by 

OASL , CCL5 , BST2 and ISG15 . PC6 separates interferon-response genes from antioxidant genes such as 

TXN , NQO1 and PRDX1 . For both programs, the observed score rises clearly from the bottom to the top third of the CloneOT prediction. The predictions without lineage and the scores before treatment separate the clones much less. CloneOT therefore forecasts which clones will induce the interferon program and which the antioxidant program, the two programs that separate non-cycling from cycling persisters. 

## 4 Discussion 

In this work, we develop CloneOT as a neural inverse optimal transport method. CloneOT learns the trans-port cost of single-cell perturbation responses from partial lineage information. Key features distinguishing it from previous methods include (1) a neural transport cost learned from clone sets, with an objective that generalizes the classical inverse OT likelihood from pairs to sets of cells, and (2) a neural entropic OT solver with closed-form conjugates and an envelope gradient, so the cost is learned without unrolling Sinkhorn iterations or map updates. Unlike previous uses of lineage tracing, CloneOT uses lineage to supervise the transport cost itself. More interestingly, all cells share the learned cost, so the information of the labeled clones transfers to clones without labels. Another advantage of CloneOT is its speed, since one model trains in a median of 1.6 minutes on a single GPU. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate that CloneOT recovers the true transport on two 2D datasets, while the squared Euclidean cost fails. On 14 lineage-tracing scenarios, CloneOT predicts clone-level responses more accurately than five baselines, and it captures the differences between clones. In Watermelon, it is the only method that predicts clone means more accurately than the strong Observed baseline [45,46]. In the scenario of drug-tolerant persister cells, CloneOT forecasts from the pre-perturbation state which clones will become cycling persisters, clearly better than without lineage. It also forecasts which clones will induce the interferon and antioxidant programs of the two fates [6], although these programs are not upregulated before treatment. As is previously highlighted, CloneOT learns the transport cost from clone sets, a weak form of super-vision that does not pair individual cells. A potential future development is therefore to add lineage-tracing experiments to the training data of virtual cell models [3,4,5]. These models currently learn perturbation responses from unpaired populations, and our results show that lineage improves the accuracy even when only 10% of the clones are labeled. Benefited from its neural formulation, CloneOT can also be extended to condition the cost on drugs, doses and time points, similar to conditional OT maps [14]. In summary, CloneOT represents a widely applicable framework for learning transport costs from lineage tracing. It also provides a practical training scheme for neural inverse optimal transport. Considering the fact that lineage tracing is increasingly combined with perturbation screens, spatial profiling and multi-modal measurements, we believe that CloneOT will be helpful wherever groups of observations are known to correspond while individual pairings remain hidden. 10 5 Acknowledgements
