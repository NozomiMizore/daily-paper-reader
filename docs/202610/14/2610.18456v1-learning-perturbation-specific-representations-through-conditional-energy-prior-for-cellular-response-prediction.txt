ABSTRACT 

AI-based cellular perturbation prediction is essential to accelerate drug discovery and therapeutic identification. However, perturbation-specific signals are gener-ally obscured by cellular complexity, making it difficult for existing methods to correctly capture perturbation effects. To address this problem, we propose Per-turbEnergy, which introduces a conditional energy-based prior over perturbation representations. It assigns lower energy (higher probability) to representations compatible with the given perturbation and higher energy to others, thereby ex-plicitly tilting the latent space toward perturbation-preferred regions. Besides, PerturbEnergy can be integrated into autoencoder architectures as we find its Ev-idence Lower Bound (ELBO) is equivalent to the ELBO of the complete autoen-coder. We further prove that optimizing PerturbEnergy increases the lower bound on the conditional mutual information between representation and perturbation. Extensive experiments on four benchmark datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of our PerturbEnergy: it improves perturbation recognition especially in latent space with improvement of up to 83.2%; It also achieves state-of-the-art per-formance for predicting top-50/100/200 differentially expressed gene(DEG) re-sponse and remaining competitive on highly variable gene(HVG) prediction. The code is provided: https://github.com/BlueWhaleLab/PerturbEnergy 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Cellular perturbation prediction aims to forecast cell responses to drug and genetic interventions, supporting therapeutic target identification, drug discovery, and personalized medicine. While mod-ern high-throughput CRISPR technologies Jinek et al. (2012); Gilbert et al. (2014); Dixit et al. (2016); Replogle et al. (2020) have dramatically increased the scale of single-cell transcriptomic profiling, experimentally screening the vast space of perturbations across diverse cellular contexts remains costly and labor-intensive. To address this challenge, AI-based virtual cells (AIVCs) Bunne et al. (2024); Resasco et al. (2012) have emerged as an efficient approach for predicting cellular responses using deep learning models. Existing methods generally follow a representation learning paradigm Jiang et al. (2026); Russkikh et al. (2020) where the perturbation effect is captured within the latent representation space. Some approaches use foundation models Cui et al. (2024); Hao et al. (2024); Wang et al. (2024) or spe-cialized encoders Lotfollahi et al. (2019); Russkikh et al. (2020); Jiang et al. (2026); Roohani et al. (2024) to encode cells into latent representations and subsequently predict perturbation responses through generative decoding. Other studies develop more powerful architectures to directly model perturbation-induced transitions between control and perturbed cell representations. For example, STATE Adduri et al. (2026) employs a transition Transformer to model perturbation-induced shifts between cell representations; CellFlow Klein et al. (2025) uses optimal transport to learn mappings between control and perturbed cell distributions; Squidiff He et al. (2026) and PerturbDiff Yuan et al. (2026) characterize such representation transitions through diffusion processes.

Although these methods have demonstrated effectiveness across multiple datasets, they overlook an important issue that may substantially limit their performance. Accurate perturbation prediction depends on two key factors: (i) whether the perturbation effect is well described within the repre-sentation space, and (ii) whether the model is powerful enough to capture such perturbation effects. Existing methods mainly focus on the second factor where they develop sophisticated models and assume that such models can effectively extract perturbation effects from the latent space. However, they pay considerably less attention to the more fundamental question: how much perturbation-specific information is actually encoded in the learned representations for the model to exploit? 

Addressing this issue is non-trivial due to the inherent complexity of cellular systems. Gene expres-sion is influenced by a wide range of context-dependent factors, including cell type, donor back-ground, and culture conditions. Moreover, biological heterogeneity Altschuler & Wu (2010) further complicates the problem: even cells under identical experimental conditions may differ in their physiological states or microenvironments, resulting in substantially different expression profiles. Collectively, all these variations can overwhelm perturbation-induced signals in the representation space, making it difficult for models to distinguish true perturbation effects from spurious and non-generalizable correlations. Although several approaches, such as stVAE Russkikh et al. (2020) and PerturbedVAE Jiang et al. (2026), claim to learn perturbation-specific representations, they remain largely heuristic: These methods typically rely on contrastive or adversarial objectives to implicitly regularize representation learning, providing only soft constraints without a theoretical guarantee that perturbation-specific signals can be well preserved. We conduct an empirical analysis exam-ining such concern, as shown in Table 3 and Figure 1. We find that existing methods, including those specifically designed to learn perturbation representations, exhibit limited perturbation dis-tinctiveness both in the latent space and in their predicted expression profiles. These observations motivate the central question of this work: how can we directly enrich cellular representations with perturbation-specific information to improve cellular response prediction? 

In this paper, we propose a novel method named PerturbEnergy , which leverages an energy-based model to address this issue. The philosophy behind our method is that an energy function provides an effective mechanism for reshaping the distribution landscape Pang & Wu (2021); Zhou et al. (2022). Specifically, to enhance perturbation-specific information in the representation space, we explicitly formulate the latent prior in a normalized energy-based form. The energy model measures the compatibility between latent representations and perturbations, assigning lower energy (higher probability mass) to compatible pairs while assigning higher energy to others. Unlike existing meth-ods that rely on implicit constraints, this design addresses the problem more directly by tilting the latent prior toward perturbation-preferred regions, thereby encouraging the learned representations to contain richer perturbation-specific information. Moreover, the proposed energy model can be easily integrated into autoencoder architectures. We show that the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) associated with the energy model can be optimized separately from the remaining architecture while remaining equivalent to the ELBO of the complete autoencoder. Our energy formulation also offers an additional computational advantage: the gradient of the normalized energy function can be de-rived analytically, while the samples required for optimization can be efficiently obtained through negative sampling or Langevin dynamics. Together, these properties allow the proposed energy model to be optimized independently of specific model architecture. Finally, we theoretically ana-lyze the effectiveness of PerturbEnergy and prove that it can increase a lower bound on the mutual information between perturbations and latent representations. To validate our method, we conduct comprehensive experiments on several benchmark datasets. The results show that PerturbEnergy substantially enhances perturbation-specific information, particularly in the latent representation space, with improvements of up to 83.2%. It also consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance 2Preprint on top-50/100/200 differentially expressed gene (DEG) prediction and competitive performance on highly variable gene (HVG) prediction. 

2 RELATED WORK 

We summarize existing methods for cellular perturbation prediction from a representation learn-ing perspective. Linear models Ahlmann-Eltze et al. (2024) perform regression in the expression space or on representations from foundation models such as scGPT Cui et al. (2024) to predict responses. VAE-based methods model perturbation effects in latent space. For example, scGen Lot-follahi et al. (2019) and Biolord Piran et al. (2024) represent perturbation effects as additive latent factors; SAMS-VAE Bereket & Karaletsos (2023) models perturbations using sparse latent repre-sentations. Other approaches design more advanced architectures to capture perturbation effects in the representation space. GEARS Roohani et al. (2024) learns genetic perturbation representations using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs); STATE Adduri et al. (2026) uses a Transformer to model perturbation-induced latent shifts; and diffusion or flow-based methods He et al. (2026); Yuan et al. (2026); Klein et al. (2025); Bunne et al. (2023) learn transitions between control and perturbed representations through diffusion or flow matching process. All these methods focus on learning perturbation effects from the representation space, but ignore whether the latent space contains suf-ficient perturbation-specific information. Although some studies claim to learn perturbation-awared representations, they remain intuitive: PerturbedVAE Jiang et al. (2026) employs a Contrastive ob-ject to disentangle perturbation representations from other factors; stVAE Russkikh et al. (2020) and CPA Lotfollahi et al. (2023) use adversarial training to promote such disentanglement. These aux-iliary objectives provide only implicit constraints without a theoretical guarantee that perturbation-specific information is well preserved in the latent space. 

3 METHOD 

3.1 PRELIMINARY 

Before introducing our method, we first formalize the basic modeling framework and then explain why perturbation-specific information is important. Cellular perturbation prediction aims to pre-dict gene expression induced by genetic or drug perturbations and we study it from a probabilis-tic perspective, assuming that each response is jointly determined by the cellular context and the applied perturbation. Formally, Let x ∈ RG denotes the gene-expression profile of a perturbed cell, a ∈ A denotes its perturbation, and c ∈ C denotes its cellular context. 1 Given observations 

D = {(xi, c i, a i)}Ni=1 , the goal is to learn a distribution p(x|c, a ) that predicts the cellular expres-sion profile. To learn perturbation-specific effect, we decompose the latent representation into two components z = ( zb, z a), where zb captures the basal cellular state that is invariant to perturbation and za captures perturbation-specific variation. The resulting joint distribution factorizes as: 

p(x, z b, z a|c, a ) = p(x|zb, z a)p(zb, z a|c, a ), (1) where p(x|zb, z a) is the perturbation expression distribution conditioned on latent representations and can be parameterized by a generative decoder pθ (x|zb, z a) with parameter θ; p(zb, z a|c, a ) is the latent prior distribution, we adopt the variational inference like prior work Jiang et al. (2026) to approximate it as: 

qϕ(zb, z a|xctr , c, a ) = qϕ(zb|xctr , c )qϕ(za|xctr , a, c ), (2) where xctr is the controlled (unperturbed) cell expression under the same context c. To model such a joint distribution, existing approaches Jiang et al. (2026); Yuan et al. (2026); Adduri et al. (2026) either use VAE to learn perturbation-aware representations via implicit constraints or employ power-ful decoders pθ (x|zb, z a) to learn perturbation effects from the latent space. However, these methods do not directly address the concern: does the latent space contain sufficient perturbation-specific information for the model to exploit? Actually, the amount of perturbation-specific information retained in the latent space directly affects how easily the model can learn perturbation effects. 3.2 CONDITIONAL ENERGY -B ASED PERTURBATION PRIOR AND ENERGY -ELBO To address the above concern, we propose PerturbEnergy , which introduces a conditional energy-based prior over the perturbation latent space. The framework is shown in Figure 2. Our design is motivated by the observation that an energy function can serve as a flexible mechanism for revising a target distribution Pang & Wu (2021); Zhou et al. (2022). Specifically, we factorize the joint latent prior in Eq. equation 1 as: 

p(zb, z a|c, a ) = pβ (zb|c)pα(za|zb, a ), (3) where pβ (zb|c) denotes the marginal prior over the basal representation, and pα(za|zb, a ) denotes the conditional prior over the perturbation representation. To enrich the latent space with perturbation-specific information, we formulate pα(za|zb, a ) as a conditional energy-based distribution: 

pα(za|zb, a ) = p0(za) exp [ −Eα(za, z b, a )] /F α(zb, a ), (4) where p0(za) denotes the prior without perturbation constraints (i.e, the prior used in existing works for perturbation space). Eα(za, z b, a ) is the energy-based model that revises the prior according to the perturbation: for a given basal state zb and perturbation a, it assigns lower energy (higher probability mass) to the perturbation representation za that are more compatible with this condition and assigns higher energy to others. Unlike existing approaches Yuan et al. (2026); Russkikh et al. (2020) that encourage perturbation-specific information through implicit constraints, our method di-rectly modifies the latent prior of the perturbation space, explicitly tilting it toward the perturbation-preferred region. Here, Eα(za, z b, a ) can be implemented as a scalar-valued neural network param-eterized by α. Fα(zb, a ) is the corresponding partition function ensuring the normalization of the distribution, which is defined as:

Implementation: PerturbEnergy is model-agnostic and and we implement it with a standard VAE backbone. Both encoders use two-layer MLPs with a latent dimension of 512, while context c and perturbation a are represented by learnable embedding tables. Eα(za, z b, a ) is implemented as a simple neural network: we concatenate za and zb and feed it to a two-layer MLP and then output the cosine similarity score with the embedding of a. A two-layer MLP decoder predicts the expression of 2,000 HVGs from the concatenated latent representations. We train with Adam Kingma & Ba (2014) at a learning rate of 10 −3, using batch sizes and maximum training steps of (256, 20,000) for Norman2019, (512, 40,000) for Replogle, (512, 60,000) for PBMC and (512, 160,000) Tahoe100M. By default, PerturbEnergy-Langevin(Lang) uses K = 20 and η = 10 −4, while PerturbEnergy-Mismatch(Mis) randomly samples embeddings from other perturbations as negatives. During in-ference, we set the candidate number M = 8 by default. HVG Group. Figure 3 summarizes the performance of all methods on the HVG group, with detailed results provided in Appendix A.3.8. DEG Group. Unlike the HVG group which evaluates general gene expression patterns, the DEG group metrics focus on predicting perturbation-specific responses. We conduct experiments for the DEG group metrics and report the RMSE results in Table 2, with the remaining metrics and cor-responding analysis provided in the Appendix A.3.7. We have the following key observations: (i) Although linear methods can capture general expression patterns in the HVG group, they strug-gle to predict perturbation-induced changes, suggesting that simple regression models may focus on house keeping gene patterns rather than perturbation effects. (ii) CellFlow, Squidiff, and Per-turbDiff model perturbation effects through diffusion or flow-matching processes, but their perfor-mance varies across datasets. For example, PerturbDiff achieves state-of-the-art performance on Norman2019 but struggles on Replogle. This variation suggests that relying primarily on model expressiveness may not consistently capture perturbation effects when they are obscured by other sources of cellular variation. Notably, STATE achieves competitive performance, potentially benefit-ing from batch-level self-attention that mitigates cellular heterogeneity and its large model capacity. (iii) Among representation learning-based methods, PerturbedVAE achieves better performance than other baselines, highlighting the potential benefits of explicitly modeling perturbation-specific infor-mation. (iv) Despite using a simple MLP backbone, PerturbEnergy achieves the best performance on Replogle, PBMC, and Tahoe100M and the second-best performance on Norman2019. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of our design in facilitate capturing perturbation-specific effects. 

Perturbation Study. To evaluate how effectively different methods capture perturbation-specific information, we conduct an analysis using two types of inputs: (i) latent representations and (ii) ex-pression differences between predicted perturbed cells and observed control cells. For each setting, we train a two-layer MLP classifier to predict perturbation labels. The results are reported in Table 3 and Figure 1. For latent representations, scGPT captures limited perturbation effect as it’s likely to be subtilized; STATE and PerturbDiff achieve limited classification accuracy, as their representations primarily capture basal cellular states; VAE-based methods perform better by incorporating pertur-bation information into the latent space. Besides, richer perturbation-specific information generally leads to better perturbation prediction performance. For example, PerturbedVAE achieves higher accuracy than CPA and outperforms CPA in Top-50/100/200 RMSE. (See Table 2). However, their accuracy remains below 50% on Replogle, PBMC, and Tahoe100M, suggesting that existing rep-resentation learning strategies may not sufficiently preserve perturbation-specific information. For expression differences, STATE achieves competitive performance, whereas PerturbDiff struggles compared with other baselines. This variation suggests that relying primarily on model may not consistently capture perturbation effects. VAE-based methods also exhibit degraded performance, potentially because cellular complexity obscures perturbation-induced changes in the expression. In contrast, PerturbEnergy achieves the best performance in both settings, with particularly substan-tial improvements in latent representation classification (83.2% on Replogle and 28.2% on PBMC). These results demonstrate the effectiveness of our method in enriching latent representations with perturbation-specific information.

5 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we introduce PerturbEnergy, a framework designed to enhance perturbation-specific information for cellular perturbation prediction. PerturbEnergy uses an energy function to reshape the latent prior, directing it toward perturbation preferred regions. We theoretically demonstrate that PerturbEnergy increases the mutual information between the representations and perturbations. Extensive experiments on four datasets validate its effectiveness.

Replogle (genetic perturbation). The Replogle dataset Replogle et al. (2022) is a landmark re-source for studying single-cell genetic perturbation effects. It integrates multiple pooled CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) Gilbert et al. (2013); Nadig et al. (2025) experiments across four human cell lines: K562, RPE1, Jurkat and HepG2. It systematically repressing thousands of essential genes while measuring transcriptome-wide responses. Because the targeted genes are essential, a single knockdown often induces broad transcriptional changes that affect hundreds of downstream genes. 

PBMC (cytokine signaling). The PBMC dataset Yuan et al. (2026) is a large-scale signaling per-turbation atlas of peripheral blood mononuclear cells. It profiles approximately 10 million human cells collected from 12 healthy donors, covering 18 immune cell types and 90 distinct cytokine treatments alongside a PBS control condition. After quality control, about 9.7 million cells are re-tained, with a mean sequencing depth of roughly 31,000 reads per cell. Many cytokines elicit strong transcriptional responses, typically upregulating more than 50 genes, which makes this dataset a representative testbed for studying perturbation-induced shifts at the population level. 

Tahoe100M (drug perturbation). Tahoe100M (Zhang et al., 2025) is the largest single-cell pertur-bation atlas released to date. It comprises over 100 million single-cell expression profiles measured across 50 human cancer cell lines exposed to more than 1,100 combinations of small-molecule drug and dosage. The atlas adopts a multiplexed cell-village design during sequencing, which substan-tially reduces batch effects and yields high data quality. Its scale and the breadth of its cellular contexts make it a demanding benchmark for context-dependent drug response prediction. 

Norman2019 (combinatorial genetic perturbation). The Norman2019 dataset Norman et al. (2019) is a Perturb-seq screen of combinatorial CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) in K562 cells. Its value lies not in the diversity of cellular contexts but in the structure of its perturbation space: it comprises 105 single-gene activations and 131 two-gene combinations, and both constituent genes of every combination are also activated individually elsewhere in the screen. This makes it the stan-dard benchmark for combinatorial generalization, meaning predicting the transcriptional outcome of a gene pair from the effects of its individual members. Such predictions are far from trivial, because gene pairs frequently interact non-additively: two genes whose individual effects are mild may act synergistically, and a naive additive model that simply sums their single-gene effects fails to recover the resulting phenotype. We use the filtered version of the dataset as VC Benchmark Mao et al. (2026), which contains 111,445 cells. 

Preprocessing. We apply the same preprocessing pipeline as PerturbDiff Yuan et al. (2026), STATE Adduri et al. (2026) and VC Benchmark Mao et al. (2026). For PBMC and Tahoe100M, raw counts are first normalized per cell to a fixed library size( 10 4), followed by a log1p transformation to rescale the gene expression values into range [0 , 1) . For Replogle, we directly adopt the processed data released by STATE Adduri et al. (2026), which first filters cells and perturbations by on-target knockdown efficacy to improve the signal-to-noise ratio of perturbation effects, and then applies the same library-size normalization and log transformation. After normalization, we follow standard practice Wolf et al. (2018) and select the top 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) for each dataset from its original transcriptome vocabulary using scanpy.pp.highly variable genes . Ex-cluding low-variance genes improves the signal-to-noise ratio without sacrificing biological fidelity, and all models are trained and evaluated in this 2,000-dimensional HVG space. Norman2019 is distributed as raw counts and no curated gene panel is available for it, so we apply the same normal-ization pipeline and then select the top 2,000 HVGs from its 33,694-gene vocabulary. 

Train/validation/test splits. For Replogle, PBMC and Tahoe100M, we follow the hold-out proto-col of PerturbDiff Yuan et al. (2026) and STATE Adduri et al. (2026), which measures generalization 18 Preprint to unseen cellular contexts. For each dataset, a subset of contexts is held out for testing, and 30% of the perturbations measured in those held-out contexts are moved back into the training set. This partial-coverage design ensures that every perturbation is observed somewhere during training, so that the test set isolates adaptation to a new cellular context rather than extrapolation to an unknown perturbation label. Concretely: • PBMC. Four of the twelve donors are reserved for testing. 30% of the perturbations from these held-out donors are moved into training, leaving the remaining 70% for testing. Note that the model conditions on cell type (18 categories), while the hold-out is taken over donors; at test time the control cells are therefore drawn from the held-out donors them-selves. • Tahoe100M. PCA is first performed on the pseudobulk expression profiles of the 50 cell lines to identify distinct phenotypic clusters, from which five cell lines are selected as the held-out test set, again with 30% of their perturbations returned to training. • Replogle. One cell line (HepG2) is held out for testing, and the model is trained on the remaining three cell lines together with 30% of the perturbations randomly sampled from HepG2. 

Norman2019 contains a single cell line, so holding out a cellular context is degenerate. We there-fore split over the perturbations themselves, following the VC benchmark Mao et al. (2026): 30% of the two-gene combinations (39 of 131) are held out as the test set, while all 105 single-gene perturbations remain in training. This is the “0 of 2 unseen” generalization setting. Each held-out combination is never observed during training, yet both of its constituent genes have been perturbed individually, which isolates the ability to compose known single-gene effects into an unseen combi-nation, rather than confounding it with extrapolation to genes for which no perturbation data exist at all. It is also what keeps the held-out combinations representable by our model: we embed a perturbation as the sum of the embeddings of its target genes, so an unseen pair is encoded from the two single-gene rows that training has already fitted. A per-label lookup table, by contrast, would have no entry for such a pair and could not make a prediction at all. Control cells remain in the training pool under all three protocols: they are the unperturbed reference population that the model is given at inference time, and they are therefore counted separately from the train/validation/test splits. Finally, we hold out 2% of the training cells uniformly at random as a validation set. This set is used only for checkpoint selection; all reported results come from the held-out contexts in the test split. A.3.2 BASELINE SETTINGS 

Baseline models 

Linear methods. The linear baseline Ahlmann-Eltze et al. (2024) predicts a perturbed cell as the control cell plus the mean expression shift of that perturbation measured on the training split, ˆx =

xctrl + ∆ a, with the shift ∆a taken against the controls of the contexts in which the perturbation was observed. Despite its simplicity, this predictor is a strong baseline on average-expression metrics and serves as the linear reference point of the comparison. scGPT (Cui et al., 2024) replaces the raw expression profile with a pretrained cell representation: we use the released scGPT human 

checkpoint (12 layers, dmodel =512 , 8 heads, 51.9M parameters, pretrained on 33M cells), keep it frozen in evaluation mode, and train only a lightweight head on top, ˆx = xctrl + MLP([ u − z]) ,where z is the <cls> embedding of the control cell and u the perturbation embedding. Cells are tokenised exactly as during scGPT’s pretraining: gene symbols mapped through its 60,697-entry vocabulary, nonzero genes only, per-cell quantile binning into 51 bins, and a prepended <cls> 

token. The head’s output layer is zero-initialised, so training starts from “predict no change”. This row answers a narrower question than the others: whether a representation pretrained on external data already encodes how a cell responds to a perturbation. 

VAE-based methods. All the VAE-based methods are implemented via their official codes. sc-Gen Lotfollahi et al. (2019) trains an unconditional VAE on all cells. After training, the pertur-bation vector ∆a = mean {za} − mean {zctr } is measured on the training split, where za is the representation of perturbed cells and zctr is the representations of controlled cells. And prediction is ˆx = Dec(Enc( xctrl ) + ∆ a). CPA (Lotfollahi et al., 2023) decomposes a cell into a basal state 19 Preprint plus additive perturbation and covariate embeddings, z = Enc( x) + ua + uc, and enforces the decomposition with adversarial discriminators trained to disentangle the perturbation and the co-variates of the basal latent. Prediction swaps the perturbation embedding to the corresponding one. 

stVAE (Russkikh et al., 2020) is a style-transfer VAE: expression is decoupled into a perturbation-independent content latent and a perturbation-specific style vector, and prediction decodes the sum of the control cell’s content and the target perturbation’s style. PerturbedVAE (Jiang et al., 2026) splits the latent into a perturbation-invariant part zi and a perturbation-responsive part zv , and makes the split persist with a contrastive term that anchors zi to the corresponding control cell. The predic-tion mechanism is a learned conditional prior p(zv | zi, u ), which supplies the response at inference time. As their official implementation suggests, CPA, stVAE, and PerturbedVAE are trained on ob-served cells and scGen is trained unconditionally. For a fair comparison, we adopt their official VAE architecture but unify the latent embedding size the same as ours: 512. 

Diffusion-based methods. PerturbDiff (Yuan et al., 2026) follows its official implementation, it runs a denoising diffusion process over the cell population rather than over individual cells. The de-noiser is an MM-DiT stack (4 blocks, 8 heads, latent dimension is setted as 512) with joint attention over the control and perturbed token streams, a single shared timestep noises the whole population so that the diffused object remains a distribution, and the denoising objective is the MMD between the denoised and the real population. Inference uses 50 DDIM steps as it general leads to excel-lent performance. Squidiff (He et al., 2026) runs a conditional DDIM in a VAE semantic latent (dimension of z is adjusted to 512 as other models) with an MLP denoiser and sinusoidal timestep embeddings, and decodes the sampled latent. Its autoencoder is warmed-up trained alone for the first 4,000 steps, after which the encoder is frozen so that the latent diffusion trains against a fixed target. 

Flow-based methods. CellFlow Klein et al. (2025) learns a conditional velocity field that transports the control population to the perturbed one. Training pairs the two minibatches by exact minibatch optimal transport under a squared-Euclidean cost, interpolates xt = (1 −t)x0 +tx 1 with t ∼ U (0 , 1) ,and regresses vθ (xt, t, cond ) onto x1 − x0, where the conditioning combines the perturbation and context embeddings with the pooled control population. Inference integrates the field from t=0 to 

t=1 with a Heun solver in 50 steps. 

Transformer-based method. STATE Adduri et al. (2026) is a bidirectional transformer over sets of cells, where the perturbation embedding is added to every token and the model predicts the transition from each control cell. We reproduce the State Embedding (SE), State Transition (ST), and adapt the embedding size to 512, where SE uses ESM2 embedding and project into 512 dimension. For Replogle, PBMC and Tahoe100M, we use the the same training dataset for SE: CellxGene Program et al. (2025) with the same reconstruction target. The learning rate is set as 1 × 10 −5, weight decay is set as 0.01, batch size is set as 128 and epoch number is 12. As for Norman2019, SE is pretrained on its training set with the same training settings but 5 epochs. Training settings All the baselines follow the same data preprocess and split(train/validation/test) settings as described in Appendix A.3.1. We use the same condition sampler and random seed for all the methods: A batch is n blocks of set size cells, where each block pairs control cells of a context with real perturbed cells of one (context, perturbation) condition. Every model therefore sees the same 512 cells per step (256 on Norman2019). As for the maximum training step budget, we set 20,000 steps on Norman2019, 40,000 on Replogle, 60,000 on PBMC and 160,000 on Tahoe100M for all the methods. All models are optimised with Adam (weight decay: 10 −6) under their own best learning rate. The loss function and corresponding settings of each baseline follows its official suggestion. A cosine learning-rate decay is used for PerturbDiff, Squidiff, CellFlow and STATE, and a constant rate otherwise. For the two diffusion baselines and CellFlow we additionally keep an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights (decay 0.999) and validate, checkpoint and evaluate that average, since EMA is part of how diffusion and flow-matching models are trained in practice and their reported sample quality is not what the raw iterate gives.
