# Abstract 

A central hypothesis in aging biology proposes that the gradual accumulation of stochastic 

molecular damage drives transcriptional deregulation (TD), ultimately leading to functional 

decline. However, existing single -cell analyses largely rely on static ex pression measurements and 

provide limited resolution of how regulatory responses change at the level of individual cells.  Here, 

we leverage DKO, a deep learning framework that predicts cell -specific virtual gene  perturbation 

effects from unperturbed transc riptomes, capturing cell -to -cell  differences in regulatory context 

without requiring a reconstructed gene regulatory network or matched perturbation data.  Applying 

DKO to single -cell transcriptomic datasets from humans, mice, Drosophila, and C. elegans 

revealed that the transcriptional consequences of perturbing the same gene became increasingly 

heterogeneous across cells with age, indicating that gene regul atory responses become less 

consistent and more dependent on cellular context. Furthermore, we find t hat cells with higher 

somatic mutation burdens  have  greater  knockout -response variability, linking accumulated 

molecular damage to regulatory  inconsistency . Within the same cellular populations, higher impact 

variability was also associated with higher senescence scores, linking transcriptional dysregulation 

to senescence -associated states . Our  findings identify a progressive loss of regulatory robustness 

at both the gene and cell levels as a conserved feature of aging, linking stochastic molecular 

damage to increasing heterogeneity in cellular regulatory responses. 2

# Introduction 

Aging is characterized by the progressive accumulation of stochastic molecular damage that 

challenges cellular homeostasis and ultimately contributes to functional decline 1,2 . Genomic 

instability and epigenetic alterations are established hallmarks of aging, while somatic mutations 

and epimutations accumulate throughout life as a consequence of endogenous processes and 

environmental exposures 2,3 . Because these alterations arise stochastically, different cells 

accumulate distinct patterns of molecular damage 4,5 . Therefore, a central unresolved question is 

how heterogeneous and largely random alterations at the level of individual cells can converge on 

the recurrent functional deterioration that characterizes aging 6.

Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) provide a plausible mechanistic bridge between 

stochastic molecular damage and systematic cellular dysfunction 7. GRNs organize interactions 

among genes and their regulators, coordinate transcriptional programs, stabilize cellular identity, 

and help maintain cellular functions in the presence of internal and external perturbations 8. Their 

architecture can confer robustness through feedback, modularity, redundancy, and distributed 

regulation, while specific regulatory motifs determine properties such as response speed, 

persistence, and fluctuations  8,9 . Thus, the impact of molecular damage on regulatory components 

is not local, as perturbations can propagate through interconnected regulatory circuits and 

influence broader transcriptional programs. Progressive disruption of this regulatory architecture 

could weaken the capacity of cells to buffer molecular perturbations, converting initially local and 

stochastic damage into increasingly widespread transcriptional abnormalities 10 .

This reasoning motivates the transcriptional deregulation (TD) hypothesis of aging, in 

which accumulated molecular damage , such as mutations and epimutations,  progressively 

compromises transcriptional regulation and thereby contributes to age -associated functional 

decline 3,11 . Advances in single -cell transcriptomics have made it possible to examine this process 

at the level where stochastic damage occurs: individual cells. A widely studied manifestation of 

TD is increased cell -to -cell transcriptional heterogeneity. Increased v ariability in gene expression 

has been reported in aged mouse cardiomyocytes, immune cells following stimulation, human 

pancreatic cells, and other aging tissues 11,12 . These observations suggest that aging can be 

accompanied by a loss of transcriptional consistency among otherwise related cells. 

However, conventional measures of transcriptional heterogeneity provide an incomplete 

description of TD. Differential expression captures changes in mean transcriptional state but 3

cannot distinguish adaptive or compensatory responses from regulatory deterioration. Likewise, 

increased expression variance does not necessarily imply failure of the underlying regulatory 

system, because transcriptional variability can arise from intrinsi c gene -expression stochasticity, 

changes in cellular state or composition, and technical variation. Indeed, systematic comparisons 

across aging single -cell datasets have shown that conventional measures of transcriptional noise 

do not consistently increase  across tissues and cell types 13 . Thus, TD cannot be defined simply as 

increased variability in the expression of individual genes. 

A more system -level view is provided by gene -to -gene transcriptional coordination. Rather 

than asking whether individual genes become more variable, this approach asks whether genes 

continue to exhibit coordinated transcriptional behavior across cells.  Recently, we  showed that 

global gene -to -gene coordination declines with age across diverse organisms, tissues, and cell 

types 14 . Importantly, reduced coordination was also associated with a higher burden of an age -

related somatic mutational signature and with radiation -induced DNA damage 14 . These findings 

support the idea that aging -associated TD may involve a broad deterioration of transcriptional 

organization and provide a link between accumulated molecular damage and loss of regulatory 

coordination.  Yet global gene -to -gene coordination leaves important aspects of TD unresolved. A 

population -level decline in transcriptional coordination cannot show how strongly dysregulation 

differs among genes or among individual cells. Because molecular damage accumu lates 

stochastically, individual cells  may gradually develop different regulatory states, leading to 

increasing divergence within an aging population ( Fig. 1a ). We therefore asked whether this loss 

of regulatory coherence could also be detected through the effects of gene perturbation. If  gene 

regulatory networks  become more heterogeneous with age, perturbing the same gene should 

produce increasingly different effects across cells, while cells with  increased GRN heterogeneity, 

possibly due to  greater aging -related  molecular  damage , should show  greater divergence in their 

overall gene -impact profiles ( Fig. 1b ). Examining TD at these levels could therefore provide a 

more detailed view of how transcriptional regulation changes during aging. 

Here, we  leverage  DKO 15 , a deep learning framework that uses transcriptome -wide virtual 

gene perturbations to study TD at gene and single -cell resolution. Rather than explicitly 

reconstructing GRN topology, DKO learns the relationship between cellular gene states and 

steady -stat e transcriptional profiles. By virtually perturbing individual genes and propagating 

these perturbations through the learned model, DKO estimates the transcriptional impact of each 4

gene in each cellular context. Because these predicted impacts depend on the transcriptional state 

of individual cells, they allow differences in regulatory behavior to be measured across genes and 

cells.  This framework therefore provides a gene - and cell -resolved view of regulatory behavior 

underlying TD. 

Applying DKO across aging single -cell transcriptomic datasets from humans, mice, 

Drosophila, and  C. elegans  revealed a  increased virtual KO impact variability across single cells, 

suggesting a conserved age associated loss of gene regulatory network coherency across cells . The 

predicted transcriptional consequences of perturbing the same gene became increasingly 

heterogeneous across cells with age, indicating that gene regulatory influence becomes less 

consistent and increasingly dependent on cellular conte xt.  We then, further asked what drives this 

increased transcriptional perturbation -response variability and found that  cells with higher somatic 

mutational burdens exhibited greater perturbation -response variability, further associating 

accumulated molecular damage with regulatory instability at the single -cell level.  Similarly, cells 

with higher senescence levels exhibited greater gene - and cell -level perturbation -response 

variability, including within the same cellular population, further linking senesce nce to reduced 

regulatory robustness.  These  result s suggest that aging -associated TD involves a progressive loss 

of regulatory consistency at the level of individual genes and cells. DKO therefore provides a 

framework for quantifying how the robustness of transcriptional regulation deteriorates during 

agi ng and for investigating how stochastic molecular damage may be translated into heterogeneous 

cellular dysfunction. 

# Results 

DKO enables transcriptome -wide virtual perturbation profiling at single -cell resolution 

Although experimental perturbation remains essential for functional validation, profiling 

perturbation responses across many genes and large numbers of individual young and aged cells 

is not yet feasible at scale. We therefore leveraged a data -driven knock out (DKO) framework to 

predict single -cell gene expression profiles and knockout impacts directly from unperturbed 

transcriptomic data, without requiring experimentally perturbed data for  model training 15 . DKO 

adopts a top -down, data -driven strategy rather than explicitly reconstructing complete gene -to -

gene regulatory networks. Given single -cell gene expression data from a defined cell type, DKO 

uses a deep -learning model to learn the mapping between a cell 's gene assemblage and its 5

corresponding gene expression profile  (see  Methods ). This approach leverages naturally observed 

variation in gene -expression space to learn the implicit relationships that constrain cellular 

expression states, thereby providing a data -driven representation of the expression landscape of a 

specific cell typ e. 

After training, we performed single -cell virtual knockout experiments by removing an 

individual target gene from the gene assemblage of each cell and using the trained model to predict 

the corresponding post -knockout expression profile ( Fig. 1c ). The knockout impact was quantified 

as the Bray -Curtis dissimilarity between the predicted post -knockout profile and the original pre -

knockout profile.  Repeating this procedure across cells and expressed genes enabled systematic, 

transcriptome -wi de  virtual knockout at single -cell resolution.  DKO  can be applied across species, 

tissues and cell types for which suitable single -cell expression data are available . It therefore 

provid es  a framework for testing the aging -associated transcriptional deregulation hypothesis at 

both the gene and single -cell levels .

Gene p erturbation -response variability increases with aging 

To examine the transcriptional deregulation hypothesis at the gene level, we quantified the 

variability of gene perturbation effects across individual cells. DKO assigns an impact value to 

each gene -cell pair  (𝑔 , 𝑐 ), representing the predicted transcriptomic effect of perturbing a given 

gene  𝑔  in a specific cell  𝑐 . For each gene, we defined gene -level perturbation -response variability 

as the  median absolute deviation  of its predicted impact across cells (see  Methods ).  Higher 

variability therefore indicates that perturbing the same gene produces more heterogeneous effects 

among individual cells. 

We first investigated  gene -impact variability  relationship using GRN dynamical 

simulations, in which individual cells were initialized from a common baseline GRN and 

subsequently subjected to stochastic regulatory damage (Fig.  S1a ). Each regulatory edge was 

independently subjected to damage with probability p. Increasing p from 0 to 0.15 progressively 

increased the extent of regulatory damage, allowing the GRNs of individual cells to diverge from 

their shared initial state and from  one another. To obtain gr ound -truth perturbation impacts, we 

individually knocked out each gene in each simulated cell by setting the target gene to zero and 

rerunning the GRN dynamics to steady state. The resulting expression profile was then compared 

with its corresponding unper turbed profile ( see Methods ). Ground -truth gene impact variability 6

increased with regulatory damage (Fig.  S1b ), indicating that perturbing the same gene produced 

increasingly divergent effects across cells.  Importantly, using only the corresponding unperturbed 

expression profiles, DKO successfully predicted the same increase in gene impact variability with 

increasing regulatory damage ( Fig. S1c ), closely matching the trend obtained from the ground -

truth perturbation effects. 

We next systematically examined whether a similar  increased gene impact variability 

pattern could be observed in real single -cell aging datasets. DKO was applied separately to each 

species, tissue and cell type, and the variability of each gene's predicted impact was calculated 

independently within young and aged populations. Each violin  plot in  Fig. 2  represents the 

distribution of gene -level perturbation -response variability across the analyzed genes. We 

evaluated  single -cell  data across a diverse panel o f cell types spanning four distinct species, 

including human ovary (23 -29 vs. 49 -54 years;  Fig. 2 a, Fig. S2 ), human skeletal muscle (15 -20 

vs. 70 -75 years;  Fig. 2 b, Fig. S3 ), mouse tissues from Tabula Muris Senis (3 vs. 24 months;  Fig s.

2c,d, Fig. S4 ),  C. elegans  (day 3 vs. day 11;  Fig. 2 e) and Drosophila brain (day 0 vs. day 30;  Fig. 

2f). Age groups followed the definitions of the original studies.  Detailed dataset information, 

including data sources,  cell -type annotations, young and old cell numbers, and  exact  P values for 

all comparisons, is provided in  Table 1  and Supplementary  Tables S 1-S6. Across 45 tissue –cell -

type comparisons, gene impact variability was higher in aged cells in 41 comparisons, with 40 

reaching statistical significance. Four comparisons showed lower variability in aged cells, three of 

which were significant, while two compa risons were not significant. Thus, although the direction 

was not uniform across all cell types, the predominant pattern was an age -associated increase in 

gene impac t variability, indicating that the predicted effect of perturbing the same gene becomes 

more heterogeneous across individual cells with age. 

To assess whether this age -associated increase depended on the age group used for model 

training, we further performed a cross -age evaluation using mouse naive B cells, granulocytes, and 

promonocytes  from bone marrow  (Fig. S 5). DKO models were trained exclusively on either young 

or aged cells, and each trained model was subsequently applied to both age groups without 

retraining. Under both training strategies, aged cells consistently exhibited higher gene -level 

perturbation -re sponse variability than young cells. These results indicate that the observed age -

associated increase is robust to the choice of training age group, rather than arising from training 

separate models for young and aged populations. 7

These results demonstrate a consistent aging -associated increase in gene -level 

perturbation -response variability across the examined biological contexts, supporting the 

transcriptional deregulation hypothesis at the gene level. 

Somatic mutation burden is associated with increased cell impact variability 

The accumulation of somatic mutations is a common feature of  aging 1,4,16 . A fundamental question 

is whether the increase in impact variability observed during  aging  is linked to mutational damage 

accumulation, or whether these represent independent characteristics of  aging . Because somatic 

mutations occur stochastically in individual cells, their accumulation may alter cellular regulatory 

states and thereby change the predicted impacts of multiple gene perturbations. Here, we asked 

whether greater somatic mutation burden is  associated with increased cell impact variability, 

ref lecting greater divergence of each cell’s gene -impact profile from a low -mutation reference 

state  (see  Methods ). To test this association, within each dataset or donor, cells were ranked by 

mutation burden and divided into low -, mid -, and high -mutation groups. The low -mutation group 

was used to establish a gene -specific reference impact profile, defined by the median predicted 

impact of each gene. Cell impact variability was then calculated as the summed absolute difference 

between e ach cell’s gene impacts  and the corresponding reference values (see  Methods ), and 

directly compared between the  mid - and high -mutation groups. 

We first evaluated this relationship using the GRN dynamical simulations described above. 

Cells without regulatory damage (p = 0) were used to establish the gene -specific baseline impact 

profile, against which cell impact variability was calculated at incr easing damage probabilities (p 

= 0.05, 0.10 and 0.15). Cell impact variability increased progressively with regulatory damage, 

both when calculated from ground -truth knockout impacts ( Fig. S1d ) and when estimated using 

DKO -predicted impacts ( Fig. S1e ). 

We next examined human pancreatic glucagon cells from five participants ( Fig. 3a ). 

Within each participant, cells were ranked according to the fraction of mutations attributed to the 

study -defined S1 mutational signature, which accumulates with  aging  in pancreatic cells^12. 

Because the contrast between mid - and high -mutation cells depends on the within -participant 

spread of S1 mutation burden, we expected participants with broader S1 distributions to show 

larger differences in cell impact variability.  The 50 cells with the lowest S1 mutation burden were 

used to establish the gene -speci fic reference impact profile, while the next 50 and the 50 cells with 8

the highest burden were defined as the mid - and high -mutation groups, respectively. Cell impact 

variability was then calculated for the mid - and high -mutation cells relative to the low -mutation 

reference. In four of the five participants (P1 -P3 and P5), high -mutation cells showed greater cell 

impact variability than mid -mutation cells. P4 showed the smallest difference between the two 

groups, consistent with the expected dependence of the effect on within -participant mutation -

burden spread. 

We further tested this association in cardiac fibroblasts from aged mice ( Fig. 3b, Fig. S6 ). 

Because mutation burden was not provided as a predefined signature score in this dataset, we 

calculated a per -cell mutation burden by integrating the single -cell mutation -count and gene -

expression matrices, using an expression -normalized mutation ratio  to reduce transcription -

dependent coverage bias (see  Methods ).  Cells were ranked independently within each donor 

according to this mutation -burden score, and the same  low -, mid -, and high -mutation grouping 

strategy was applied. The low -mutation cells were again used to establish the reference 

perturbation -response profile. Across donors, high -mutation cells generally showed greater cell 

impact variability than  mid -mutation cells, although the magnitude of the difference varied among 

animals. Together, these results associate the accumulation of somatic mutations with increasing 

divergence of single -cell perturbation -response profiles from relatively low -damage cell ular  states. 

These  result s suggest that the accumulation of regulatory alterations and somatic mutations 

is associated with increased cell impact variability, reflecting greater divergence of individual cells 

from the low -damage or low -mutation perturbation -response reference stat e. 

Cellular senescence is associated with increased gene and cell impact variability 

Finally, we asked whether increased perturbation -response variability was associated with cellular 

senescence, a well -established  aging -associated cellular state that accumulates with age and 

contributes to age -related tissue dysfunction 17,18 . We tested  this association in  two datasets:  human 

umbilical vein endothelial cells 19  (HUVECs) and WI -38  cells 20  (Fig. 4 ). In the HUVEC dataset, 

we retained the four clusters defined in the original study, which represent a progression from a 

proliferative state in  cluster 1, through intermediate states in  clusters 2 and 3, to a senescent state 

in  cluster 4 ( Fig. 4a ). We independently calculated senescence module scores for individual cells 

(see  Methods ) and observed significant differences among the four clusters, with  cluster 1 

showing the lowest senescence -associated transcriptional activity ( Fig. 4b ). Notably, the module 9

score did not increase monotonically across the four clusters, with  cluster 3 showing slightly higher 

values than  cluster 4. This pattern is consistent with the multi -step nature of replicative senescence 

described in the original study, in which different senescence -associated transcriptional programs 

can peak at intermediate rather than terminal states.  We  first  examined perturbation -response 

variability along this cluster progression. Gene impact variability increased  from  cluster  1 to 4 ,

indicating prog ressively greater cell -to -cell heterogeneity in the predicted effect of perturbing the 

same gene ( Fig. 4c ). At the single -cell level,  cluster 1 was used to establish the gene -specific 

reference impact profile. Cell impact variability subsequently increased from  clusters 2 to 4, with 

the largest deviation observed in  cluster 4 ( Fig. 4d ). Thus, although the senescence module score 

itself was not strictly monotonic, both gene - and cell -level perturbation -response variability 

increased along the proliferative -to-senescent progression defined in the original study. 

To test this association using a direct measure of cellular senescence, we focused on cells 

within  cluster 2 and grouped them by senescence module score. Cells were ranked by senescence 

module score, and the 50 cells with the lowest scores, cells ranked 51 -100, and the 50 cells with 

the highest scores were selected as low -, mid -, and high -senescence groups, respectively ( Fig. 4e ). 

Gene impact variability was significantly higher in high -senescence cells than in low -senescence 

cells ( Fig. 4f ). Using the low -senescence cells to establish the reference impact profile,  cell impact 

variability was also significantly higher in high -senescence cells than in mid -senescence cells ( Fig. 

4g ). These within -cluster results further support an association between cellular senescence and 

increased heterogeneity and divergence of perturbation responses, independent of the original 

cluster assignments. 

Then, w e applied the same analysis to an independent WI -38 replicative -senescence dataset. 

Cells were sampled across six population doubling levels (PDLs 25, 29, 33, 37, 46 and 50) defined 

in the original study ( Fig. 4h ), and senescence module scores increased along the replicative -aging 

trajectory ( Fig. 4i ). Gene impact variability increased with PDL ( Fig. 4j ). Similarly, when PDL 

25 cells were used to establish the reference impact profile,  cell impact variability increased at 

later PDLs ( Fig. 4k ). We further focused on PDL 50 and selected low -, mid -, and high -senescence 

cells according to the same senescence -score ranking strategy ( Fig. 4l ). High -senescence cells 

showed significantly greater  gene impact variability than low -senescence cells ( Fig. 4m ), while 

cell impact variability was significantly higher in high -senescence cells than in mid -senescence 

cells when the low -senescence group was used as the reference ( Fig. 4n ). 10 

These  findings indicate that cellular senescence is associated with increasing heterogeneity 

in gene -specific perturbation responses and greater divergence of single -cell perturbation -response 

profiles, consistent with a loss of regulatory robustness. 

# Discussion 

Transcriptional dysregulation has been proposed as a possible central mechanism contributing to 

functional decline during  aging 21,22 , but the evidence supporting its generality remains incomplete. 

Previous studies have provided evidence for this process through reduced gene co -expression in 

bulk transcriptomic data 23  and increased transcriptional variability at the single -cell level 11,24 .

However, bulk analyses do not capture stochastic differences among individual cells, whereas 

early single -cell studies were often limited to specific cell types or tissues. More recent work has 

shown reduced global gene -to -gene transcriptional coordinati on across diverse organisms and cell 

types 14 , providing broader evidence that transcriptional dysregulation is a general feature of  aging .

However, such global measures provide limited resolution regarding which genes contribute to 

this loss of coordination and how strongly individual cells deviate from a relatively stable 

regulatory state. Here, we extend this concept to a higher -resolutio n, perturbation -based 

perspective. Using DKO, we examined how predicted gene -perturbation effects vary across cells 

and how individual gene -impact profiles diverge  from low -aging , low -damage, or low -senescence 

reference states, allowing transcriptional dysregulation to be examined at both gene and single -

cell resolution. 

Through systematic analyses of  aging  datasets from multiple species, we first found that 

the predicted effect of perturbing the same gene became increasingly variable across cells with 

age, indicating a loss of consistency in gene -specific regulatory responses. We then asked whether 

this age -related increase in impact variability was associated with two major features of  aging : the 

accumulation of somatic mutations and cellular senescence. Cells with higher mutation burden 

showed greater cell impact variability, linking accumulated molecular  damage to increased 

divergence of cellular gene -impact profiles.  We next asked whether this regulatory instability was 

also related to cellular senescence, which may represent a downstream consequence of disrupted 

regulation rather than an upstream source of damage. Higher senescence levels were associated 

with increase d gene and cell impact variability, both across senescence -associated populations and 

within the same cellular population. Together, these findings support a model in which somatic 11 

mutational damage is linked to increasing regulatory divergence, while cellular senescence may 

emerge as one consequence of this loss of regulatory stability. Our results therefore provide further 

evidence that transcriptional dysregulation is a general fe ature of  aging .

An important implication of our findings is their potential to support the development of 

aging  interventions. The age -associated increase in impact variability suggests that transcriptional 

dysregulation may reflect a broad loss of regulatory consistency across genes and cells, rather than 

disruption of only a small number of specific  aging -related processes. Its association with somatic 

mutation burden further supports a link between accumulated molecular damage and this loss of 

regulatory stability. If s tochastic damage contributes to the progressive increase in impact 

variability, interventions aimed only at individual  aging  mechanisms may therefore be insufficient, 

and strategies that slow damage accumulation or preserve regulatory robustness may also be 

important. At the same time, gene impact variability could be used to identify sets of genes whose 

perturbation responses  change most strongly with age and determine which biological pathways 

and functions are most affected. These  aging -related pathways  could then provide candidates for 

functional testing and targeted intervention. 

Several limitations should be considered. First, although DKO has been validated for 

predicting gene perturbation impacts, the impacts analyzed in the  aging  datasets are model 

predictions rather than direct perturbation measurements from the same cells. Second, the observed 

relationships between impact variability,  aging , somatic mutation burden and cellular senescence 

are associative and do not establish causal direction. Mutation accumulation may contribute to 

regulatory divergence, whereas senescence may  represent a consequence or correlate of regulatory 

instability. Third, because DKO models were trained separately for different tissues and cell 

populations, absolute impact values should not be directly compared across independently trained 

datasets; our  conclusions therefore rely mainly on within -population comparisons. Finally, 

mutation burden estimated from scRNA -seq is incomplete because mutation detection depends on 

gene expression and transcript coverage. Although expression -aware normalization and  within -

donor comparisons reduce this bias, genomic measurements would provide a more complete 

assessment of somatic mutational damage. 

In summary, DKO provides a practical approach for studying  aging -related changes in gene 

regulatory responses at single -cell resolution without requiring a complete GRN or matched 

perturbation data. This makes it well suited to the large number of  aging  scRNA -seq datasets 12 

already available across species, tissues and cell types. Applying DKO to these datasets could 

reveal how gene perturbation effects change with age, identify genes showing the strongest age -

associated changes, and test whether these genes overlap with know n aging  biomarkers or 

represent previously unrecognized candidates. Comparisons across tissues and cell types could 

further distinguish  aging -related regulatory changes that are broadly shared from those that are 

cell -type specific. In this way, DKO may ex tend existing  aging  atlases from describing changes in 

gene expression to mapping how gene regulatory responses change during  aging .13

Cell quality control  procedure .

To ensure data quality,  cell quality control was performed jointly across all cells within each cell 

type before age -group comparisons.  (1)  First,  for each cell,  genes with log -transformed expression 

𝑥 𝑖 ,𝑗  > 0.2 were defined as expressed , and  the number of expressed genes was calculated for each 

cell.  Cells with expressed -gene counts outside two standard deviations from the mean of the entire 

cell -type population  (μ ± 2σ) were removed to exclude cells with unusually low or high gene 

detection.  (2)  Second,  we calculated the group average expression vector and measured the 

Spearman distance ( 𝑑  = 1 − ρ, where  ρ is the Spearman correlation coefficient) between each 

individual cell and this mean profile. We removed cells with a distance exceeding two standard 

deviations above the average distance ( 𝑑  > μ𝑑  + 2σ𝑑 , where  μ𝑑  and  σ𝑑  are the mean and standard 

deviation of distances to the mean profile). 

Senescence module scores  calculation .

Senescence module scores were calculated for individual cells using a predefined set of 

senescence -associated genes, including  CDKN1A , CDKN2A , IGFBP3, IGFBP4, IGFBP5, 

IGFBP7, TIMP1, TIMP2, MMP1, MMP2, CCL2, PAPPA , and  CTSB . Scores were calculated using 

the “ scanpy.tl.score_genes”  function in  python  Scanpy  package  (version 1.11.5 ). This approach 

quantifies the relative activity of the senescence -associated transcriptional program by comparing 

the average expression of the senescence gene set in each cell with  that of background genes with 

comparable overall expression levels. This expression -matched background correction reduces the 

influence of differences in baseline gene abundance and provides a cell -level measure of 

coordinated senescence -associated transc riptional activity. Higher scores therefore indicate a 

stronger senescence -associated transcriptional state.

Fig. 1. Conceptual and computational framework for linking aging -associated regulatory 

incoherence to heterogeneous perturbation responses.  (a) Aging is conceptualized as a loss 

of single -cell regulatory coherence.  Young cells are expected to maintain relatively coherent 

gene regulatory states, whereas aging is associated with increasingly dysregulated and 

heterogeneous regulatory states across individual cells.  (b) Proposed multi -level consequences 

of regulatory incoherence.  Increasing cellular aging burden and stochastic molecular damage are 

expected to increase cell -to -cell divergence in responses to the same gene perturbation. At the gene 

level, the response to perturbing a given gene is predicted to become more heterogene ous across 

aged cells. At the cell level, cells with a higher aging burden are predicted to exhibit greater 

variability in their responses to gene perturbations.  (c)  Deep -learning -based framew ork for 

testing these predictions across species.  Single -cell transcriptomic data from a defined cell type 

within each species are used to train a deep -learning DKO model that learns the mapping between 21 

gene assemblages and cellular expression profiles. The trained model is then used to perform 

single -cell virtual gene knockouts, in which individual expressed genes are computationally 

removed from each cell and the resulting perturbation responses are pre dicted. Differences 

between the original and virtual -knockout expression profiles are quantified as gene -level impacts, 

generating a cell -by -gene  perturbation impact matrix. This matrix enables quantification of 

perturbation -response heterogeneity at both  the gene and cell levels and provides a framework for 

testing the predicted consequences of aging -associated regulatory incoherence. 22

Fig. S1. Simulated g ene perturbation -response variability  increases with aging.  (a) Ground 

Truth.  In a dynamical simulation, with damage probability  p ∈ 0,0.05 ,0.1,0.5, each cell ’s gene -

regulatory weights are resampled at random ( p = 0 means all cells share the same network). For 

each gene in each cell we perform an  in silico  knockout and define the cell -level impact as the 

Bray –Curtis dissimilarity between expression profile before and after knockout. For a given gene 

across cells, impact variability is quantified by the median absolute deviation (MAD). As  p

increases, cellular heterogeneity rises and the true impact variability increases. (b)  Prediction.  On 

the same simulated data, our DKO model predicts the increasing trend in impact variability. (c) 

Real data.  Applying the DKO model to real data and comparing young vs old, we likewise observe 

higher gene mpact variability in the old group.

Fig. S5.  Robustness of the aging -associated increase in gene impact variability to training 

age.  To assess whether the aging -associated increase in gene impact variability was robust to the 

age group used for model training, DKO models were trained separately on young or old cells and 

then applied to both age groups without retraining. For each traini ng strategy, gene impact 

variability was compared between young and old cells using the same trained model. Naive B cells, 

granulocytes, and promonocytes were analyzed  as representative cell types. In the young -trained 

models, the model was trained exclusively on young cells and evaluated on both young and old 

cells. Conversely, in the old -trained models, the model was trained exclusively on old cells and 

evaluated on bo th young and old cells. In both training settings and across the examined cell types, 

old cells exhibited higher gene impact variability than young cells. These results indicate that the 

observed age -associated increase in perturbation -response heterogenei ty is robust to the age group 

used for model training and is not dependent on training separate models for young and old cells.
