# Predicting Unseen Scientific Interactions: Conditional Flow Models Hallucinate Non-Additive Effects 

Sam Gijsen ∗ Moritz Seiler Kerstin Ritter 

Hertie Institute for AI in Brain Health, University of Tübingen, Germany Tübingen AI Center, University of Tübingen, Germany Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin, Department of Psychiatry and Psychotherapy, Germany German Center for Mental Health (DZPG), partner site Tübingen, Germany 

Abstract 

Conditional generative models are increasingly used for scientific prediction be-yond the direct training support. Yet scientific questions often concern whether one intervention changes the effect of another. Such interactions can be of primary inter-est while explaining little of the total variation, allowing models to appear accurate overall without recovering the relevant scientific effect. It remains unclear how these models learn interactions and when these successfully extrapolate to unseen conditions. We therefore study interaction recovery in flow-matching models using a physiological time-series simulator, drug dose-response data, combinatorial cell perturbations, and molecular conformer generations, where we separate recovery from hallucinated interaction structure. In the controlled setting, individual effects are learned much earlier than interactions and models hallucinate interactions where none exist. Across settings, we find qualitatively different generalization problems, with successful prediction depending on whether condition structure continues from observed to held-out targets. When it does, interactions are recoverable. If not, models can generate plausible responses while missing the interaction. We furthermore find that hallucinations increase as targets become less identifiable, and that successful interaction recovery in output space can fail under nonlinear readouts. Finally, we show that aggregate fidelity metrics can conceal whether models learn and extrapolate interactions. Conditional generative models such as diffusion and flow matching models have gained significant attention for generating novel biological samples and for predicting biological responses under unseen experimental conditions. Prominent use cases include single-cell transcriptomics [He et al., 2026, Yu et al., 2026], neuroscience [Gijsen et al., 2026], and protein design [Watson et al., 2023, Ingraham et al., 2023]. To be scientifically valuable, models must be able to predict how experimental conditions interact, rather than only generating broadly plausible responses. For example, administering two drugs together may be antagonistic, independent, or synergistic, and redundant pairs require predicting that a drug has a clear effect on its own but not when combined with another. Importantly, however, the bulk of overall variability across samples is often dominated by effects of individual factors. Conversely, interactions often account only for a small fraction, despite regularly being the primary object of scientific interest. For instance, in single-cell perturbation experiments, the interaction between cell-type and perturbation determines less of the variance than either factor by itself, yet it is often the central question [Schäfer et al., 2026].

As aggregate fidelity metrics (such as MSE or FID) are heavily influenced by those stronger effects of individual factors, they may tell us relatively little about how interactions are learned during training or whether an interaction learned from observed combinations can be extrapolated to unseen ones. This is critical for scientific prediction and discovery, as this implies risks where models may appear converged while still learning the interaction, or appear reasonably accurate while predicting the effect that motivated an experiment incorrectly. Furthermore, interactions are not only a property of the system but also of the data representation. A nonlinear readout of a sample can imply a different interaction, and therefore interaction recovery in output space does not transfer to a quantity which often can be more scientifically relevant. The difficulty of this generalization problem directly depends on the available training data. A held-out target may be identifiable via smoothness of the condition space, via graph-relational knowledge, or underdetermined if only marginal support is provided. The latter problem, often called compositional generalisation [Wiedemer et al., 2023], is fundamentally different in the presence of interactions, as a training support covering each factor alone no longer determines the response in the held-out region of condition space [Brady et al., 2025]. Simply put, observing two factors separately does not determine how they interact [Lachapelle et al., 2023, Dong and Ma, 2022]. In this case, the missing relationship must come from observed joint conditions, inductive biases in the model, or meta-knowledge [Shojaee et al., 2025]. Here, we study how conditional flow models learn and extrapolate non-additive interactions in four systems that differ in the manner in which the training support constrains the held-out targets. Specifically, 1) a photoplethysmography (PPG) simulator with ground truth and tunable interaction strength, 2) drug dose-response screens where observed doses surround the held-out conditions, 3) combinatorial perturbations in single cells where components of a held-out pair are optionally excluded from observed pairs, and 4) radii of gyration over generated conformers given distinct molecular supports. 

We summarize our contributions as follows: 

• Diagnostics for interaction prediction. We decompose predicted interactions into interac-tion recovery and orthogonal hallucination, with an identity to interaction error. • Hallucination of non-additivity. In a simulator with a controlled interaction strength, we find flow models to generate interactions even in fully additive regimes. We additionally find stronger hallucinations for less constrained targets across settings. • Continuation rather than distance determines extrapolation. We show that held-out regions of a shared condition space at equal distance from the training support can be recovered almost completely or barely at all, depending on the continuation of the condition structure off the training support. • Aggregate metrics conceal failures to predict interactions. We observe distinct learning trajectories for interactions compared to main effects, that low endpoint errors can accom-pany failed interaction recovery, and that recovery in output space can fail after a scientific readout. 

1 Related work 

Predicting perturbation and combination responses. Deep models are now commonly used to predict responses to unseen chemical and genetic perturbations and their combinations [Roohani et al., 2024, Klein et al., 2025, Preuer et al., 2018]. However, recent work shows that such models can fail to beat additive or mean baselines for double perturbations [Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025], and that common metrics are inflated by systematic variation [Viñas Torné et al., 2025]. As additive baselines predict no interaction, we use these as a reference for scoring interaction errors. We complement these existing evaluations by decomposing interaction errors and relating them to the identifiability of held-out condition regions. 

Compositional generalization and inductive biases of generative models. Compositional general-ization has often been studied in settings where the joint response is determined by its components [Wiedemer et al., 2023, Okawa et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2022, Lippl and Stachenfeld, 2024], which is an assumption that the presence of interactions violate. Interactions depend on representations. Whether factors interact depends on the data representation in which we measure, which has been long established in epidemiology, where it is described as the interaction scale [Rothman et al., 1980, VanderWeele and Knol, 2014]. We extend this to the evaluation of generative models, showing that interaction recovery can differ between the output space and under a scientific readout. 

2 Methods 

2.1 Setup 

We start by providing a brief general framework. Given two factors A and B taking values a and 

b (categorical or continuous), flow matching models predict response distributions Pa,b over Rd

with conditional means μ(a, b ) = E[Y | a, b ]. The factors are additive if μ(a, b ) = g(a) + h(b)

for functions g and h and the interaction here is the part of the response not captured by such decomposition. For reference levels (a0, b 0) (Section 2.3), we define the additive interaction contrast as 

I(a, b ; a0, b 0) = μ(a, b ) − μ(a, b 0) − μ(a0, b ) + μ(a0, b 0) ∈ Rd,

which vanishes for all (a, b ) if and only if μ is additive. In the canonical 2 × 2 design with a 0

baseline, individual conditions A and B, and their joint condition AB , this reduces to 

IAB = μAB − μA − μB + μ0.

For continuous factors without a natural reference level, there is no scientifically meaningful choice for (a0, b 0). We therefore use functional ANOVA (Section 2.2), which avoids choosing a reference. Namely, its interaction at each condition is simply the average of the contrast across all possible reference points in the grid. The interaction is contrasted with the total effect μ(a, b ) − μ(a0, b 0)

which is what most aggregate evaluations are sensitive to. 

Interactions depend on the representation. Scientific disciplines are often concerned with representations in a semantic space (rather than the raw output space), which then form the basis for drawing conclusions. For a fixed, deterministic readout function g : Rd → Rk ,

IgAB = g(μAB ) − g(μA) − g(μB ) + g(μ0). If g is nonlinear, then interaction recovery in the output space does not imply recovering it after applying the readout. Therefore, ∥ ˆI − I∥ may be 3considerably smaller than ∥ ˆIg − Ig ∥, in which case evaluating model predictions only in the output space can be incomplete or misleading. 

Relating endpoint errors to interaction errors. Let ˆμc denote the predicted mean response under condition c and ec = ˆ μc − μc the endpoint error. By substituting into the definition of IAB we get: 

ˆI − I = eAB − eA − eB + e0,

where ˆI denotes the implied interaction based on the model predictions. These four errors have no guarantee of cancellation meaning that a small joint error does not bound the interaction error. Conversely, any errors shared across conditions may cancel out and thus small interaction errors do not indicate that individual conditional means are accurate. Due to this, we distinguish between ’anchored’ and fully generated estimates. The former relies on reference conditions while the latter relies fully on the model: 

ˆIanch = ˆ μAB − μA − μB + μ0, ˆIfull = ˆ μAB − ˆμA − ˆμB + ˆ μ0.

The anchored error corresponds to eAB and isolates model error for unseen conditions, which is analogous to experimental settings in which only single factors (but not their joint) have been measured. ˆIfull instead corresponds to using the model for the whole experiment.

Identification and generalization regimes. Many conditional-mean functions agree on the training support while yielding distinct interactions for held-out targets. Consequently, IAB is not identified by the training support alone, with inductive biases determining the prediction. Hence our experiments can be seen as evaluating this bias. Crucially, this distinguishes an interaction setting from the additive compositional generalization problem. Namely, with interactions, the widely studied L-shaped support for grid-based contexts [Wiedemer et al., 2023] therefore requires function extrapolation that the observed marginals do not determine. 2.2 Metrics 

We normalize errors such that a value of 1 corresponds to a trivial predictor. Specifically, we use the response scale RMS (Y − Y0) for response errors and the interaction scale RMS (I) for interaction errors (Table 1). Y0 is a domain-appropriate control or reference response. For a 2 × 2 design, IAB is the AB response minus the additive part μA + μB − μ0. Our simulator uses a large 2D grid of conditions and therefore has many condition levels per factor. We therefore obtain the same decomposition using functional ANOVA over this grid, with Y = ˜Y + I, where ˜Y is the grand mean plus the two main-effect surfaces and I the residual non-additive component. We also apply this to the model’s generated grid ˆY , giving ˆY = ˆ˜Y + ˆI, where ˆ˜Y is the additive part of the model’s prediction and ˆI its interaction. All grid cells are used to compute the grand and main-effect means. EI , α, and β are computed afterwards on the held-out region of condition space. 

2.3 Domain-specific interaction targets 

The additive contrast is realized differently across our four studied systems (described in Section 2.5): • Physiological time series: given continuous factors without a natural reference level, we use the fANOVA decomposition directly in the waveform representation, such that IAB is itself a time series. • Physiological readout: a nonlinear physiological readout g is applied to the generated waveforms before the fANOVA decomposition, yielding IgAB .• Single-cell perturbation: we use unperturbed controls as the reference, with IAB computed from condition-level mean expression vectors, thus yielding a vector over genes. • Molecular conformers: as graphs with different atom counts cannot be averaged in coordi-nate space, we define the interaction on a readout: the mean heavy-atom radius of gyration over conformers; as reference, we use the unedited parent molecule • Drug dose–response: the zero dose of the combined drug serves as the reference, and we define interaction relative to Bliss independence rather than additively (see below). Additivity is not a sensible null for drug combinations as fractional effects are bounded to [0 , 1] ,meaning simple addition of two drug effects can violate these bounds. Consequently, Bliss indepen-dence is commonly used in pharmacology, which assumes that two drugs act independently [Bliss, 1939]. Concretely, we define EA and EB as normalized (to [0 , 1] ) fractional drug effects such as inhibition, where 0 indicates no effect and 1 fully inhibiting. 2.4 Models 

We train conditional flow matching models following Lipman et al. [2023] with minor variations across domains which we summarize in Appendix Table 5. In the standard setting, we learn a velocity field vθ (xτ , τ, c ) that transports p0 = N (0 , 1) to the conditional data distribution p1(·| c) along the probability path xτ = (1 − τ )z0 + τ z 1 where τ ∈ [0 , 1] indicates the flow matching time. Models are trained using an MSE loss and, unless stated otherwise, sampled by numerical integration of the learned flow. Table 5 also includes conditioning, optimization, model architectures, and prediction targets. For molecular conformers, we use a published equivariant flow-matching model conditioned on molecular graphs [Hassan et al., 2024]. We compare three ways of obtaining a prediction for the joint condition AB :• Joint model is trained and evaluated with conditioning directly on AB and explicitly represents interaction. • Composed composes the learned conditional velocity fields

(x, t ) = vA(x, t ) + vB (x, t ) − v0(x, t )

2.5 Datasets 

We study four systems, which cover different aforementioned identification regimes while having well defined interaction targets (Figure 2). The physiological simulator of photoplethysmogram (PPG; widely used in wearables) maps melanin and blood-volume fractions to 16-channel PPG waveforms with 64 timesteps and provides exact simulator ground truths and a tunable interaction strength. Consequently, γ modulates the interaction while leaving both main effects and the grand mean unchanged. We include three further datasets enabling investigations in measured data under smoothness-identified and relationally-identified holdouts. Specifically, the dose-response dataset consists of longitudinal yeast growth responses (96-dimensional) to pairs of drugs administered on 8 × 8 dose grids [Cokol et al., 2011]. We hold-out and predict 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 blocks for seen drug pairs. The Norman CRISPRa Perturb-seq dataset contains single-cell responses to single- and paired gene perturbations [Norman et al., 2019]. As edits are commonly observed on hundreds of other molecular cores, relational support is constant while local smoothness support varies. We provide more details on these datasets in Appendix C. For the empirical data, we report the anchored estimate to approximate more realistic scientific workflows. For PPG, only generated estimates are applicable. Interaction magnitudes vary across the systems, with interaction-to-total norm ratios of 5.6% (PPG), 23.2% (dose-response; median assay), 46.1% (perturbation; median pair), and 47.4% (conformer; median family). This corresponds to 0.3%, 5%, 21%, and 22% of response energy respectively. For Y0, we use the lowest melanin/blood volume value, untreated responses, unperturbed conditions, and unedited parent molecule. 

3 Results 

3.1 Aggregate endpoint error conceals interaction learning dynamics 

For the synthetic PPG dataset, we hold-out two corners of the condition space ( low-low (LL) and 

high-high (HH)), referring to the melanin and blood volume values) and investigate errors across training (Figure 3). Whereas additive and endpoint errors appear to have largely converged by 10K optimizer steps, the interaction error has not. Specifically, for seen targets, the model needs at least an order of magnitude longer training. For unseen targets, we observe two distinct dynamics: when LL is held-out, interaction error stays high and plateau’s by 50K steps. In contrast, for unseen HH targets, interaction error is approximately 7 times smaller by 20K steps, We also find such decoupling between trajectories of endpoint and interaction errors in measured data, albeit in the opposite direction. For held-out Norman pairs, EI begins to increase after ∼30K steps while Eend continues to decrease until ∼50K steps (Appendix Figure 7). Consequently, checkpoint selection using endpoint error selects models whose interaction predictions are already degrading. 3.2 Distinguishing interaction-prediction failures Distinct recoverability of a shared condition space. We further investigate these bifurcated results by inspecting model interaction recovery and hallucination (Figure 4). We observe stark differences, with joint models largely recovering the interaction for HH but not LL targets ( α ≈ 1.00 and α ≈ 0.31 

at γ = 1 , respectively). Hallucinated structure is also higher for LL ( β ≈ 0.08 vs 0.43 ). For this LL corner, we provide the produced maps of interaction in Appendix Figure 6, showing that composed and shared-model AB baseline significantly underestimate interaction amplitude. These results showcase that the extend to which interactions can be extrapolated based on a joint training support can strongly depend on the modeled domain. The two corners are geometrically matched but not physically equivalent. We find that a simple log-additive continuation rule selected based on the training support transfers to the held out HH corner. LL, in contrast, approaches a physical boundary where it is not (Appendix D). This maps to the aforementioned identifiability problem.

However, at γ = 0 , we have I = 0 and thus EI and α are not applicable. In most systems, as identification gets harder, we observe reduced interaction recovery ( α), with distinct delta magnitudes across systems. For PPG ( γ = 1 ) LL this nearly entirely removes weak recovery ( 0.30 → 0.05 ), indicating the importance of locality for extrapolation. Meanwhile, HH is recoverable even far from the training support (1.03). For dose-response, we observe only a very weak drop ( 0.98 → 0.97 ), with sufficient smoothness constraint persisting to the larger held-out block. We confirm this by fitting a standard (deterministic) regression baseline and find nearly identical recovery ( 0.98 → 0.97 ). For perturbations, recovery is again reduced ( 0.66 → 0.41 ), highlighting the importance of other observed combinations. We observe an exception for molecular conformers, with less local support leading to increases in α (0.69 → 0.70 → 0.76 ). We find this results from exaggerated differences between scaffold families, while within-family recovery remains attenuated (further details in Appendix E). Interestingly, we observe increased hallucination across systems: PPG HH β 0.12 → 0.25 , dose-response 0.18 → 0.24 , perturbations 0.80 → 0.86 , and conformers 0.63 → 0.69 → 0.79 . This suggests hallucinated structure not to be a fixed property of the model and that they do not merely add a baseline level of hallucinated structure, but that this scales as targets get less identifiable. Comparing endpoint and interaction errors instead, reveals an important further insight (Table 2). Namely, for PPG LL, enlarging the held-out region of condition space maintains non-trivial endpoint prediction ( Eend ≪ 1), while making the interaction prediction no better than predicting no interaction at all ( EI ≈ 1). 

4 Discussion 

We have investigated how flow models learn interactions and generate them for unseen targets using error decompositions. We have found multiple interesting dynamics: aggregate endpoint errors can conceal interaction errors, scientifically-relevant readouts can materially change interaction prediction scores, and found that both recovery and hallucination depend on identifiability regimes. Our findings suggest that scientific evaluation should test interaction recovery directly in the repre-sentation space which is used to draw conclusions. With respect to data, individual factors appear insufficient; either collecting observations or appropriate structural assumptions are necessary to constrain unseen interactions. Computing interaction recovery and hallucination in addition to aggre-gate errors can help distinguish succesful extrapolation from plausible predictions which miss the scientific effects of interest. Limitations of the presented work include our restriction to two-factor interactions and a reliance on a mean-based (rather than distributional) contrast. 9Acknowledgments and Disclosure of Funding

C Dataset details 

C.1 PPG simulator. 

The PPG data are generated using deterministic replay of a fixed Monte Carlo photon-path bank using a three-layer skin model (simplified from Behrmann et al. [2026]). A path bank contains 10 8

photon histories for 35 scattering settings with linear interpolation between adjacent settings, and Beer-Lambert reweighting applying absorption. This makes the simulator deterministic conditional on the path bank. We generate 64 time points for four source-detector distances (3-6 mm) across four wavelengths (525, 660, 850, 940 nm). The condition space is made up of epidermal melanin ( 0.25 –14% ) and dermal blood volume (BV2; 

0.1–3.9% ). Meanwhile, arterial oxygen saturation is fixed at 80% and subcutaneous blood volume at 

4.05% . The temporal blood-volume modulation follows a normalized arterial luminal-area waveform (from the PWDB database; age-45). Dermal and subcutaneous blood volumes are multiplied by 

1 + 0 .02 w(t) with w(t) ∈ [0 , 1] .First, we simulate a uniform 101 × 101 Mel-BV2 grid. We apply balanced functional ANOVA to the clean γ = 1 reference grid, writing Y1 = ˜Y + I, where ˜Y contains the grand mean and the Mel 13 and BV2 main-effect functions, and I is the residual interaction. The controlled interaction-strength grids are then defined as Yγ = ˜Y + γI . As such, γ = 1 is the original grid, with γ < 1 reducing its interaction while preserving the mean and main effects. 10 6 Mel-BV2 pairs are drawn independently and uniformly across the condition space. C.2 Drug-response data 

The Cokol et al. [2011] yeast screenings consist of 200 drug-pair assays across 33 drugs, with 175 different-drug assays and 25 same-drug controls. Each assay is an 8 × 8 dose grid, with every cell consisting of a 96D growth curve. This results from 15 minute measurements across 24 hours, yielding 12800 total trajectories. 

Splitting. For each assay we hold out a fixed set of blocks (rather than cross-validation). The 2 × 2

hold-out yields [11577, 759, 464] train/validation/test trajectories, with 3 × 3 sample sizes being [11077, 769, 954]. The same 106 assays are used to compare across the two block sizes. Untreated, single-drug, and same-drug conditions are included in both training sets. 

C.3 Single-cell perturbation data 

The Norman et al. [2019] K562 CRISPRa Perturb-seq dataset consists of 91205 cells and 5045 genes, including controls, single perturbations, and 131 double perturbations which involves 73 genes. The double-perturbation conditions contain 49-1030 cells (median=257). Models use a 500D PCA representation fitted to 20000 control and single-perturbation cells. For evaluation, we decode predictions to the 5045-gene log-expression space. 

Splitting. We use cross-validation with four outer folds holding out each double perturbation once, while all controls and single perturbations are always included in the training set. Train/validation/test sample sizes range between [78539-80391, 2628-3770, 7348-9992] cells. Of the 131 test pairs, 113 are ’graph-supported’ meaning that both genes are included in other training pairs. For the remaining 18 ’cold-start’ pairs, at least one of the two genes has no observed training pairs. The four folds are repeated across three training seeds. 

C.4 Molecular conformer data 

GEOM contains conformer ensembles of drug-like molecules [Axelrod and Gomez-Bombarelli, 2022]. We use a pool of 26830 molecules with at least one retained conformer. The target distribution for a molecule is uniform over its 30 conformers which have the highest CREST weights (following Hassan et al. [2024]), sampled randomly during training. As a readout, we use the heavy-atom radius 14 of gyration ( Rg , Å) over conformers. For generated estimates, we average Rg across 128 draws per molecule. 

Chemical squares. Molecules are decomposes into a core with two attachment sites, each carrying one substituent. A ’square’ is made up of a parent molecule (00), two single edits each swapping the substituent at one site (10, 01), and the double edit (11). From a set of 3319 squares, we retain those for which all corners share a scaffold and have ≥ 10 conformers. We select 30 scaffold families with five squares per family, which yields 150 test queries with 384 unique parent molecules. 

Splits. The ’neighborhood’ of the test squares is specified as the union of the 30 scaffold families and all pool molecules with Morgan fingerprint (radius 2, 2048 bits, chirality-aware) Tanimoto similarity 

≥ 0.6 to a square corner (4504 molecules). Each of the three arms contain 22076 training molecules of which 17572 are shared. The Neighborhood arm adds all 4504 neighborhood molecules (including the 384 parents), while Parents only adds the 384 parents and 4120 additional distant controls, and finally Distant adds only 4504 distant controls such that no parent or neighborhood molecule is seen. Controls are matched to the removed molecules across multiple graph properties (heavy atoms, rings, bonds, rotatable bonds, heteroatoms, formal charge, aromatic atoms; all absolute mean differences 

≤ 0.11 ). The same validation and target sets were used across the three arms. 

C.5 Evaluation 

Prior to computing endpoint and interaction metrics, we average across sampling seeds within each trained model. For PPG, we average across four sampling seeds, for drug-response data we average eight predictions, and for single-cell perturbation data we average eight batches of 24 generated cells. We report variation across training seeds, with PPG using three seeds, drug-response data uses four seeds, single-cell perturbation data using three, and conformers using three. 

D Why do the PPG hold-out corners differ? 

To understand why flow models trained on PPG data extrapolated well into the held-out high-high (HH) corner, but not into the geometrically matched low-low (LL) corner, we performed a small check on spatial continuation. Specifically, we use the training support to select from a small set of simple continuation rules. This procedure selected a raw-log-additive rule which constructed HH highly accurately (RMSE = 0 .007 , interaction cosine= 1.00 ). While the same rule was selected when LL was held out, it failed to extrapolate (RMSE= 0.771 ). This suggests that the response dynamics in the held-out HH corner continue smoothly from observed support, while the LL region has interaction structure which cannot be accurately inferred from the L-shaped training support.
